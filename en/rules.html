How Mahjong Works: Wild Tiles and Winning Hands in Legend of the Mahjong King

This page helps you follow every mahjong hand in Legend of the Mahjong King, from recognising tiles to understanding the choices that decide a match.

The tiles: a world in a set of 136

A set contains 136 tiles, with four copies of each tile. The three suits, Characters, Bamboo and Dots, each run from one to nine. That makes thirty-six tiles per suit and 108 altogether. The four Winds, East, South, West and North, add sixteen tiles. The three Dragons, Red Dragon, Green Dragon and White Dragon, add twelve. The blank face of the White Dragon still represents a distinct tile.

The novel gives these tiles a symbolic place in the cosmos. Characters, Bamboo and Dots stand for wealth, wood and metal; the Winds represent the four directions. The Dragons represent the heart of heaven, vitality and clarity, respectively. These are the story's meanings. To follow a hand, begin with suits and numbers.

Shuffled tiles are stacked into the wall, from which players draw. Each player's own tiles form their hand; discarded tiles enter the discard pool. Watching those discards helps reveal what has appeared and what others might need. In Chapter One, the ninth turn follows Qinglan's progress through his draws.

A winning hand: four groups and a pair

The usual structure is four groups plus a pair of eyes. A sequence contains three consecutive numbers in one suit, such as One, Two and Three of Characters. A triplet contains three identical tiles, such as three copies of the Three of Dots. A kong contains four identical tiles but still counts as one group. Two identical tiles form the pair of eyes.

Without a kong, four groups of three plus a pair total fourteen tiles. A kong adds a physical tile to its group, so fourteen is not a fixed physical count for every winning hand. Winds and Dragons cannot form numbered sequences.

To call pung, take another player's discard to join two matching tiles in your hand, then expose the triplet. A kong groups four matching tiles under the applicable rules. To call chow, take the previous player's discard to complete and expose a sequence with two tiles from your hand. The Tuidao School allows pung and kong but forbids chow. You can still form sequences from tiles you draw yourself.

If one suitable tile would complete your hand, you are ready to win; the tiles that complete it are your winning tiles. A concealed hand has no groups exposed through chow, pung or an exposed kong. Qinglan builds his hand through his own draws in Chapter One.

Four groups and a pair are not the only winning pattern in the story. The story also allows Seven Pairs, with a wild tile able to fill a missing partner.

Wild tiles: the revealed type becomes wild

At the start of each Tuidao School hand, the scorekeeper will reveal a tile to determine the wild tile, turning over the third tile from the end of the wall. Tiles of that same type can act as a wild tile, replacing any needed tile when forming a hand. In the last hand of Chapter One, the revealed tile is a Red Dragon. Red Dragons are therefore wild for that hand; they are not always the wild type. When you set up a room in the game Queba Mahjong, you can choose “Revealed Is Wild”, which matches the novel. The more common rule makes the tile after the revealed one wild: reveal a Five of Characters, and the Six of Characters is wild.

The revealed tile stays at the end of the wall and cannot be drawn in the chapter's example. Qinglan holds a different copy. A wild tile fills a gap without adding another tile to his hand. His final arrangement shows exactly how:

One, Two and Three of Characters form the first sequence.

Five, Six and Seven of Bamboo form the second sequence.

Three copies of the Three of Dots form a triplet.

Six of Dots, Seven of Dots and a Red Dragon form the third sequence, with the Red Dragon standing in for the Eight of Dots.

Two North Winds form the pair of eyes.

Three sequences, one triplet and one pair complete the structure. Qinglan's final draw, the Three of Dots, completes the triplet. His Red Dragon fills the gap in the Dots sequence. The wild tile's power is a precise substitution within the hand.

Self-draw and dealing in: from minus 28 to plus 4

Complete your hand with a tile you draw from the wall, and you win by self-draw. You can also win using another player's discard; the player who discards that tile is said to deal in. Qinglan needs the first kind of win.

For the scoring used here in the novel, a small discard win earns four points; a self-draw requires each of the other three players to pay sixteen points. Transfers within the same side cancel in that side's combined score. These are the story's scores, not a universal scoring table for mahjong.

Chapter One has two players on the Tao family's side and two representing Hengchang Trading House. Hu Shan deals in during the first hand, giving the Tao side four points. The second is a drawn hand, leaving that total unchanged. Hu Shan wins the third by self-draw: both Tao players pay sixteen, losing thirty-two between them. Transfers within Hengchang cancel for its side.

Four minus thirty-two equals minus twenty-eight.

Qinglan takes the final hand for the Tao family. Winning from Tao Lingtao's discard would only move points within their own side. A small discard win from Hengchang would earn just four, too little to recover the deficit. That is why he passes on Tao Lingtao's Eight of Dots on his sixth turn.

On his ninth turn, Qinglan draws the Three of Dots and wins. Individually, he receives sixteen points from each of three players. But the payment from his Tao teammate cancels within their combined account. Only Hengchang's two payments increase the Tao side's total:

Sixteen plus sixteen equals thirty-two. Minus twenty-eight plus thirty-two equals a net win of four points.

The final hand adds thirty-two to the Tao side's score. Counting all forty-eight points Qinglan receives as the side's net gain would count an internal transfer as a gain.

A sworn match of honour: who establishes the truth?

A sworn match of honour settles a dispute under agreed terms: what is contested, how the match is played and what the result commits each side to do. Chapter One concerns access to a dye works' waterway. At the table of justice, a result carries authority because people pledge to honour it and the play must withstand scrutiny.

The scorekeeper records points, checks tiles and adjudicates. Yuening's work includes finding an extra tile as well as calculating scores. The chronicler records play, making it possible to examine old hands through Zhumo's game records. By the customs of the mahjong world, it takes joint attestation by three parties—scorekeeper, witness and chronicler—before a person suspected of cheating can be detained for questioning.

Open game records make play available for examination: discards are recorded in public, and the record can be checked. Proven cheating can invalidate a verdict. Winning does not make someone's claim forever beyond challenge.

Three schools, three game modes

The Tuidao School values steady play and a concealed hand. It allows pung and kong, forbids chow and determines a new wild type each hand. Its corresponding game mode is Tuidao Hu.

The Red Dragon Society uses the four Red Dragons as wild tiles and favours flexible play. A bird procedure follows a win. Its corresponding mode is Red Dragon Mahjong.

Pinghe Hall allows chow and pung, emphasising speed and skill in ordinary winning hands. A horse reveal follows a win. Its corresponding mode is Guangdong Mahjong. That is a game mode name; the fictional schools are not assigned real geographical origins. The names of the bird and horse procedures alone do not specify their scoring formulas.

To try a hand yourself, visit the game Queba Mahjong and explore the mode corresponding to the school that interests you.
