Chapter 13The Great Tide

As the sun sank into the sea, the far eastern end of the docks was deserted.

A row of abandoned timber piles stretched from shore into the water, crusted with oyster shells. As the tide rose and fell, the piles seemed to shorten and lengthen. On the third sat a patch of red.

Feiyan had taken off her shoes. Her two white feet dangled above the water, kicking at the waves. The sea wind lifted her skirt, and this time Qinglan saw clearly: beneath it trailed eight crimson tails with snow-white tips, soft and furry, swaying gently in the dusk.

Where the ninth should have been, nothing.

“Scholar.” She did not turn. “You really came.”

“I promised.”

“And brought a little scorekeeper.” Feiyan glanced back at Yuening and smiled. “Afraid I’ll eat you?”

Yuening hugged her ledger, chin high. “Afraid you won’t honour your debts.”

Feiyan laughed until her tails swayed in disarray.

◆

Qinglan stepped up to the pile, reached inside his robe and took out the red cloth parcel.

He unfolded it, layer by layer. A clean, bone-white tile face.

He held it on his open palm for a while. On the night his father pressed it into his hand, he had held it just like this, his palm damp with cold sweat.

He did not bargain, and he did not look again. He held out his hand, loosened his fingers one by one, and set it in Feiyan’s palm.

The moment the tile left his hand, his chest went suddenly hollow. The sound of the waves swelled, beat after beat, pouring straight into his ears.

She went still.

She looked down at the White Dragon in her hand and stayed there, not moving. The sea wind blew past. One by one, her tails drooped.

“Just like that?” she asked softly. “No conditions? No safeguard? Aren’t you afraid I’ll take it and leave?”

“I am afraid,” Qinglan said. “But this one is mine to give, and I want to give it.”

“Three hundred years.” Her voice was a little hoarse. “For three hundred years, everyone who bargained with me made me hand over what they wanted first. You’re the first to give me something before I gave you anything.”

She closed her hand tightly round the White Dragon.

“Do you know what it is?” she asked.

“My father left it,” Qinglan said. “It’s a Heavenly Tile. It has shown something from the past. The rest, I haven’t found out yet.”

“You haven’t found out, and you’re giving it to me anyway?”

“The part I’ve seen, it hasn’t forgotten.” Qinglan watched her. “But if the old man sinks into the sea tonight, Changfeng will never have him back.”

Feiyan lowered her eyes. In the dusk, the White Dragon gave no light at all. Just an ordinary old bone tile.

Her finger brushed the little Ghost Hand mark on its back, as though touching something hot. She looked up and glanced at Qinglan’s hand.

Qinglan thought she would press it back into his hand. So did Yuening. She had already taken half a step forward.

But Feiyan simply rose and tucked the White Dragon inside her dress, close against her body.

“A tile kept long enough will guard its keeper.” She pressed a hand to the front of her dress and did not look at him. “It has been with you for ten years. From now on, take care of yourself.”

“At daybreak,” she said, “the old man will be on the quay.”

She turned. Eight tails swept through the dusk, and she was already in a small boat. Silently, it moved towards the unlit ship offshore.

“Feiyan!” Yuening called after her.

The patch of red did not look back.

◆

Qinglan stood beside the pile for a long time without moving.

His robe was empty inside. For ten years something had rested there against his chest, cool and hard. Now the sea wind entered that place and passed straight through to his back.

Instinctively he reached out to press it. When he closed his fingers, he gripped only cloth chilled by the sea wind. He opened his hand and glanced behind him.

Yuening came to his side without speaking, only hugging her ledger a little tighter. The ink rubbing was still in its inner pocket.

“Do you regret it?” she asked at last.

“I don’t know.” Qinglan watched the little boat recede. “Father said the tiles were clean. It was people who were dirty. I’ve put it in someone’s hands. Whether those hands are clean is up to her.”

◆

Around midnight, a string of lights appeared in the harbour.

Three fast boats from Pinghe Hall. Two lion boats from the Huo family. Changfeng’s little sampan. Li Boyuan’s diving boat. One by one they slipped quietly out from the quay and spread into a semicircle roughly three hundred yards from Sea Whale Trading House’s ship.

Jinshi and Huo Xiao stood in the bow of a lion boat. Their two lions rested side by side before them, one gold, one blue-green.

“Brother Jin,” Huo Xiao said quietly. “No contest this time.”

“No contest.” Jinshi grinned. “This time we go together.”

Li Boyuan had stripped down to nothing but his glossy brown fur and wound a coil of rope round his waist. He was rubbing fish oil into his fur. Liuxia and Meng Chuan stood ready on Pinghe Hall’s fast boats, gripping their oars. Changfeng crouched in the sampan, staring fixedly at the ship.

Lu Zhaoye perched on top of a mast, tail hanging, his golden eyes bright as two lamps in the dark.

“Yuening,” Qinglan asked softly. “How many lights aboard?”

She narrowed her eyes and counted. “Two at the bow. Three at the stern. On deck… seven figures. And one more lamp, in a stern cabin. It hasn’t gone out.”

“Start with that cabin,” Lu Zhaoye said. “A light doesn’t mean he’s still inside.”

Zhumo crouched on Li Boyuan’s diving boat, basket in his arms, one hand holding his brush and the other pressing the paper. By the bow’s little lamp he recorded line after line: “Midnight: three Pinghe Hall boats, two Huo boats, one diving boat, one sampan…”

“Master,” Changfeng whispered. “Even now, you’re writing it down?”

“Especially now.” The panda’s ears drooped. His brush did not tremble. “Especially now.”

◆

In the hours after midnight, the tide began to ebb.

Fast. As though someone had pulled a plug from the seabed. Water that had covered half the piles fell inch by inch. Rocks emerged one by one, wet and shining.

Suddenly, Sea Whale’s ship stirred into activity. Figures hurried back and forth. Cables were lowered into the sea. Splash after splash as men jumped in.

“They’re going down.” Li Boyuan rubbed the last of the fish oil onto his face. “So am I.”

“Brother Li—”

“Don’t worry.” The diver grinned, showing white teeth. “I know that water better than they do.”

He rolled over the side without raising a splash.

Beneath the surface was another world.

Li Boyuan sank and sank. The firelight from the ships grew fainter overhead, until only ink-black darkness remained. The cold bit to the bone. His ears roared. He could hear nothing else. Feeling the rope at his waist, he eased down inch by inch until his hand touched bottom.

Mud. So soft it seemed bottomless. He reached into it and his whole arm sank.

He felt his way east through the dark. A hundred feet. Ninety. Nearby, someone else’s hands and feet thrashed through the water. Sea Whale’s men. Searching too.

◆

At that moment, the lamp that had burned so steadily in the ship’s stern went out.

A little boat emerged from the ship’s shadow and headed straight for Changfeng’s sampan. Two figures sat inside: a huddled old man, and a patch of red.

“Grandpa!” Changfeng’s voice cracked.

The boat came alongside. Supporting Old Hong, Feiyan pushed him into Changfeng’s arms. The old man shook all over, his face black and blue. But he was alive. He could breathe. He could clutch his grandson’s arm and cry.

“I keep my word.” Feiyan looked towards Qinglan. Her quiet voice drifted over the water. “It isn’t dawn yet. You can have him early.”

“What about the White Dragon?” Yuening shouted.

Feiyan did not answer. She pressed a hand once to the front of her dress, then rowed back into the ship’s shadow.

◆

An hour and three quarters after midnight, the tide reached its lowest point.

The clouds suddenly parted.

The Big Dipper hung slantwise in the northern sky, its handle pointing straight at the water.

Then the seabed lit up.

Not lamplight. A deep jade-green glow rose through the black water, like a pool of spring water, like an opening eye. A hundred feet east of the third pile, it brightened and dimmed. Brightened and dimmed. Like breathing.

Every boat fell silent.

Old Hong leaned over the sampan’s side, staring at the glow, lips trembling, tears streaming. “Dingchao… My father’s tile… It still remembers…”

Qinglan watched the light. Suddenly, a heat seemed to burn against his chest.

Not his chest. Something aboard the great ship had begun to glow too.

At the bow, a man stood beneath a lamp, holding something aloft. It shone with the same jade-green light, brightening and dimming in exact rhythm with the glow beneath the sea. One breath in. One breath out.

His father’s White Dragon.

The man holding it wore a woven hat.

◆

“Two Heavenly Tiles.” The man in the woven hat’s voice travelled low and steady across the sea. “Once the Dipper showed, they recognized one another across the water.”

The two lights brightened and dimmed of themselves. Qinglan stared at the raised hand. Across half a li of sea he could not see whether the man had touched the back of the tile.

The man in the woven hat slipped the White Dragon inside his robe. The light on the seabed grew clearer, as though someone were pointing it out.

“Those below,” the man in the woven hat said. “Dig beneath the light.”

A patch of red stood beside the cabin door.

Feiyan’s hand rested against her dress. It was empty now. The White Dragon was in the man’s keeping.

“You promised,” she said softly. “My tail.”

He did not turn.

“When the one below comes up,” he said. “Two tiles for one tail.”

Her hand slowly fell. She said nothing more, only stepped back into the cabin’s shadow. Then another step. Until darkness swallowed all but the smallest patch of red.

Three hundred years. They had never kept their word once.

This time was no different.

One after another, the divers plunged towards the glow.

“We can’t let them have it!” Jinshi roared. “Brother Huo!”

“Raise the lions!”

Gongs and drums burst from both lion boats. Oars flashed as they charged straight at the ship.

A tremendous crash. The lion boat’s bow struck the ship’s flank. Jinshi stood forward, one hand on the rail, the other raising the lion head. “Yunchuan’s lion is here!” Beside him stood Huo Xiao, his blue-green lion’s jaws wide. The brass-bell eyes of both lions flashed in the firelight. For a moment, the men aboard recoiled in fright.

Pinghe Hall’s fast boats swept in from either side. Liuxia stood in a bow with a bamboo pole. Whenever a Sea Whale diver surfaced, she jabbed him back under.

In the confusion, a yellow-and-black shape launched itself from a masthead onto the ship’s bow.

◆

Lu Zhaoye landed on deck just as the man in the woven hat turned.

They stood facing each other, three paces apart.

Three years. Lu Zhaoye had pursued him for three years. Through seven cities. Through countless moonless nights. For the first time, only three paces separated him from that woven hat.

“The Lu family’s little cat,” the man said. “You’ve come too close.”

Lu Zhaoye said nothing. His short blade was already drawn.

The man’s wrist turned. Three bone tiles flew from between his fingers: one at the throat, one at the heart, one at the knee. Lu Zhaoye dropped low and twisted aside, avoiding the first two. The third grazed his thigh, drawing blood.

He landed and lunged again, blade aimed straight at the man’s wrist.

The man turned sideways and raised a hand to block. The blade skimmed its back. A hiss as it slit the black leather glove.

Lu Zhaoye reached and seized it.

He tore the glove off.

◆

For the first time, the hand was bare in the torchlight.

Long, pale, the joints sharply defined. But old scars twisted from its back to the wrist. The skin was shrivelled, like paper licked by fire.

Lu Zhaoye froze.

Seven years ago. The night before we were to bring our complaint. A fire. Eleven in the Lu household. Only I survived.

“You.” His voice shook uncontrollably. “That fire—was it you who set it?”

The man studied his hand in the starlight for a while, as though it belonged to someone else.

“Those who set fires,” he said indifferently, “are bound to feel the flames.”

Red-eyed, Lu Zhaoye hurled himself forward.

From the lion boat, Qinglan watched the two figures. His heart leapt into his throat.

Gongs and drums, the waves, the battle cries, all poured into his ears at once. At moments like this before, the tile had lain cool against his chest; his heart would quiet first, and his eyes would follow. Tonight that place was empty. He clenched his teeth and drew his breath down, one breath after another. Only after several did the two figures at the bow come clear.

He could read it. Lu Zhaoye was lunging too hastily. A man in haste saw only his enemy, not his enemy’s hands. The man’s left shoulder had not dipped. His right foot had not turned. He was not going to dodge. He was waiting.

“Lu Zhaoye! Don’t—”

The cry vanished beneath drums and waves.

This time, the man did not dodge. He raised the scarred hand and struck Lu Zhaoye in the chest with his palm.

Lu Zhaoye flew backwards, broke through the rail and fell into the sea.

◆

“Lu Zhaoye!”

Jinshi leapt from the lion boat into the water and caught Lu Zhaoye as he sank. Huo Xiao’s blue-green lion boat pulled alongside. Hands reached down and hauled them both aboard.

Lu Zhaoye lay on the deck, blood at his mouth, chest heaving. But his right hand was clenched tight.

Qinglan prised it open.

Inside was the torn-off black leather glove. A single word had been embroidered into its lining in tiny, meticulous silver stitches:

Star.

◆

Below, the glow was so bright that Li Boyuan could barely keep his eyes open.

It lay less than an arm’s length away, half buried in the mud, like a piece of jade that breathed. He had never seen such a colour. Green, clear and burning, as though all the light in the sea had gathered in this one small tile.

He reached for it.

Another hand reached faster from beside him. A Sea Whale diver. Both touched the mud around the glow at once. Li Boyuan drove an elbow into the man and shoved him away. His own fingers touched something cool and hard.

The corner of a tile.

So close.

Then the tide returned.

Faster than it had ebbed. Waves surged in, layer upon layer. The rocks vanished one by one. Beneath the surface, the jade-green light brightened and dimmed, brightened and dimmed. Then a churning swell of murky water engulfed it.

The light went out.

In the sampan, Old Hong stretched out a hand as though to catch the vanished glow. It hung trembling in the air for a long time before he slowly lowered it.

“Father,” the old man murmured to the black water. “Sleep a little longer. I’ll… I’ll watch over the water for you.”

Changfeng supported him. Tears fell one by one, but he made no sound.

Li Boyuan surfaced, gasping, every whisker trembling.

“The mud collapsed,” he said. “When the tide swept through, everywhere I could feel sank away. It’s deeper than before. No one can dive the same route again tonight.”

He opened his right hand. The tile’s corner had cut a fine line in his palm. The seawater had washed it pale.

“I touched it.” The diver’s voice trembled. “So close.”

Curses rose from the ship. Sea Whale’s men climbed out of the water one after another, empty-handed.

The man in the woven hat stood at the bow, watching the water turn black again. For a long time, he did not move.

Then he pressed the White Dragon deeper inside his robe.

“One is enough.”

The lion boat’s bow had split. Seawater poured into the hold, and everyone was busy bailing and hauling people out. Far off, the branch office’s patrol boats showed their lamps, held back beyond the reef shoals. The ship weighed anchor. Without lighting a lamp, it slowly vanished into the darkness offshore.

◆

At daybreak, the quay was crowded.

Wrapped in a blanket, Old Hong sat on the dock’s stone steps. Changfeng stayed pressed beside him, never willing to let go. Someone brought hot congee. The old man could not hold it steadily, so Changfeng supported the bowl and fed him mouthful by mouthful. Halfway through he suddenly choked. Changfeng hurried to set the bowl down and gently stroked his back.

Lu Zhaoye lay aboard a Huo boat, blue ointment spread across his chest, his face white as paper. His first words on waking were, “Where’s the glove?”

Qinglan placed it beside his hand. The silver-stitched word “Star” glimmered faintly in the morning light.

“I’ll know him.” Lu Zhaoye gripped the glove, teeth clenched. “Next time. I’ll know him.”

Jinshi crouched beside him, still soaked. His voice was heavy. “Next time, don’t leap at him alone. You leap, I’ll be behind you to take the blow.”

Lu Zhaoye glanced at him. For once, he made no retort. He turned his face away.

Huo Xiao stood at the stern with his blue-green lion, watching them. He said nothing, only took off his outer coat and spread it over Lu Zhaoye.

Qinglan stood on the quay and looked out to sea.

Nothing. The great ship was gone. The patch of red was gone. His father’s White Dragon was gone.

His robe felt empty.

Yuening came to his side, took the ink rubbing from her ledger’s inner pocket and placed it in his hand.

A hand with five spread fingers. Seven points the size of pinpricks.

“Lose one,” she said, “and we still have the other.”

Qinglan held the thin sheet of cotton paper for a long time.

Far away, a gong sounded from the direction of Pinghe Hall.

At the other end of the quay, a middle-aged man in a purple robe approached slowly with a walking stick. Jinghe.

He looked at Old Hong wrapped in his blanket on the steps, at Lu Zhaoye lying in the boat, then at the ink rubbing in Qinglan’s hand. He asked nothing.

“Pinghe Hall’s qualifiers,” he said, “begin at nine.”

He paused. “Meng Chuan says he’ll win for Xiaohe to see. Liuxia says she’ll watch you learn to be quick. I say I’d like to see whether a Tuidao man can still sit steady at the mahjong table after losing his tile.”

Qinglan looked up.

“I can,” he said.

The qualifiers had begun.

(End of Chapter Thirteen)
