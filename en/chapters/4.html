Chapter 4Captain Qi

Before dawn had fully broken, someone knocked at the Rain Pavilion's door.

Three knocks, a pause, then three more. Neither urgent nor heavy, each spaced exactly alike, as though measured with a ruler.

The knocking woke Qinglan. He had spent half the night curled on a bench in the upstairs storytelling room. The frost on his right arm had long since melted into cold sweat, leaving his sleeve stuck to his skin. He tried to brace a hand on the bench, but the arm seemed to belong to someone else, a dead weight hanging from his shoulder. Below the elbow, he felt nothing.

With his left hand, he took the White Dragon from his robe and turned it over.

Only the little engraved hand remained on the back, its five fingers spread. No line. Not a trace.

The moon still hung over the western roofs, white tinged with blue, not yet set after all that night. The Dipper was too faint to see.

Downstairs, someone drew the door bolt.

Jinshi's voice reached the stairs first.

“Who's there? It's not even light! The teahouse isn't open!”

The lion-dance troupe had stayed the night, bedding down in the main hall. Jinshi slept at the foot of the stairs, the broken lion head his pillow. At the first cockcrow he had gone barefoot to search the gutter for its copper eye, feeling his way along half the street. He found no eye, only black mud for both feet; his trouser legs were still wet. Returning, he had dozed against the stairs. At their first creak he sprang up, his red headband askew, his red-and-gold jacket half open, his yellow sash trailing over his feet. Lion head in one hand, the other braced against the rail, he blocked the staircase completely.

In the grey dawn, the crack across the lion's brow looked like a knife scar. Its empty left socket stared blackly towards the door.

Two men remained tied to the hall's pillars: Hengchang hired men subdued by the troupe in the street last night, rags in their mouths, slumped crookedly asleep. No one had seen the one-eyed man since the vine dragged him through the window.

Three people stood outside.

The central figure wore a dark blue-green official robe embroidered at chest and hem with silver-white xiezhi among curling clouds. The wings of his black headpiece extended long and level, edged in gold. Black shoulder guards picked out in gold protected his shoulders, and black leather bracers clasped his forearms. At his waist hung a blade in a copper-bound ebony scabbard, its hilt wrapped with red cord ending in a red tassel.

A short moustache and a small tuft at his chin were neatly trimmed. He was not old, twenty-seven or twenty-eight, but his eyes belonged to someone who had seen many mahjong tables.

To his left stood a man in dark-grey robes and a black headpiece, a large copper tag engraved “Evidence Courier” at his waist, a register held against his chest. Behind him, a man in short dark-grey clothes carried an empty wooden chest with copper-bound corners. Farther back, several horses waited at the crossroads, their reins held by two Guards of Balance in short blue-green clothes.

“Qi Tingjun, Captain of the Bureau of Balance,” said the man in official robes. “This is the evidence courier of the Arbitration Office's Yunchuan branch. We have come for the evidence and to question those involved.”

His voice was neither loud nor soft, each word as level as his knocks. He glanced at the two men tied to the pillars and tilted his head. The two guards entered, untied them and took them outside.

Jinshi did not move.

“There's an injured man upstairs,” he said. “No sleep all night, his arm frozen like an icicle. You want to question him, wait for daylight.”

“It is daylight,” said Qi Tingjun.

A grey-white line shone through the doorway behind him.

Jinshi's neck reddened. He stared at the xiezhi robes for a while, then set the lion head down hard. “Ten years ago, at Wangjiang Teahouse in Tiandu, robes just like those went upstairs. When they went up, Master Qing was still at the table. When they came down, his tiles lay smashed across the floor. I was below, wearing the lion head for the opening. I couldn't stop a thing. Today I'm here. You go up one at a time.”

Qi Tingjun rested a hand on his sword hilt. He did not draw it. Only rested it there.

“Ashi,” someone said from above. “Let them up.”

Qinglan stood holding the rail at the second-floor turn. He still wore the substitute player's robe borrowed from the Tao family, deep green with fresh white lapels, its gold-thread bamboo leaves dulled by last night's frost. The red waist cord had broken once; a length of Xingyu's red hair tie joined it in a firm knot.

Jinshi looked back at him, then at his hanging, motionless right arm. Pressing his lips together, he turned aside and cleared half the stairs.

As Qi Tingjun passed him, his steps paused.

“Your old troupe master's lion,” he said, “was missing an eye too, at the opening outside Wangjiang ten years ago.”

Jinshi froze. By the time he recovered, all three visitors were upstairs.

A lamp was lit in the storytelling room.

Yuening had slept over the accounts table for the latter half of the night. When she rose, a row of little red circles from the abacus beads marked her cheek, making the skin seem softer still. One flower in her twin buns was crooked; ink stained the cuff of her pale yellow dress. She did not fix her hair, only rubbed her nape and tilted her head, drawing a long fine line from ear to collarbone. First she reset the beads of her small ebony abacus, then opened the ledger to last night's page. Only then did she look up.

“Scorekeeper Shen Yuening,” she said. “The evidence is all here.”

Three objects lay on the accounts table.

In the middle, the official wooden box: dark-brown wood, brass clasp, barely wider than two tiles side by side, its seal cut neatly through the centre. To the left, the small flat box supplied by the branch, seal intact, holding the medicine. To the right, Hengchang's red-lacquer tea tray. A paper tag at its rim sealed the needle, still slanting from its lump of wax, together with the tray. The tag read: “Needle to remain with tray; tray to remain with scorekeeper.”

Xiao Cheng'an emerged from the back room with his old folding fan and stood on the other side of the box.

The courier laid his register at a corner of the table. Without touching the box, he first bent to inspect the seal.

“The two fingerprints. Whose?”

“Mine and Master Xiao's,” said Yuening. “Sealed yesterday morning before the branch clerk. The small seal across the join is the branch's.”

The courier brought the two severed ends together. The little vermilion square had split down its middle. Joined, the halves matched exactly.

“Our seal,” he said. “It wasn't altered. A blade passed through it. Who made the cut?”

“A catfolk calling himself Lu Zhaoye. Last night, third quarter of the hour of the Pig. With a blade tip.”

The courier nodded, asked Yuening and Xiao Cheng'an to stand to either side, and opened the lid before them.

Only one tile lay inside. Snow-white bone, a little hand engraved at the lower right of its back, five fingers spread.

“There should be two.”

“He took the old Red Dragon.” Yuening pushed the ledger over. “It's recorded.”

The courier read the line aloud. When he finished, his eyes moved down to the next entry and stopped. Without speaking, he turned the ledger and pushed it towards Qi Tingjun.

Qi Tingjun looked down. Yuening's small, orderly hand had stayed perfectly straight despite her trembling the night before:

“Before leaving, the catfolk told Qinglan: tomorrow at midnight, the old stone archway south of the city; bring your father's White Dragon. Heard by the scorekeeper and Master Xiao.”

The room went very quiet.

Beneath the rear window, Zhumo was pushing a cold bamboo-shoot bun into his mouth. Halfway, he stopped.

Qinglan looked at Yuening. She did not avoid his eyes.

“The ledger stays as it is,” she said. “What someone said is what someone said.”

Qi Tingjun closed the ledger and looked at Qinglan. “A thief cuts open an evidence box before everyone. Instead of fleeing, he first arranges a meeting with you. Do you know each other?”

“No.”

“The old Red Dragon he took was found in your father's wall at Wangjiang Teahouse ten years ago,” said Qi Tingjun. “Who in all Yunchuan wants that tile most?”

Qinglan did not answer.

The answer was too plain. The man who wanted that old Red Dragon most bore the name Qing.

“I won't arrest you,” said Qi Tingjun. “One line isn't enough for that. But it is enough for me to watch you.”

The courier produced a fresh seal. Yuening and Xiao Cheng'an each pressed a fingerprint onto it. On the back he wrote the time, place and remaining contents, copied the same into his register, and asked them to sign.

“One new Red Dragon,” he read. “One old sealing strip, cut edges preserved, the branch seal's two halves matching. Resealed for transport.”

He examined the flat box next. Seal intact, fingerprints clear. Then the tray's paper tag: the needle still slanted from the wax beneath the rim, the paper unbroken.

“These two,” the courier said, “are outside the Arbitration Office's charge.”

Yuening stared.

“The Office handles evidence from matches: tiles, game records, ledgers, agreements.” The courier turned a page. “A needle wounded someone. Medicine was intended for a person's body. That is a case of bodily harm, for the Bureau of Balance. I record only: sealed by the scorekeeper, seals intact, handed over in person to Captain Qi.”

Qi Tingjun did not reach for them.

“Miss Luo's hand has not yet been seen by a doctor,” he said. “I don't know what is on the needle or inside the medicine. Take them away now, and I merely carry two mute objects.” He looked at Yuening. “Is there a doctor in this city who can identify poisons?”

“Doctor Lan, in the east. She went into the hills for herbs and said she would return today,” said Yuening. “Before dawn I sent a waiter to meet her at the east gate.”

“Then we wait for her,” said Qi Tingjun. “Keep them sealed, here. No one touches them.”

The courier added a line to his register without arguing. He turned to Zhumo.

“Chronicler Zhumo. In the notice Master Xiao sent last night, he said you had surrendered something. Tell us yourself.”

The panda swallowed his bun and wiped his mouth on his sleeve. His green bamboo-patterned long robe had short sleeves, exposing two furry forearms. He came to the table and stood rather than sitting.

“At the beginning of the original game record was a strip of writing: the opening search register for the match at Wangjiang Teahouse ten years ago,” he said. “At an inn ten years ago, someone forced me to play a hand. I lost. He left me alone and entered a debt. Last night, he came to collect. I took the strip from my sleeve and passed it through the rear window.”

The courier's brush paused, then continued.

“I remember the words,” said Zhumo. “The upper part was in my hand. I can dictate it, have it written down before the witness. The lower part was the supervisor's sealed endorsement, folded over and sealed across the join. A chronicler must not open it. I never did. That man tore it away before my eyes. I never saw a word inside.”

“Yesterday morning you wouldn't let me copy it,” said Yuening.

“Yesterday morning the paper was still in my sleeve,” said Zhumo. “Then, anyone holding a copy would become a second person for him to pursue. The paper has been surrendered; that half of the debt is cleared. Words entered in the Arbitration Office's register are public business. If he wants them, he must come to the Office first.”

Brush raised, the courier looked from the panda to Yuening. “The scorekeeper remembers too?”

“I memorised it.”

“Then both of you,” said the courier. “One recites, one checks.”

Zhumo spoke. Yuening's lips moved with his. Their voices, one higher, one lower, overlaid every word like two hands building walls at the same table without a single tile out of place:

“Opening search: East, Qing Yuanshan, one White Dragon inside robe, declared as a Heavenly Tile, excluded from tile count, permitted by match supervisor. North, Xuanshu, inside robe—”

They stopped together.

The courier finished the last words, drew a line beneath them and added: remainder torn away; originally the supervisor's sealed endorsement, unopened.

Qi Tingjun had said nothing. At “permitted by match supervisor,” the fingers resting on his hilt moved slightly.

“Why didn't you give him a false strip?” he asked Zhumo.

“A chronicler's hand does not write lies.”

Qi Tingjun looked at him and asked no more.

The courier turned the register for Zhumo's pawprint. The panda pressed hard. Then he tipped the single-marked coin from the little cloth pouch at his waist and laid it beside the book.

“This one is his too.”

The courier looked at the coin, then at Yuening. “Did the scorekeeper seal this?”

“No.”

“Then I cannot receive it,” said the courier. “I record only: Chronicler Zhumo carries one such object.”

Zhumo put the coin back and tied the pouch. “On me, it brings him only to me.”

The courier did not argue. Another single-marked coin lay beneath a time-stamped sheet on the table: the one that had fallen from Zheng Shouzhuo's sleeve before the whole hall two days earlier. Last night Zhumo had laid his own beside it for comparison.

“This one was entered by the scorekeeper and ruled on by the witness. It is evidence.” The courier lifted it with copper tongs.

As it passed Qinglan, cold struck his heart, as though someone had slipped ice down his collar.

The courier placed it in the escort guard's wooden chest. The lid closed, the copper clasp fastened, and the chill slowly withdrew, leaving a faint warmth resting against the White Dragon.

Qinglan pressed his chest without speaking. Yuening looked up from the ledger and glanced at him.

“Where is Zheng Shouzhuo?” the courier asked. “Master Xiao, please come downstairs to witness a statement for his handover.”

Xiao Cheng'an followed him down.

Qi Tingjun stayed.

He stationed the escort guard at the stairs, pulled over a bench and sat opposite Qinglan. From his robe he took a rolled sheet and spread it beneath a tea bowl.

A page of a game record. Small, formal writing, its strokes hard as if cut with a knife.

“A sworn match in Nanchao, three months ago,” Qi Tingjun said. “East won by self-draw on the last turn. The other three immediately accused him of cheating. Spectators joined them. The witness ruled him defeated. The next day, East threw himself into the river.”

He pointed down the record, line by line:

“East's nine discards, in order: North Wind, West Wind, Nine of Characters, One of Dots, Eight of Bamboo, Two of Characters, Five of Dots, Seven of Characters, Six of Characters. Beneath Two of Characters, the chronicler added ‘declared ready’. He had called a pung of East Winds, exposed on the table. On the tenth turn he drew a Five of Dots and won.”

One of Yuening's abacus beads clicked softly. She stilled it herself.

“Your father read tiles,” said Qi Tingjun. “Everyone says his son can too. Read this for me. What did he hold? Why did everyone say he cheated?”

Qinglan studied the page.

There was no breath left to borrow from the White Dragon. Last night had burned away its mark; he could feel not a thread of spiritual power. All he had were his eyes and the things his father had taught him, one by one, beneath the lamp.

He looked first at the opening two tiles.

“North Wind and West Wind were isolated, discarded straight away. Nine of Characters, One of Dots, Eight of Bamboo: edge tiles, unwanted too,” he said. “By the fifth turn, he had no useless honours left. He had a pung of East Winds, and some other pair he was keeping. Otherwise he wouldn't have cleared out both North and West so early.”

“Which pair?”

“I can't tell. Only that he wasn't worried,” said Qinglan. “Then the Two of Characters. He discarded Nine earlier, and now removes a Two. That suggests plenty of Characters in his hand: the Two came from the head of a run. Two-Three-Four-Five. Discard Two, keep Three-Four-Five.”

“Go on.”

“Then Five of Dots.” Qinglan stopped.

He looked at those words for a while, then farther down: Seven of Characters, Six of Characters.

“Wrong,” he said.

Something shifted in Qi Tingjun's eyes.

“He won by drawing Five of Dots,” said Qinglan. “So he was waiting for Five of Dots. A wait for that tile alone would be Four and Six, with the middle missing. If he truly drew a Five on turn seven, Four-Five-Six was complete. He wouldn't discard it.”

“Perhaps he didn't hold Four and Six then.”

Qinglan opened his mouth and had no reply. Qi Tingjun was right. The record did not show what East held on the seventh turn.

“Then all we can call it is suspicious,” he said.

He studied the page again. At the small note beneath Two of Characters, his finger stopped.

“It says he declared ready here.”

“The Nanchao agreement included that provision,” said Qi Tingjun. “Declaring is optional. If a player does, the chronicler notes it.”

“Once declared ready, the hand is locked,” said Qinglan. “Every tile drawn thereafter must be discarded unless it wins. No tile in the hand can be exchanged. Seven and Six of Characters were drawn and discarded on turns eight and nine. Whatever he waited for from turn six remained his wait to the end. Since he won on Five of Dots, he was waiting for Five of Dots from turn six.”

His fingertip rested on the Five of Dots.

“If he had truly drawn it on turn seven, he would have won then. Discard your own winning tile, and you've passed up a win; you may not win again that hand. Someone who has declared ready wouldn't throw his winning tile into the pool, then after two more turns draw the same one to win.”

He looked up. “No one plays like that.”

“Then what is this Five of Dots?”

“It was placed there later,” said Qinglan. “The seventh discard was originally something he drew and threw away. I can't tell what, only that it wasn't Five of Dots. Someone replaced it with a Five, so the discard pool showed him throwing away his own winning tile. The spectators saw that he had discarded Five of Dots, then won by drawing Five of Dots. To them, he must have swapped or stolen a tile.”

He paused, voice lowering. “He held Three-Four-Five of Characters, Five-Six-Seven of Bamboo, Four and Six of Dots, three East Winds exposed as a pung, and another pair. I would guess White Dragons. A man who discards North and West at the start wouldn't break up a pair of White Dragons. His wait was the middle Five of Dots. A self-draw, cleanly won.”

No one in the room spoke.

Beneath the window, Zhumo had forgotten to chew his bun.

Qi Tingjun took a second sheet from his sleeve and laid it over the first. The same hand, but different writing, older ink.

The seventh discard read Nine of Bamboo.

“This is the original record, copied by that match's chronicler that night,” said Qi Tingjun. “Whoever moved the tile altered the discard pool but had no time to alter the chronicler's record. By the time I obtained it, East had been in the river for ten days.”

He rolled the two sheets together and returned them to his robe, very slowly. Qinglan saw his hand. Beneath the cuff of the xiezhi robe, it trembled faintly, like a string brushed by a fingertip.

“Spectators can condemn a man to death on a single discard pool,” said Qi Tingjun.

He stood and took two steps towards the window, his back to everyone. Outside, the mist was dispersing. Someone on Crossroads Street was taking down shop shutters. The old sugar seller had not yet brought out his carrying pole.

“I was at Wangjiang Teahouse ten years ago too,” he said.

Qinglan's left hand clenched.

“I was seventeen. I held the search register and stood behind the match supervisor.” Qi Tingjun did not turn. “After play ended, the supervisor wrote several lines beneath North's entry. I saw him writing, not what he wrote. In the years that followed, the people who were upstairs that day disappeared, one after another.”

He turned, his face unreadable. “I watch you because, for ten years, whenever someone tries to reopen that match, someone dies. Not because your name is Qing.”

A shout suddenly came from below.

Pei Zhiwei. Head cloth on, deep-blue apron tied, he had run from the kitchen to the stairs, still gripping a cleaver. “The rear storeroom! Old Master Zheng—”

Qi Tingjun was first down the stairs.

Qinglan followed through the main hall. The troupe's apprentices had just rolled their bedding, and stood bare-chested, staring. Behind the counter, beneath the shrine, the retired square wooden table held its closed tile box. Qinglan's plain copper coin was jumping against the lid, click-click, just as it had at the hour of the Pig last night.

As he ran past, he saw a hand beside the old table.

Grey-white, ending at the cuff, half transparent, as though through steam. It rested on the box lid, its fingers tapping rapidly like a player urging someone to discard. Each tremor of the coin made it fade; another, and only shadows of a few fingers remained.

The troupe's apprentices saw nothing. One scratched his head. “Who's building tiles over there?”

The clay God of Mahjong above remained as always: purple robe, gold crown, seven stars at the chest, a white tile in its hand. Jinshi had lit a stick of incense at dawn. Its smoke had risen straight, but as Qinglan passed, it inclined briefly towards the rear kitchen, then straightened again.

He did not stop.

The storeroom stood at the back of the Rain Pavilion, beside the alley: stone floor, brick walls, a single high window. Zheng Shouzhuo, confined there last night, still sat on the rice sack against the wall, his indigo robe crumpled, his greying topknot half undone. The blue cloth strip hung by his ear, his goatee in disarray.

The courier had just taken his handover statement and returned to the hall. It had been no more than the time needed to drink a cup of tea since he left that door.

Now a vine circled the old man's neck.

It came through a crack in the rear brick wall, green and jointed like bamboo, thicker than last night's vines. Another rose through a seam in the stone floor like a snake lifting its head from the earth, binding his ankles. The old man clawed at the coil around his neck. His face was purple; his mouth opened without a sound.

“A blade!” Jinshi roared.

As Qinglan burst through the door, the White Dragon inside his robe suddenly chilled.

Not cold. Ice, like a stone freshly hauled from the bottom of a well, pressed to his heart through two layers of cloth. He shuddered. The right arm that had been numb all night jerked, as though something had tugged it from within.

The Five of Bamboo. Very close.

“He's in the rear alley,” Zhumo said behind him. “Holding the tile outside the wall.”

“Why Master Zheng?” Yuening panted, gripping the doorframe. “He told us everything yesterday. What use is silencing him now?”

“He told us his own part,” said Qinglan. “Once the Bureau takes him, they'll ask about what he hasn't told us.”

“More than that.” Zhumo stared at the vine. “Look. The vines fill the room. Why do they leave me alone?”

Qinglan remembered last night. Vines everywhere, circling round Zhumo, whose paw pressed hard against the little pouch at his waist.

“The vines recognise the coin,” Zhumo said quickly. “Carry the coin, and you are his man. The vines go round you. Those are the debt collector's rules. Master Zheng's coin left him two days ago. Now it's sealed in an official chest, beyond his reach. Without it, he is no longer one of the man's people. No longer his, but full of his secrets—”

“So he collects the man as well,” said Qinglan.

How had the vines found him? Qinglan's eyes settled on the old man's hands. Two afternoons earlier, Zheng Shouzhuo had accepted an oilpaper packet from the man in the woven hat in the rear alley, carried it back inside his sleeve as “medicine”, then held it out to Luo Jinke. That man's breath had clung to these hands. Even now, it had not fully dispersed.

The vine tightened. Zheng Shouzhuo's heels scraped twice against the stone, then could no longer move.

Qi Tingjun had already drawn his Balance blade. It left the scabbard with a short sound: narrow and straight, with an oval copper guard. He struck at the vine near the wall crack. A section broke, giving off green vapour. Immediately another thrust through, faster than the first, lunging for his sword wrist. He stepped aside and cut again. Again it broke. Again it grew.

“You can't cut them all,” said Zhumo. “Each pull he makes outside sends another growing in here.”

Qinglan reached into his right sleeve.

He had kept the coin with him. It had landed at his feet last night, two filed marks at its edge, one deep, one shallow, and a line on the back: midnight, old archway, collect one hand.

That man had given it to him. That man had decided he owed a hand.

He crossed the room, crouched and used his left hand to press the coin into Zheng Shouzhuo's clawing hand, closing the old man's fingers around it.

“Hold tight.”

The vine stopped.

The coil around the old man's neck stiffened abruptly, as though someone had whispered in its ear, then loosened a finger's width. Zheng Shouzhuo drew a long, rasping breath like a bellows.

But the vine from the floor did not loosen. Still coiled round his ankles, its tip slowly rose and turned towards Qinglan.

He no longer had a coin.

As the vine tip sprang, Jinshi lunged forward.

The burly man tore open his yellow sash and pulled out something wrapped in old cloth. A shake revealed a dull-grey stone tile, its face worn smooth, its original markings unreadable. He gripped it in his right palm and breathed out.

A faint golden line lit its edge.

Then two circles appeared on its face, one above the other, pale gold, as though someone had traced them in gold dust on the stone.

“Two of Dots,” Zhumo murmured.

Jinshi slammed his palm onto the stone floor.

Two pale-golden rings spread from beneath his hand, one inside the other, travelling flat along the floor and enclosing Qinglan and Zheng Shouzhuo. Dust within the rings stopped at once, hanging bright and motionless in the air. The vine struck the outer circle as though meeting an invisible wall. Its tip curled into a hook and halted less than a foot from Qinglan's brow.

One breath.

Jinshi turned white. Sweat ran from beneath his red headband. Veins stood out on the hand pressed to the floor, and a faint scorched smell rose between his fingers.

“Captain—Qi—” he forced through his teeth. “Quick—”

Qi Tingjun did not strike the wall crack. He stepped beyond the ring, turned his blade down and swept it flat along the stone floor, cutting the vine emerging from the floor seam at its root.

The severed end did not regrow.

Outside the wall, someone grunted in the alley.

The White Dragon inside Qinglan's robe turned cold, then hot, as though a bowl of fresh soup had been pressed against his heart. Slowly it cooled back to the temperature of bone.

Every vine in the room withered together, falling in dry rustles. Blue-grey dust rose from the dead growth and floated through the high window like incense ash.

“The rear alley!” Qi Tingjun snapped.

The escort guard and two troupe apprentices rushed out. By the time they rounded the building, they found only dead leaves at the wall's foot, a single footprint and several yellow bamboo leaves beside it. Ahead lay the rooftops, their tiles wet and empty.

Jinshi released his hand. Both pale-gold rings vanished at once; the suspended dust finally settled. He opened his right palm. A round red burn marked its centre, as though a red-hot copper coin had been pressed there.

He wrapped the stone tile in its old cloth and tucked it back into his yellow sash. His lips still white, he grinned at Qinglan. “Fine. Just a little burn.”

“When did you get that thing?” Qinglan asked.

“Found it under some old bricks while repairing the lion practice ground.” Jinshi scratched his head. “The old troupe master said not to show it in public. Once people see it, they know my affinity.”

“What affinity?” Yuening asked.

Zhumo answered for him. “Dots.”

He came over, crouched by the dead leaves and held one to the light.

“The marks a Heavenly Tile shows belong to its bearer,” he said. “With Bamboo affinity, one's thoughts turn to plants and their connections; each strand appears as a bamboo stalk. The vines you saw were Bamboo breath. With Dots, thoughts turn to the workings of heaven and to objects, and each strand appears as a circle. A Dots bearer sends a breath into the tile, which marks out a space around him. Breath from outside cannot penetrate it. The size of the circle and how long it lasts depend on his number of Dots. Jinshi has two. Two circles, lasting one breath.”

“And the man in the woven hat?” Yuening asked. “The vines were Bamboo. Does that make him Bamboo?”

“If he were, the Five of Bamboo would recognise him. Borrowing its power would come easily. He would not need to wrench it out.” Zhumo crumbled the yellow bamboo leaf. “You saw it. Each time he pulls, a stalk on the tile yellows, and yellow leaves fall from him. Two last night. These leaves yellowed when the vine was cut just now: another stalk. He is not of that affinity. The Five of Bamboo will not give to him, so he uses the glove to tear the power out. Every pull damages the tile.”

“Then what is his affinity?” Qinglan asked.

Zhumo did not answer. He only glanced at Jinshi, then at the circular burn on Jinshi's palm.

Only then did the courier arrive from the hall. Seeing the dead vines, he had the escort guard open the chest and took out an empty box. He was about to lift the most cleanly severed section with his tongs.

The severed vine moved by itself.

Green sap still dripped from its cut end into the stone seam. The yellow tip curled, then straightened, like a worm newly emerged from the earth. Along the floor it inched, arching and easing forward towards the wooden chest beside the escort's feet. In that corner of the chest, Zheng Shouzhuo's coin lay sealed.

It moved an inch.

Qi Tingjun's blade tip descended precisely, pinning the vine between two slabs. Its tip twitched twice more beneath the steel, then stopped.

“Even dead things,” he said, “recognise debt?”

No one answered. Zhumo flattened his ears a little.

Only when the vine was still did the courier lift it with tongs into the empty box, seal it before Xiao Cheng'an and write the time.

“One severed vine,” he read. “One emergence point in the rear alley wall, one in the storeroom floor. Continued crawling towards the copper coin after separation from the tile. Record that too.”

Objects were his concern, not questioning people.

Zheng Shouzhuo gasped against the wall for a long time before he could speak.

He still gripped the two-marked coin, knuckles white. He looked down at it for a while, then held it out to Qinglan.

“Take it back,” he rasped. “This is the one that calls in your debt. Holding it makes me his man, but also makes your hand the one I owe. I cannot owe it for you.”

Qinglan accepted it with his left hand and returned it to his right sleeve.

Qi Tingjun sheathed his blade, untied his ebony waist tag and set it before the old man, face outward. A balance was engraved on the front; on the back, the words “Uphold Balance”.

“Zheng Shouzhuo,” he said. “An arrest warrant of the Bureau of Balance. Concealing an unbranded Red Dragon with intent to plant it on another person; accepting another man's coin and acting for him. Come with me.”

The old man stared at the tag.

His eyes first fixed, then narrowed, as though trying to recognise a face through fog.

“That tag…” he said. “Ten years ago in Tiandu, the hand that dropped a coin before my toes belonged to a man with one just like it at his waist.”

Qi Tingjun's face did not change.

“Who?”

“I don't know who. I saw only the hand and the tag.” The old man rested his head against the wall and closed his eyes. “Someone in the Bureau of Balance has taken a coin too. Captain, that is all I can say. No names. I still have a son carrying sacks at the quay.”

Qi Tingjun watched him a moment and retied the tag at his waist.

“Take him.”

Two Guards of Balance entered and helped Zheng Shouzhuo up, one on each side. He staggered on rising but refused their support, walking with his own hand against the wall. At the door, he looked back.

“Captain, in my room south of the city, there's a camphorwood chest beneath the bed. A letter of recommendation lies at its bottom,” he said. “Lost in that hand, returned to me on the spot. Please send someone to collect it too. Let it form part of my statement.”

Qi Tingjun nodded once.

Passing Qinglan, the old man paused.

“Tonight at midnight,” he whispered, “don't bring the White Dragon.”

By full daylight, Tao Lingtao brought Luo Jinke upstairs.

Proprietor Tao wore the same pink embroidered cross-collared top and dark-brown embroidered skirt, with two fresh pomegranate flowers in her twin buns. But shadows beneath her eyes showed how little she had slept. Behind her, Luo Jinke's red-brown curls were wrapped in a blue headscarf. Tea had blurred part of the blue-and-white pattern on her orange top. Her right hand hung against her chest, swollen shiny to the fingertips. Two nights later, it still would not close. In her left she held the dyeing brush, its tied-on Mahjong Jade swinging green and bright.

With them came Doctor Lan from the eastern quarter.

The waiter had waited a watch at the east gate and, when it opened, met her coming down the mountain road. She still wore her visiting cloak, thick grey wool with a hood, old gold-thread scrollwork along brim and opening. Beneath showed a dark-blue long robe and part of an ivory underskirt, its hem flecked with mountain mud. A thick braid fell over her right shoulder to her chest, ending in a blue tassel. Two small white flowers at her temple also hung with blue tassels; loose hairs at her forehead stuck out from the mountain wind. An elmwood medicine box was strapped to her back, brass at its four corners, a pale-gold flower painted on its face. She still carried a little copper-framed lantern, its wick burning in daylight. Only on entering did she remember to blow it out.

Lan Ruoheng was in her early thirties, her features understated, her voice quiet. Her clothes were fastened close. She walked without haste, yet with a steadiness like mountain bamboo, whose resilience became clear only after the wind passed. She set her medicine box on the table and opened its copper clasps before looking up to nod to everyone.

Taking Luo Jinke's wrist, she turned the hand over, examined the puncture at the thumb's web and leaned close to smell it.

“The needle was coated,” she said. “Bone-numbing herb juice.”

“Bone-numbing herb?” Tao Lingtao asked.

“A plant from the southern hills. Its smoke stiffens the hands when burned. Boil it down and coat a needle, and it spreads on entering the blood.” Lan Ruoheng took a small porcelain bottle from her box and shook powder over the puncture. “Not fatal. But the fingers stiffen for three to five days, unable to hold things steadily.”

“Three to five days.” Luo Jinke clenched her teeth. “Just enough to lose a sworn match.”

Yuening lifted the red-lacquer tea tray from the accounts table and set it, paper tag and all, before Lan Ruoheng. Hengchang had brought it themselves, their name branded on the back; the Pavilion's trays were plain. The needle still slanted from the wax beneath the rim, its tag reading, “Needle to remain with tray; tray to remain with scorekeeper.” Since being sealed yesterday morning, it had remained on the accounts table—the same table over which Yuening had slept last night.

“I sealed it,” Yuening told the courier. “Before the branch clerk.”

The courier inspected the tag and nodded. Only then did Yuening remove it.

Lan Ruoheng lifted the needle with tweezers, examined it against the window light and held it beneath her nose. “The same substance. No one in this city knows this coating method. The herb isn't local either. A few Nanchao seafood merchants send it north with dried kelp, hidden inside the bundles. At checkpoints, its smell goes unnoticed.”

“Dried kelp,” said Qinglan.

Yuening was already sliding the flat box towards her.

“Master Zheng brought this medicine two days ago, saying it was for Miss Luo's hand,” she said. “He himself said he could smell dried kelp through the oilpaper. We sealed it in the branch box yesterday morning. We've been waiting for you.”

The courier checked the seal and entered in his register: “Small branch box opened publicly for examination of medicine by doctor.” Yuening and Xiao Cheng'an each pressed a fingerprint. Only then did he break the strip.

The oilpaper and hemp cord were dry, just as that day. Lan Ruoheng untied the cord. Inside the paper was a wrinkled black-brown layer; as she lifted it, a rank smell filled the room.

“Kelp.” Jinshi pinched his nose.

Inside the kelp lay the medicine itself, grey-green powder fine as incense ash. Lan Ruoheng rubbed a pinch between her fingertips, smelled it, touched it with her tongue and spat it out.

For the first time, she frowned.

“This is real medicine,” she said. “Mung-bean skins, liquorice and two southern roots. A remedy for bone-numbing herb. Applied to the hand, it would halve the time needed for the swelling to subside.”

Everyone stared.

Lan Ruoheng continued, spreading the kelp bit by bit with her tweezers. Several leaf fragments lay caught in its folds, smaller than fingernails, grey-brown with serrated edges. She lifted one and placed it beside the needle at the tray's edge.

“Bone-numbing herb,” she said. “From the same bundle of goods.”

“The people who sent the poison and the people who sent the medicine…” Luo Jinke stared at the leaf fragment. “The same?”

“Not necessarily the same person,” said Lan Ruoheng. “But whoever prepared this medicine knew what was on the needle. Without that knowledge, they couldn't have made this packet.”

Qinglan looked at the grey-green powder.

He understood why Master Zheng had dared carry it so openly through the door. A doctor would expose false medicine at once. Real medicine gave no grounds for objection. If the medicine was real, the man bringing it was a kindly helper. Approaching Luo Jinke then gave him the chance to pass on the unbranded Red Dragon hidden in his sleeve.

That man had even planned for an inspection.

The courier wrapped the powder, kelp and leaf fragments as before, returned them to the little box and resealed it. He wrote: “One packet of medicine, identified as a remedy for bone-numbing herb; several fragments of bone-numbing herb leaves found within wrapping. Resealed. Handed in person, with one tea tray and one needle, to Captain Qi of the Bureau of Balance.”

This time Qi Tingjun held out his hand. He wrapped the tray and needle in cloth and gave them, with the box, to a Guard of Balance, then signed the courier's register himself.

“Tea tray and needle, one box containing medicine,” Yuening said as she wrote. “Transferred to Captain Qi of the Bureau of Balance.”

“The servant who carried the tray?” Qi Tingjun asked.

“Hengchang says he took leave the night before last to visit his sick mother, without even rolling his bedding,” said Yuening. “He had not returned by this morning.”

Qi Tingjun said nothing, only glanced at the doorway. A guard turned and went downstairs.

Lan Ruoheng left the small porcelain bottle with Luo Jinke, instructing her to apply it morning and evening and keep away from the dye vats for seven days. Luo Jinke drew her slung hand closer and nodded without bravado. Before leaving, Lan Ruoheng shouldered her medicine box, fastened her cloak and glanced back at Qi Tingjun.

“If your inquiry reaches the Nanchao seafood merchants,” she said, “ask what goods they sent to the hill behind the northern academy this spring.”

She picked up her lantern and went downstairs.

Scarcely had Lan Ruoheng left when someone knocked at the door below.

A boy of twelve or thirteen had come, dressed in blue cotton, sleeves rolled, a flat book box held to his chest. Sweat covered his head from running. He stood at the stairs, afraid to come up, until Jinshi brought him up bodily.

“Master Wen sent this.” The boy presented the box to Qinglan. “The academy lost its rear hill to Hengchang last month. Master Wen and Master Du petitioned for a fresh examination. Headmaster Yanqing says the original ledger will be reviewed in person at the academy this afternoon. He asks Young Master Qing to help read the game record.”

Wen Yanli taught mathematics at the academy. In his forties, he wore half-rimmed copper-wire spectacles. Few in Yunchuan knew him, but many knew those spectacles.

Qinglan opened the book box.

Inside lay a freshly copied game record and a note. Headmaster Yanqing's note gave only the time and place. The academy's own chronicler had copied the record. Its first line was neatly written:

“Third turn. South discards Two of Dots.”

Zhumo's reaching paw stopped in midair.

Qinglan stopped too.

Two nights earlier, as Yuening's finger had passed line by line down the original Wangjiang record, the first sentence she read had been exactly this.

Third turn. South discards Two of Dots.

Yuening leaned in, her finger moving down the record faster and faster. Her face changed. “Third turn, South discards Two of Dots. West calls pung, exposing three. Seventh turn, East discards another. Eleventh turn, North discards one more…”

Her fingertip stopped on the final line.

“Two of Dots.” She looked up. “Five of them appeared in this hand.”

A set held only four of each tile.

At Wangjiang Teahouse ten years ago, five Twos of Dots had appeared too.

Zhumo's paw dropped to his knee and stayed there for a long time.

Qi Tingjun had stood silent by the window. At “Two of Dots” he turned towards the scroll, and for the first time something different showed in his eyes.

Qinglan remembered his words: in the years that followed, the people upstairs that day had disappeared, one after another.

He did not ask. Qi Tingjun did not explain.

“I'll go to the academy this afternoon too,” was all the Captain said. “Reviewing old ledgers and examining loopholes in the original agreement belong to the Arbitration Office. Who passed a tile, who concealed a needle: those belong to the Bureau of Balance. I won't sit at the table. Only watch.”

As the boy prepared to leave, Yuening stopped him and put a handful of roasted chestnuts from a fruit dish into his pocket. Blushing, he ran downstairs, feet drumming on the steps.

Before noon, Qinglan accompanied Yuening to the eastern Arbitration Office branch. A copy of every match's scorekeeper ledger had to be filed there. She had not yet submitted the one from two days earlier.

The mist had cleared. Half the breakfast stalls on Crossroads Street had packed up, the last wisps of steam rising from their baskets. Red paper scraps left by yesterday's lion-awakening ceremony still clung in the paving seams, trampled into mud. The old sugar seller had set up in his usual spot. Seeing Qinglan, he raised the half-finished sugar lion in his hand.

They walked side by side, neither speaking first. Yuening held her ledger against her chest, the abacus hanging from her wrist, its beads knocking with each step. She had had no time to comb her hair that morning. The crooked flower in her bun slid lower as she walked, unnoticed. Sunlight from her side shone through loose hairs at her temple; her earlobe was thin enough to glow faintly red. Her skirt brushed back and forth against her ankles, one pale ankle appearing and disappearing beneath the green trim. Qinglan looked twice, then dared look no more.

The flower slipped another fraction. Qinglan glanced three more times. On the fourth, he finally raised his left hand and nudged it back into place.

Yuening's step faltered. She neither turned nor spoke. The tips of her ears reddened first.

“That line in the ledger,” said Qinglan. “You could have left it out.”

“No,” said Yuening. “Leave it out, and I conceal a statement for you. Next time you win a match, someone checks my ledger and finds a missing line. Then that victory won't stand either.”

She stopped and turned to him. “Because I wrote it, Captain Qi knows the cat arranged to meet you. Because I wrote it, he was willing to tell us he was there ten years ago. The ledger says what happened. No one can claim you concealed it.”

Qinglan looked at her. The abacus marks had not quite faded from her cheek. A few round impressions lined her cheekbone, like a calculation left unfinished.

“I didn't take the old Red Dragon,” he said.

“I know,” said Yuening. “The cat took it. I saw him. That is what the ledger says too.”

Wheels suddenly rattled behind them. A tofu cart burst from an alley, its driver running and shouting, “Make way!” Wooden trays clattered on its bed.

Yuening had lowered her head to the ledger and did not hear. Qinglan reached to pull her aside, raised his right arm halfway and remembered it was still numb. He switched to his left, caught her wrist and drew her towards him.

The cart brushed her skirt and sent a spray of tofu water splashing up.

She turned sideways into his arms, her shoulder against his chest, her twin buns brushing his chin with a tickle. She smelled of ink, a little soap pod and dust from the storeroom. Through two layers of clothing, he felt her heart racing. Whether from fright or something else, he could not tell.

His hand did not release her wrist. Somehow his other arm had come round her waist. That right hand was still numb, unable to feel warmth or cold, but he could see it resting on her slender waist, see her waist rise and fall with her breathing. The arm numb for two days seemed almost ready to wake. Only two breaths. Yet for those two breaths he could not think of a single tile. He only wanted to keep holding her.

Neither moved.

After two breaths, Yuening withdrew her wrist first and lowered her head, straightening the ledger's corners over and over. Her ears were scarlet all the way to the lobes.

The spray had soaked the side of her skirt. A large patch of pale yellow deepened from the waist down, its green border darkened almost to ink. She could not see it and continued fussing with the ledger.

Qinglan shifted his eyes to a tuft of foxtail grass atop the wall and studied it with great concentration.

With his left hand he began undoing the outer substitute's robe. One-handed, he fumbled for some time before slipping it off.

“What are you doing?”

“Your skirt is wet.” He held out the robe, eyes still on the wall. “Put this round you.”

Yuening reached behind herself and stiffened. She snatched the robe, wrapped it around her waist and tied the two sleeves in front, quick and fierce as though flicking a stubborn abacus bead.

“Your left hand,” she said, her voice a little muffled. “Your right arm is still numb. Don't try to be a hero.”

“Mm.”

Qinglan put his left hand behind his back, the warmth of her wrist still in his palm. He wanted to say something. At his lips the words turned, becoming something else:

“Thank you. For that line in the ledger.”

Yuening looked up at him once, then quickly lowered her head.

“What for?” she said. “I'm the scorekeeper. Keeping the ledger is my duty.”

But the knot of sleeves at her waist tightened again.

“The robe,” she said. “Once I've changed, I'll have someone return it to the Rain Pavilion at noon. It belongs to the Tao family. Xingyu's hair tie is still on it. I can't afford to soil it.”

She resumed walking. After two steps, she said softly, “But tonight, you're going.”

“I'm going.”

“The coin names the same time and place as the cat,” she said. “The man who invited you says bring the White Dragon. Master Zheng says don't bring it. You told me all that just now.”

Qinglan reached inside his robe with his left hand and touched the White Dragon. The bone was warm; there was no mark on its back. Then he felt the coin in his right sleeve, two filed cuts, one deep, one shallow, a line of tiny words on the reverse.

“There's no mark left on the White Dragon,” he said. “Taking it or leaving it makes no difference.”

“It does,” said Yuening. “When the coin was sealed away, the White Dragon chilled, then warmed. In the storeroom it chilled again. I saw you press your chest.”

Qinglan did not deny it.

Qi Tingjun's man was already waiting at the branch entrance to check the courier's handover register. The Captain himself leaned against a stone lion beside the door, ebony tag at his waist, the red cord on his scabbard lifting in the breeze. He glanced at the substitute's robe round Yuening's waist and asked nothing.

“At midnight,” he said, “my men stay outside the archway.”

Qinglan looked at him.

“If they go in, neither of those two will come,” said Qi Tingjun. “You enter alone. My men will be one li beyond the archway. Call, and they come. If you don't, they wait until dawn. Whether you call is yours to decide.”

He paused, then added, “Zheng Shouzhuo said not to bring the White Dragon. I won't tell you whether to bring it. I only want you to tell me when you return whether that hand is the left or the right.”

Qinglan remembered the man hanging beneath the eaves, the black-gloved right hand with its gold-thread hand on the back, holding a Five of Bamboo. He had seen two stalks yellow. Zhumo said the attack in the rear alley had cost another.

Five of Bamboo. Five stalks. Three yellow, two still green.

He gripped the coin in his sleeve and looked at the sky. The sun had climbed above the ridge. Twelve hours remained until midnight.

(End of Chapter Four)
