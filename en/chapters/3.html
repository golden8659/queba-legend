Chapter 3Half a Finger

The frost reached the windowsill and stopped.

Qinglan did not move. His right hand was still numb; he could only press his left onto the White Dragon. Its face stabbed cold into his palm, like a stone freshly drawn from a well. Beneath the frost, the blue-green line finer than spider silk, only just restored in the third watch, flickered like a wick about to die in the wind.

Zhumo was faster. With one paw he rolled up the strip of paper and tucked it into his cuff. He swept the three coins into the lamp’s shadow and turned, blocking the window with his back.

Not a sound came from the eaves. Yuening slept wrapped in the old felt blanket. Xiao Cheng'an’s snores rose and fell like someone working a broken bellows by the door.

But the window paper sounded.

Tap.

Tap.

Not wind. Something tapped against the paper at regular intervals, as evenly as a player drumming a fingernail on the table edge while waiting for a discard.

Qinglan looked by the lamplight. A black thread hung outside the window, a copper coin tied to its end. Someone was lowering it from the eaves, letting it knock against the lattice, now softly, now harder. The coin turned. Light grazed its edge, revealing a narrow filed mark, like half a finger.

“Master Zhu.” The man on the eaves spoke, low and unhurried, as though discussing an ordinary bargain. “Ten years.”

Zhumo’s paw tightened slowly around his cuff.

“Tomorrow night is the fifteenth,” the voice said. “I’ll come for that strip of paper. You will hand it out yourself.”

The thread rose; the coin vanished. A faint sound on the roof tiles, and then only Xiao Cheng'an’s snoring remained.

Frost slowly withdrew from the White Dragon, became a film of moisture, then dried. The spider-silk line remained, so fine one scarcely dared breathe.

Only then did Qinglan realise his back was soaked in cold sweat.

He turned. The panda still faced away from the window, round ears flattened against his head like a rain-drenched dog’s.

“He was right above us.” Qinglan kept his voice low. “The paper’s in your sleeve. You’re in this room. He could reach in and take it. Why wait until tomorrow night?”

Zhumo reached for his own coin, then withdrew his paw halfway and left it on the table.

“Because this is a debt being collected,” he said. “Something seized doesn’t count as repayment. His kind have their rules: show a coin, name an hour, and the debtor hands the thing over himself. Only then is the debt cancelled.”

“Why choose the fifteenth tomorrow?”

“Look at the sky.”

Qinglan raised his eyes. Outside, the moon was nearly full, white tinged with blue. It crowded the Dipper into a corner of the roof, its seven stars faint as seven grains of undissolved salt.

“The moon is fullest on the fifteenth. Moonlight overwhelms starlight. Your tile won’t recover a single thread all night,” said Zhumo. “Tomorrow the streets will be full of people watching the lion-awakening ceremony. Once the drums and gongs begin, you could overturn a table upstairs and no one below would hear.” The panda paused. “He chose the day.”

“Will you give it to him?”

Zhumo did not answer. Holding his basket, he sat against the wall, eyes fixed on the window, and kept them open until dawn.

At daybreak, a thin frost coated the eaves.

There should have been no frost in the fifth month. It covered only a foot-long stretch above the storytelling room’s rear window, its edges neat, as though someone had crouched there all night and left his cold behind. Sunlight melted it. It ran down the tile grooves and fell drop by drop onto the sill.

The dripping woke Yuening.

She sat up, the old blanket sliding halfway from her shoulders. Still half asleep, she raised her arms and stretched. Her sleeves slipped to her elbows, revealing pale forearms in the morning light. Her waist arched backwards with the stretch, then softened again. Qinglan, standing by the window, quickly turned his eyes to the drop at the eaves. She looked down at the blanket for a while, did not ask who had covered her, and folded it into a square beside Xiao Cheng'an’s feet. Only then did she reach for her ledger. On last night’s page, the blood over “He was cold too” had dried brown.

Her own handkerchief was still clenched in her hand. The bloodstain had darkened. She turned it over, folded it small and tucked it deep inside her sleeve pocket.

“Three copper coins,” she said. “Before I fell asleep, there were two.”

She glanced towards the rear window. A fringe of frost still hung beneath the eaves, not quite melted despite the sun. “Frost in the fifth month,” she said. “Who came last night?”

Zhumo told her what had happened, slowly, adding not a word. At “You will hand it out yourself,” his voice roughened.

Yuening took up her brush and recorded it all. When she finished, she looked up. “That paper goes into the sealed box this morning too.”

“No.”

“Zhumo—”

“He comes for whoever holds it.” The panda pressed his cuff against his chest. “In my sleeve, he comes only for me. Seal it in the box, and he comes for the box. If you hold the box, he comes for you.”

Yuening bit her lip. “Then I’ll make a copy.”

“If you copy it, your ledger becomes something he wants.” Zhumo looked at her. “Miss Yuening, your ledger must still serve as evidence.”

Yuening said no more. She laid down her brush and stared at Zhumo for a long time. Then she closed her eyes, her lips moving as though reciting a text. When she opened them, she said only:

“I’ll keep it in my head.”

Qinglan took his own woodshed coin from the table and returned it to his right sleeve. Zheng Shouzhuo’s coin, entered into Yuening’s ledger the night before, was evidence. It remained beneath a sheet of paper bearing the time.

Only now did Zhumo put his coin back into the little cloth pouch at his waist. He pulled the mouth tight with his teeth, trying twice before he managed it.

The porter at the Arbitration Office’s eastern branch opened the door at daybreak.

There was a reply to last night’s notice: the evidence courier was out checking records in the countryside. He would return early tomorrow, the same day as the Captain from Tiandu.

Four of them went. Yuening carried the evidence; Xiao Cheng'an walked with his stick; Qinglan held the cloth-wrapped Hengchang tray in his left hand. Zhumo followed with his basket, unwilling to remain alone at the Pavilion. Two waiters guarded the storeroom.

Passing the eastern street of apothecaries, Qinglan took a second look. Doctor Lan’s shop was shuttered. A note had been pushed into the door crack, its ink blurred by dew: “Gathering herbs in the hills. Back the day after tomorrow. Urgent cases, please go to North Street.”

“The place on North Street can only prescribe for fever,” said Yuening. “Miss Luo’s hand has to wait, whether it can afford to or not.”

The branch was much smaller than Qinglan had imagined: a single shopfront, a long desk, a wall of wooden pigeonholes stuffed with records. In the corner stood an iron-bound cabinet with a copper lock, dust in its keyhole. Behind the desk sat an old clerk in a dark-grey robe and black cloth headpiece, a small copper tag at his waist. He was wiping an inkstone with a rag.

He heard Xiao Cheng'an out, then examined the objects laid on the desk: two Red Dragons; an empty official wooden evidence box obtained from the Rain Pavilion’s counter; a red-lacquer tea tray with a needle marked by a paper tag at its rim; a sealed oilpaper medicine packet; and Yuening’s ledger.

“The scorekeeper recorded everything in person, with the witness present. Left unsealed overnight, every use entered.” The old clerk closed the ledger. “No fault in the procedure.”

“Then please accept the evidence,” said Yuening.

“I cannot.”

Yuening’s brows shot up.

“Patience, young lady.” The clerk pointed to the locked cabinet. “The evidence-store key is at the courier’s waist. He is away; I cannot open it. Branch rules require his signature for receiving evidence. A clerk can only witness its sealing. If I take these things, where do I lock them tonight? Under my desk?” He shook his head. “We shut up at dark. There’s only one porter overnight, seventy years old and hard of hearing. Your Pavilion is safer.”

He stood and took a flat wooden box from a pigeonhole. Dark-brown wood, a brass clasp, a little shorter than a palm.

“The Pavilion had already been issued a box, and you’ve brought it. Good. This one is for the medicine.” He also produced a roll of white sealing strips and a box of seal paste. “Seal them here. I’ll witness it, record the time and stamp the branch seal across the joins. Then take them back as they are and guard them. Tomorrow, when the courier returns, he will receive them in person at your Pavilion. Evidence is handed over where the incident occurred. That too is the rule.”

Yuening looked at Xiao Cheng'an. He nodded.

The Pavilion’s official box was small, holding only two tiles. Yuening checked both Red Dragons and placed them side by side, face up, filling it exactly. The old one was yellowed bone, its red symbol faded dark brown, a rice-grain chip beneath the final vertical stroke. The little hand on its back had all five fingers intact, black grime filling the cuts. The new Red Dragon was snow-white bone, with the same little hand on its back, but a stroke missing from the little finger, white dust still in its cuts.

As the new tile went in, the White Dragon at Qinglan’s heart chilled. Once the lid closed and the clasp snapped shut, the chill faded, as though someone had covered a well with a stone slab.

Xiao Cheng'an cut a narrow white sealing strip, laid it across the clasp and folded its ends down onto the box. At one end Yuening wrote the date, “Two items of evidence,” and “Scorekeeper Shen Yuening.” At the other, Xiao Cheng'an wrote, “Witness Xiao Cheng'an.”

“Fingerprints.”

Two vermilion prints, one on either side, like fresh drops of blood. The old clerk stamped a small square seal across the join and entered the time in his register.

The medicine went into the branch’s box and received its own seal. The tray was too large for either box. The clerk used a paper strip to seal the needle to the rim, writing, “Needle to remain with tray; tray to remain with scorekeeper.”

“The Captain from Tiandu,” the clerk said as he blew the ink dry, seemingly in passing, “someone came this morning to ask when he would arrive.”

“Who?” Qinglan asked.

“Hengchang’s man. Sent by Steward Qian, he said. Wanted to know which day the courier would enter the city, and which day the Captain would.” The clerk closed his register. “I told him truthfully. We keep no secrets here.”

At the Rain Pavilion’s entrance, Xiao Cheng'an looked up at the stretch of eaves where the frost had melted.

“Hengchang knows,” he said. “So naturally does the man on the roof. Between tonight and tomorrow morning, they have this one night.”

Zheng Shouzhuo had not slept all night in the rear storeroom.

His indigo robe was crumpled; half his greying hair hung loose. He held the old bamboo-backed tile between his fingers but no longer turned it. Sitting against the wall on a sack of rice, he looked like an old carrying pole discarded in a corner.

Xiao Cheng'an brought a small stool to the doorway and sat. Yuening’s ledger lay open on her knees. Qinglan stood behind her, seeing only the crown of her head and its flowers, the faint movement of her shoulders as she wrote. Zhumo crouched outside the doorframe, somehow having acquired one of Pei Zhiwei’s bamboo-shoot buns.

“Brother Zheng,” said Xiao Cheng'an, “a witness does not conduct criminal interrogations. Miss Yuening will record every word you say, nothing added or omitted, and hand it over when the Bureau of Balance arrives. You need not speak.”

The old man looked at the folded fan in his hand and smiled more painfully than if he had cried.

“Old Xiao, you’ve told stories all your life,” he said. “Never heard of the half-finger?”

Xiao Cheng'an did not answer.

“They were scoring coins,” Zheng Shouzhuo said slowly. “At the last Tianyuan Mahjong Assembly, eleven years ago, players used these instead of silver. One coin per hand. Lose a hand, surrender a coin. When the Assembly ended, the Scorekeepers' Institute collected them against a register, one by one, to melt them down. None should ever have appeared in circulation again.”

“And the filed mark on the edge?” Qinglan asked.

“The half-finger.” The old man raised his right hand and bent his forefinger, showing only half. “It means you’ve lost at their table and still owe a hand.”

He lowered his hand to his knee.

“Ten years ago, someone invited me to play in Tiandu. Some distinguished guests needed a fourth, they said. Travel and lodgings paid. I ran a mahjong house. My name was on none of the Arbitration Office’s grade lists, nor had I ever hoped it would be. But I was proud then, thought no one south of the city could beat my tiles.” His mouth twitched. “The game was rigged. I lost the deed to my hall, then my son’s letter of recommendation to the Scorekeepers' Institute. An old Institute clerk had written it himself, in gratitude for ten years of lessons I’d given his grandson.”

“A loss is a loss,” Zhumo said indistinctly around his bun. “How did you still owe another hand?”

“Because they wouldn’t collect.” Zheng Shouzhuo looked at him. “They returned the deed and the letter on the spot. Just tossed one of these before my toes and said, ‘Master Zheng, we’ll leave this hand on account.’”

Zhumo stopped chewing.

“For the next ten years, whenever someone showed me an identical coin, I did one thing for him. No asking who, no asking why. Refuse, and the agreement recording my loss would be posted on my hall’s door the next day.” He rested his head against the wall. “Everyone in the city trusted me. They never wanted my tiles. They wanted this face. My son carries sacks at the quay now.”

“If they returned the letter,” Qinglan asked, “why is your son still at the quay?”

Zheng Shouzhuo looked down at his hands, liver spots across their backs, knuckles callused by decades of drawing tiles and building walls.

“I’ve kept it at the bottom of a chest for ten years. Never dared let him use it,” he said. “Something lost at their table and bestowed on me again. Take it into the Institute, and on his first day someone would put a coin in his hand too. My debt is mine to repay.” He smiled. “He doesn’t know. Thinks his father lost badly and gambled away his future. He’s hated me for ten years. Let him carry sacks. A porter’s hands are clean.”

No one spoke. Yuening’s brush whispered across the page. At that sentence it paused, then recorded every word unchanged.

“The medicine,” said Qinglan. “The packet you brought yesterday.”

“The man in the woven hat gave it to me himself in the rear alley. Said it would be useful for Miss Luo’s hand,” the old man said. “I didn’t open it. But my nose still works. Through the oilpaper, a rank smell of dried kelp.” He glanced at Qinglan. “Yunchuan is nowhere near the sea.”

Yuening recorded every word, then added a small note beside it: medicine sealed; awaiting a doctor’s examination.

“And the servant who carried the tray,” Qinglan said.

“Don’t know him.” Zheng Shouzhuo shook his head. “I only handled my part. His was another coin.”

A waiter poked half his head through the door and answered for him. That morning, Cook Pei had gone to borrow charcoal from Hengchang’s warehouse and casually asked. They said the servant had requested leave the night before, claiming his mother in the countryside was gravely ill. He had left without even taking his bedding.

“Where in the countryside?” Yuening asked.

The waiter shook his head.

Yuening began a new line: one Hengchang tea-serving attendant, whereabouts unknown since last night.

“Young Master Qing,” Zheng Shouzhuo called suddenly.

Qinglan looked at him.

“The unbranded Red Dragon was meant to be found on you,” the old man said softly. “Steward Qian shouted ‘Search him’ so early. Did you think he was merely flustered? He was reading his lines. I returned with the medicine to get close to Miss Luo. That put me nearest you.” His gaze dropped to Qinglan’s right sleeve. “Of yesterday’s two coins, one was yours, wasn’t it?”

Qinglan did not answer.

“He never puts a coin into your hand,” Zheng Shouzhuo said. “He drops it before you and makes you bend to pick it up. Mine landed at my toes. Where did yours land?”

The woodshed. Straw. The voice beyond the woodpile saying, “I’ve paid your fare.” A copper coin falling, bouncing on the straw and stopping before his nose.

He had picked it up. At the time, he had thought only of the afternoon boat.

“Pick it up, and you acknowledge it,” said Zheng Shouzhuo. “He calls it boat fare, and you think it is boat fare. When he comes to collect, you learn its worth. Never the single copper. Always the hand behind it.”

Outside the doorframe, Zhumo pressed a paw to the little cloth pouch at his waist, then let go.

“One other thing,” the old man said. “After the last Assembly, the Institute collected the scoring coins one by one against the register. Against mine, someone signed and wrote, ‘Melted down.’” He raised his eyes. “Go and ask whose signature it was.”

Yuening’s brush paused. Then she recorded the sentence exactly, even the words “Go and ask”.

Xiao Cheng'an rose and returned the stool to its place.

“We’ll stop here. Your handprint, Brother Zheng. This page goes at the back of the ledger. You wait for the Bureau.”

Zheng Shouzhuo pressed a heavy thumbprint onto the page. Then he tucked the old bamboo-backed tile between his fingers and slowly turned it. After two turns he stopped and spoke, to no one in particular:

“Tonight is the fifteenth. Don’t sleep.”

On the fifteenth, the Rain Pavilion opened no mahjong tables.

The street outside filled from afternoon onward. Below, the old sugar-figure seller set up his stall, syrup bubbling in its pan. The neighbouring cloth shop hung red silk above a leafy lettuce with a red packet tucked in its heart: the greens the lion would pluck that night. The lion-dance troupe had made a new lion head, red-faced with a golden mane, a small copper mirror on its brow and two polished copper bosses for eyes. It rested on a bench at the crossroads, awaiting nightfall, when it would be carried into the Rain Pavilion for its eyes to be dotted.

“Even ghosts fear an awakened lion,” the old sugar seller told the children gathering around as he blew a sugar figure. “Copper eyes, shaking drums. Copper consumes foul things; the shaking scatters them. Whenever something unclean used to trouble the streets, one lion dance would put it to rest.”

From the upstairs window, Qinglan listened closely.

Copper consumed stray breath; his father had taught him that. Drums dispersing it was new to him, but it made sense. Stray breath floated; a shock could scatter it. Yet last night’s visitor on the eaves was no stray breath.

He looked down into a sea of heads. Tao Lingtao stood with Luo Jinke beside the sugar stall. Luo Jinke’s right hand hung in a sling against her chest, swollen shiny from its back to its fingertips, unable to close. Yet she still held her dyeing brush in her left. Yesterday’s Mahjong Jade swung from a blue thread tied to its handle. Tao Lingtao kept hers in a tightly closed purse, touching it every so often.

Xingyu stood behind the crowd in pink, a plain white cord now binding her side bun, a little flower pinned to it. The wind swept her pink skirt aside, briefly tracing a slender leg beneath; when it died, the outline vanished. She watched the lion for a while, then raised her eyes to the second-floor window.

Qinglan looked down at his waist. The red cord Xingyu had mended with her hair tie still hung there, its join a firm little knot.

When he looked up again, she had turned back to the lion.

“She’s watching the lion,” Yuening said behind him. Her abacus clacked. “What are you watching?”

“The street,” said Qinglan. “So many people. Anyone could slip in unnoticed.”

Yuening’s abacus paused, then resumed faster than before.

In the first watch of the night, the gongs and drums began.

First a great drum: boom, boom, boom, each beat deeper than the last, making the floorboards tremble. Then gongs and cymbals burst together. The crowd fell back on both sides, opening a path. The troupe danced from the crossroads with the lion head whose eyes were not yet dotted, and entered the Rain Pavilion’s hall.

On the east wall behind the counter, a clay figure stood in the Mahjong God’s shrine. Purple robe, gold crown, seven stars embroidered across the chest, one hand holding a tile. The tile was white: only white powder on the clay, nothing painted on it.

From the head of the stairs, Qinglan saw that blank tile and felt his own White Dragon settle faintly against his heart.

As a child he had asked his father which tile the God of Mahjong held. No one knew, his father said. Clay sculptors followed the old likeness, and the old likeness was blank. The waiters put it more bluntly: “Worship does no good. Not worshipping makes you uneasy.”

The lion head bowed three times before the shrine. Beneath it, the old table stood quiet, Qinglan’s copper coin resting on the tile box.

The troupe leader stepped from the crowd and took a brush laden with cinnabar. Almost thirty, he wore a red headband, fitted red-and-gold clothes and a yellow silk sash tied tight at the waist. His sleeves were rolled to the elbows; his shoulders were broad, his arms thick as door bars. Standing before the lion, he touched the brush to the copper boss of its left eye.

As he moved to dot the right, he glanced at the stairs.

The brush fell to the floor.

One stride over the lion head, two bounds up the stairs, and the man stood before Qinglan, enormous hands outstretched, looking him up and down.

“Dressed like a spring onion…” His voice broke. “Xiaolan! Is that Xiaolan?”

Before Qinglan could speak, the man swept him up, feet off the floor. His head struck a beam with a thud.

“Put him down!” Yuening shouted behind them. “His arm is still frozen!”

The man set him down, stepped back, then forward again. He reached to clap Qinglan’s shoulder but withdrew halfway, as though afraid he might break him.

“Ashi.” At last Qinglan retrieved the name from more than a decade ago. “You’re Ashi.”

Jinshi grinned. As he grinned, his eyes reddened.

Back then, the Tuidao School’s headquarters had been in Tiandu, with a branch south of Yunchuan. Sent on School business, Qing Yuanshan had lived there with his family for several years, keeping the gates closed and seldom appearing in the city’s mahjong houses or teahouses. At fourteen or fifteen, Jinshi had crouched on the branch’s threshold every day to hear Qing Yuanshan explain tile strategy. He kept coming for two years. Later Qing Yuanshan told him he was too hot-blooded to sit still at a table: first he should learn a skill that taught him to stand firm. He personally took Jinshi to the southern lion-dance troupe and asked its old master to accept him. Every punch, stance and lion-dance step came from that master. Qing Yuanshan had taught him only tile strategy, yet Jinshi had always called Qing Yuanshan alone his teacher. Later the Qing family returned to Tiandu. Then came the disaster at Wangjiang, and the southern branch was left untended. Someone scraped the lettering from its signboard. To this day, the blank board still hung there.

“Ten years,” said Jinshi.

He stepped back and, at the head of the stairs before everyone in the hall, bent both knees and knelt.

“The day it happened, the old troupe master took us to dance at the opening outside Wangjiang Teahouse in Tiandu. I carried the lion head.” Head bowed, his voice was muffled. “The gongs and drums were too loud. I heard nothing of what happened upstairs. By the time I put away the lion and went up, everyone had gone. Teacher was gone too. He left that night and never returned.” Jinshi raised his head, eyes red and bright. “I owe him my life. For the rest of it, tell me east, and I won’t go west.”

Qinglan reached to help him up. With only one working hand, he could not lift him.

“Get up,” he said. “I’ll remember what you owe him on his behalf. But the people who are in his debt aren’t here.”

Jinshi looked at him for a long while, then rose. His knees struck two dull notes against the floorboards as he shifted, like drumbeats.

He went back, retrieved the brush and dotted the lion’s right eye.

Both copper eyes flashed. The great drum thundered, the lion head rose, and the whole street cheered.

Qinglan leaned against the stair rail and watched the lion tumble beneath the lanterns. After a while, he felt the coin in his sleeve growing terribly cold.

Not the coin. His right hand was growing cold again, slowly upward from his fingertips.

He looked up.

The moon had risen, huge and round, washing the whole street white. The Dipper crowded the roof corner, almost invisible. The ridge was empty, only a few tiles gleaming pale.

Just after the second watch began, the lion danced to the Rain Pavilion’s entrance.

The bamboo pole for plucking the greens was raised, the lettuce swaying beneath its red silk. With the lion head on his shoulders, Jinshi performed three rises on the benches to the crowd’s cheers. The drumbeat quickened; gongs and cymbals pressed in until the sound numbed the chest.

At the fiercest beat, someone in the crowd whistled.

A carrying pole swung through a gap between bodies and struck the lion square in the brow.

With a crack, the red-faced, gold-maned head split at the forehead. Its left copper eye flew out, bounced twice on the paving and rolled into the gutter.

The street’s cheers cut off.

The man wielding the pole had one eye. A scar ran from his brow to the corner of his mouth, sealing the other shut. Seven or eight men emerged behind him, each with a short club, the same brown lining showing beneath their lapels.

“The wooden box on the counter!” the one-eyed man bellowed. “And what’s in the panda’s basket! And the red tray! Get them, and it’s ten taels each!”

The street erupted. Spectators fled to either side. The sugar seller abandoned his stall and ran into an alley with his syrup pan. Jinshi flung aside the lion head, eyes blood-red beneath his headband, and roared. A dozen-odd young men of the troupe seized drumsticks and charged to meet them.

From the upstairs window, Qinglan saw clearly: as the seven or eight attackers reached the entrance, they split. One group stormed the hall. Three others slipped round the corner into the rear alley.

A stack of firewood stood in that alley, beside the rear window.

“Yuening! The box!”

Yuening was already at the accounts table, clutching the official wooden box to her chest with the ledger. Xiao Cheng'an rose with his stick and picked up the purple-clay teapot. Zhumo swung his basket onto his back and pulled its straps tight.

A crash at the stairs. Fighting had broken out below; tables fell and dishes shattered. Someone screamed, then came a dull blow. Pei Zhiwei’s voice rose from the foot of the stairs. “Coming up? Ask my wok first!” A clang like a copper gong followed, and someone tumbled scrambling down the steps.

The rear window crashed open.

The one-eyed man clambered in first, two others behind. Three men, three clubs. In the storytelling room: a young man who could not hold chopsticks, an old man with a stick, a girl clutching a box, and a panda.

The one-eyed man grinned and ran his tongue along his lower lip.

Qinglan watched that movement.

He had not sat at a mahjong table in ten years, but he remembered his father’s first lesson: before a player discards, his body moves. One touches his nose; another pinches a tile’s corner; another licks his lips. Watch not the tile, but the hand. Not the hand, but what happens just before it moves.

“The one on the left is going for Master Xiao!” Qinglan shouted. “Left foot forward, club in his right—he’ll strike your left!”

Xiao Cheng'an stepped right. The club grazed his cap and struck the table corner. The old man swung the teapot and smashed it squarely into the attacker’s skull. Tea and shards spattered the wall. The man swayed twice and fell facedown.

The one-eyed man cursed and charged Yuening. Qinglan stepped between them. Only his left hand worked, but he could see where that single eye was looking.

“He’s watching the box, not you. Duck!”

Yuening crouched over the box. The man grabbed empty air and stumbled forward, unable to stop. Zhumo struck him in the small of the back with his basket from one side, sending him staggering into a bench.

The third man raised his club and brought it down at Qinglan.

Qinglan saw the shoulder dip and leaned back. The club swept past his nose with a rush of air. There was nowhere else to go. The wall was at his back.

A roar from the stairs. Jinshi charged up with two men clinging to him, one on each side like sacks. He swung his arms and sent them flying into the rail. Then he seized the club-wielder by the back of his collar, lifted him off the floor and hurled him through the rear window.

The one-eyed man looked at Jinshi, then at his fallen companion, and stepped back. At the window, an odd expression crossed his face, like a gambler maddened by losses at last seeing the suit he had staked everything on.

“You’re finished,” he said.

Cold struck Qinglan’s heart.

The White Dragon.

Not cold. Ice. His chest contracted as though someone had pressed snow against it through his clothes. The chill in his right hand climbed his arm, passed the elbow and reached his shoulder.

Frost began forming on the windowsill.

Down in the main hall, a waiter shrieked, “The old table! It’s stirring again!”

Qinglan heard it through the floorboards. Beneath the shrine, the copper coin on the tile-box lid was jumping: click-click-click-click, rapid and brittle as chattering teeth. Something had startled awake the thing sleeping in that old table. It wanted to rise, but the coin pinned it fast.

It feared the same thing.

In the storytelling room, a green shoot emerged from a table leg.

Then a second. A third. Bench legs, beams, window frames, the threshold: everywhere there was wood, shoots sprang out. Bamboo strips in Zhumo’s basket snapped loose one after another, lengthening, curling and branching in the air. The shoots grew as soon as air touched them, becoming vines in a blink, green and gleaming as rain-wet bamboo. Beads of light hung on their leaf tips. Not lamplight. Breath, sent from the very seams of a bearer’s bones.

“Don’t move!” Zhumo shouted. “It follows breath! The harder you pant, the more it binds you!”

Too late. The one-eyed man was caught first. A vine dropped from the window frame and coiled around his neck. His mouth opened without a sound as it dragged him bodily outside. At the stairs, Jinshi punched through a vine. Three more sprouted from the break, coiled round his waist and hauled him downwards. His feet gouged splinters from the steps as he braced them.

In the street, the gongs and drums were still sounding. The troupe’s young men saw green burst from the upper floor and turned pale. Someone cried, voice breaking, “Beat it! Beat the drum! Even ghosts fear an awakened lion!”

They beat the drum until it thundered across the sky.

Qinglan saw the vines outside tremble at the sound. Tremble, but not disperse. Several followed the drumbeat down, climbed its frame and bound the great drum and its player together.

Not stray breath. Drums could scatter what floated loose in the air. They could not scatter a living man’s hand.

A vine emerged between the floorboards and reached towards his right sleeve.

Qinglan held his breath. Its tip touched his cuff, at the place where the copper coin lay, and recoiled sharply as though scorched. It went round him and seized a bench instead.

He looked at Zhumo. Vines bound everything: people, benches, lampstands. Only the space around the panda remained clear, like water flowing round a stone. Zhumo’s paw pressed the little pouch at his waist.

The vines did not bind anyone carrying a coin.

“Yuening!”

Yuening was caught. A vine sprang from the accounts-table leg, circled her waist and the arm clutching the box. She struggled, pressed her shoulder against her crossed collar and clung to the box as she was dragged towards the rear window. Another vine coiled around the official wooden box itself, layer upon layer, until only its brass clasp remained visible.

Qinglan lunged over, drew the coin from his right sleeve with his left hand and thrust it into her fist. “Hold it!”

Yuening clenched her hand. The vine around her waist hissed and loosened as though burned, then withdrew.

But the one around the box did not loosen. It had come for the box, not her. It tugged. Yuening pitched forward, knees striking the floor, both hands still locked around the box.

“Let go!” Qinglan shouted. “It wants the box, not you!”

“The evidence is inside!”

“Lost evidence can be found! If we lose you—”

Before he finished, something tightened round his ankle.

The moment the coin left him, the vines came. One emerged between the boards and circled his ankle, a second his calf, a third his waist. Three coils, tightening towards his chest until he could not breathe. A vine tip probed inside his robe, reached the White Dragon at his heart and paused. Its bamboo joints creaked, as though uncertain.

The vine prised Yuening’s fingers loose one by one. The box flew from her arms and was carried through the rear window.

A man hung upside down beneath the eaves outside.

A dark-brown woven bamboo hat, blackness beneath it, no face visible. The hem of his black robe hung in the air, stirring in the night wind. His legs hooked the eaves, suspending him like a bat. Black leather gloves covered both hands, each with a hand embroidered in gold thread on its back; the right glove was half an inch wider than the left. In that right hand he held a tile.

Five of Bamboo. Five stalks, each greener than the last. Green light flowed between the gloved fingers and into the vines filling the room.

The official evidence box settled securely in the crook of his left arm.

“Master Zhu,” said the man in the woven hat.

The vine around Xiao Cheng'an’s neck tightened another turn.

The old man’s stick fell. He seized the vine with both hands, face purple, mouth open without a sound.

Zhumo moved.

He walked through the vines. They parted before him like a tide meeting a reef. At the rear window, he took the strip of paper from his sleeve and held it up.

“Release him.”

The man held the box under his left arm and extended that hand beneath it, taking the paper lightly between two fingers, as though claiming another player’s discard. His right hand, holding the Five of Bamboo, did not move.

The vine at Xiao Cheng'an’s neck loosened. The old man braced himself against the table corner, dragged in a breath and coughed violently.

“A ten-year debt.” The man tucked the strip into his sleeve. “Half cleared this time.”

He tilted his head, the hat pointing towards the basket on Zhumo’s back.

“Keep carrying the record. It is more useful in your basket than in my hands.”

His hooked legs loosened and tightened; he swung upwards, about to return to the roof.

The vines around Qinglan contracted with him. They were withdrawing outside, but still held people. To pull away, they had to drag those people to the window first, like a net drawing shut and hauling everything inside it along.

Qinglan slid towards the window, a vine pressed against his heart.

The vine was green. Its bamboo joints ran one after another.

Bamboo knew bamboo.

Suddenly he understood the frost on the White Dragon, and why the vine tip had hesitated at his chest. The breath flowing through it was Bamboo breath, of the same affinity as his.

His White Dragon held one last blue-green thread. Finer than spider silk, restored in the third watch last night. He had not dared touch it all day. Tonight was the fifteenth; the moon was that bright. Once burned, it would not return.

Qinglan pressed his left hand over his heart, against the White Dragon, and sent that thread out.

Not into the White Dragon. Into the vine binding him.

It felt as though someone had pulled a bone out of him from the inside.

The vine’s breath surged towards him, cool and astringent, smelling of old dust. He saw things: a long room without daylight, rows of wooden shelves, rows of boxes upon them, each bearing a sealing strip marked with an A. One seal had been cut neatly down the middle. Its box was empty.

The Five of Bamboo had come from there.

He clenched his teeth and pulled back along that current of breath.

The joints of the three vines around him creaked together. They released him, reversed direction and shot along the window frame, out to the eaves, where they wound several times around the gloved right hand.

“Oh?”

The man in the woven hat stopped on the eaves.

The sound was very soft, like a lifelong player drawing an unexpected tile.

Qinglan saw two of the five bamboo stalks on the tile begin to yellow from the roots, joint by joint, like bamboo canes in autumn.

Warmth filled his nose, and blood ran down. Frost formed on his right fingertips, climbed his wrist and elbow, and whitened his entire arm like an icicle. Even his sleeve froze stiff.

The White Dragon at his heart was empty.

The man could not pull his vine-bound right hand free. The box remained under his left arm. To use that hand, he had to loosen his hold.

He did. His left fingers rubbed together; a bone tile appeared between them and slashed across his right wrist. Its edge was sharp as a blade. The vines snapped, giving off white smoke and the scorched smell of burning bamboo.

At the instant he loosened his arm, a shadow dropped from the ridge.

It landed so lightly that no roof tile sounded. One foot struck his left elbow. The official box flew from his arm, turned twice in the moonlight and was caught in midair by a furry hand.

Moonlight shone on the ridge.

A catfolk crouched there: orange fur, dark tiger stripes across brow and cheeks, fitted black clothes edged in gold at collar and cuffs. One hand held the box; the other twirled a short blade narrow as a willow leaf. Behind him, a long, ring-striped tail swayed slowly in the moonlight.

He glanced down through the window, golden eyes bright as two lamps, and grinned, showing a pointed canine.

“Good evening,” he said. “Lu Zhaoye. Just passing. Enjoying the show.”

His blade tip flicked. The sealing strip split cleanly at the join, the branch’s small seal cut exactly in two. His thumb opened the clasp; he lifted the lid and, without even looking at the snow-white new Red Dragon, plucked the yellowed old one between two fingers and tucked it into his robe.

He shut the lid and tossed the box. It arced through the rear window and landed in Yuening’s arms.

As the box passed the eaves, it came within a foot of the man’s left hand. That hand was empty. He did not reach for it.

“Keep the new one,” said Lu Zhaoye. “I’ll borrow the old one for a few days.”

The man in the woven hat turned on the eaves. The darkness beneath his brim faced the catfolk on the ridge.

Lu Zhaoye’s tail stood upright.

“You want it? Then you certainly won’t get it from me.” He smiled at the man, then looked down at Qinglan through the window. “Tomorrow at midnight. The old stone archway south of the city. Bring your father’s White Dragon.”

Before the words had ended, he was gone from the ridge.

Every vine in the room withdrew through the window at once.

Qinglan struggled to the sill. Beneath the moon, green vines raced like snakes across the rooftops after the orange shadow, past the cloth shop, the sugar stall and two alley mouths. Near the third, they had almost caught up when they suddenly slowed. Their tips lifted from the tiles and swayed, as though catching a scent they could not quite identify.

The shadow flashed over a row of ridges at the foot of the city wall and vanished.

On the long table, the floating pointer of the copper water clock rested precisely at a marked line. Yuening held the box, lips white, staring at the mark. Each word came separately. “Second watch… third quarter.”

The man in the woven hat remained at the eaves.

He did not pursue. Hanging there, he watched silently through the window, looking at Yuening’s closed fist.

A slender vine descended from the frame to her hand and stopped. Its tip tapped her fist lightly, like a man tapping the table.

Yuening’s fingers tightened until the knuckles whitened.

“Give it to him,” said Qinglan.

“But it’s yours—”

“It’s his boat fare.” Qinglan leaned against the sill, blood still running. “If he takes it back, the account is settled. Keep it, and next time he comes for you.”

Yuening looked at him for a long while. Then she slowly opened her fist.

The vine tip curled round the half-finger coin and withdrew.

“The boat fare,” said the voice above, “has been spent.”

Clink.

A copper coin flew through the window, struck the floor, bounced once and rolled twice, stopping precisely at Qinglan’s feet.

Just as in the woodshed.

When he looked up, the eaves were empty. Frost on the sill began melting into moisture.

The vines throughout the room yellowed from their leaf tips, so swiftly that in a blink they were withered brown-yellow strands, hanging limp and crumbling at a touch. The street’s gongs and drums had not stopped. The troupe’s young men still beat with all their strength. Each drumbeat struck the dead vines, from which wisps of blue-grey smoke rose, drifting upward through the clangour until they reached eaves-height and dispersed like incense ash falling from a temple burner.

Below, the coin beneath the shrine stopped jumping too.

Qinglan sat beneath the window, his back against the wall.

His right hand no longer belonged to him. His whole arm was frozen white, lying stiff across his knees like an icicle someone had abandoned there.

Two hands took hold of it.

Yuening’s. Kneeling beside him, she cradled his frozen hand, rubbed it back and forth and bent to breathe between his fingers. Her hands were warm, her breath warm too, so warm that pain pulsed through his fingertips until it numbed him.

She rubbed for a long time, then enclosed his hand between her palms. The pain passed; his fingertips went numb again, but warmth seeped slowly in.

As though suddenly waking, she loosened her hands and laid his back on his knee.

Then she reached deep into her sleeve pocket for the handkerchief she had folded away that morning. Turning it to a clean corner, she pressed it beneath his nose.

“Tilt your head back,” she said.

He did. Her hand stayed there.

The vine had left a red mark at her cuff, like a fresh rope burn.

Qinglan turned his eyes away, towards the moon outside.

“Your shoulder,” he said.

Yuening glanced down, and her ears reddened at once. Keeping one hand on the handkerchief, she pulled her collar firmly up with the other. Then she said nothing.

The storytelling room was wrecked. Overturned tables, broken benches, shattered crockery and dead vines everywhere. Xiao Cheng'an sat braced against a table corner, a purple ring around his neck, still gripping half the teapot’s handle. Jinshi came up the stairs with a cut on his forehead, cradling the split lion head. Its left eye was an empty hole.

Zhumo still stood at the rear window. Withered bamboo strips lay at his feet. Half his basket was gone, but the original game record remained at the bottom.

“The vines didn’t touch you,” Qinglan said, “because you carried it.”

“Yes,” said Zhumo. “Carry his coin, and you’re his man. His vines don’t bind his own.”

“Then why did he have to come in person?” Yuening asked. “Those were his vines. He could have sat on the ridge and torn the whole Pavilion apart.”

“Borrowed things are hard to use,” said Zhumo. “The Five of Bamboo does not belong to his affinity. He was forcing it to send out vines through that glove. Force it too hard, and it suffers.” He looked at Qinglan. “You saw it too. Two stalks yellowed.”

“Bamboo knows bamboo,” said Qinglan. “I’m Bamboo, and so is the tile. That’s why I can borrow from it. He isn’t.”

The panda did not reply. He untied the small pouch at his waist, tipped out the coin with its single filed mark and laid it beside Zheng Shouzhuo’s.

Exactly alike.

He looked at it a while, then returned it to the pouch.

“Last night I told only half,” said Zhumo. “Ten years ago, at the inn, he tore away the sealed endorsement and set this coin on my table. Hush money, he called it. I said a chronicler took no money. He smiled. Then let’s play a hand, he said. The stake: whether he would search my basket. I wagered he would.”

“You lost.”

“I lost. He didn’t search it. He knew perfectly well the cut strip was inside, and still refused to look. Just pushed the coin back towards me. ‘Then it isn’t hush money. Master Zhu, we’ll leave this hand on account.’” The panda lowered his eyes. “Ten years without collecting. Then tonight, of all nights. The Captain arrives tomorrow morning.”

“You could have given him a false strip,” said Yuening. “Who can imitate your hand better than you?”

“Precisely because I’m a chronicler.” Zhumo looked up. “Cutting away a passage is silence. Writing a false one is lying. A chronicler may keep silent. His hand does not write lies.”

Yuening asked no more. She picked up the ledger, brushed off the dead leaves, opened a fresh page and raised her brush.

Her hand shook, the brush shaft trembling, yet every character on the page was square and straight:

Second watch: eight Hengchang hired men entered the Pavilion, led by a one-eyed man. Vines from the Five of Bamboo entered through the rear window and seized the official evidence box. Zhumo states that he surrendered the strip cut from the opening of the original game record to the man in the woven hat.

Third quarter of the second watch, rear window: one catfolk, calling himself Lu Zhaoye, cut the seal with a blade and removed one old Red Dragon. One new Red Dragon remains in the box.

Before leaving, the catfolk told Qinglan: tomorrow at midnight, the old stone archway south of the city; bring your father’s White Dragon. Heard by the scorekeeper and Master Xiao.

The half-finger coin Qinglan picked up in the woodshed was taken back by a vine. Another copper coin was dropped at Qinglan’s feet.

She finished the last word, set down the brush and stared at the lines for a long time before releasing a long breath.

With his left hand, Qinglan picked up the coin from the floor and brought it to the lamp.

Two marks had been filed into the edge, one deep, one shallow. On the reverse were tiny words, cut lightly, as though slowly scratched with a needle:

Midnight. Old archway. Collect one hand.

He stared at the words as chills passed over his back.

Lu Zhaoye had spoken from the ridge before this coin flew inside. But the words had not been cut tonight. Black grime had already lodged in the grooves.

The time and place had been engraved long ago. The catfolk thought he had chosen them himself.

Downstairs, the lion troupe lay sprawled across the hall, snoring. Jinshi sat at the foot of the stairs with the broken lion head in his arms, his back against the rail, eyes open, guarding the upper floor.

Yuening had fallen asleep over the accounts table, her ledger beneath her arm. The official box stood beside her hand. Its seal was neatly severed; the two vermilion fingerprints remained.

Qinglan lay sleepless on a bench in the storytelling room.

His left hand rested over his heart, pressing the White Dragon through the substitute player’s robe. Its face was neither cold nor warm. Just bone. He took it out and turned it over in the moonlight by the window.

The back was bare. Not a trace of blue-green remained.

Outside, the moon was huge and full, painfully bright. The Dipper huddled by the roof corner, seven stars faint as seven grains of undissolved salt.

Tonight, it would not come back.

(End of Chapter Three)
