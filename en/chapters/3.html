Chapter 3Night of the Lion Dance

Night in Yunchuan began with gongs and drums.

On the fifteenth of every month, the lion-dance troupe from the east of the city came to the Rain Pavilion for the lion-awakening ceremony, dotting the eyes of newly made lion heads. The custom was decades old: only an awakened lion could open a sworn match of honour and keep watch over it, driving away evil and corruption so that those who took the table would have honest hearts and honest hands.

Tonight was the fifteenth.

The sun had barely set when red lanterns filled the space outside the Rain Pavilion, one after another, bathing half of Crossroads Street in red. Stalls selling sugar figures, wontons and sweet osmanthus cakes crowded the roadside. Children perched on adults’ shoulders, craning for a view.

Nine plum-blossom posts stood in the open space before the building. The tallest reached waist height; the shortest rose past the knee.

“Boom—boom—clang!”

The gongs and drums began.

Two lions leapt from the crowd. One red, one gold. The red lion was nimble, rolling and bounding along the ground until the children roared with laughter. The golden lion was fierce. Its head was a full half a person’s height; two huge brass-bell eyes rattled as they rolled, and its gaping red mouth opened and shut, sending children in the front row scrambling behind their parents.

The golden lion circled three times on the ground, then sprang onto the lowest post.

The second. The third… The posts grew higher, but the lion’s leaps only grew steadier. On the last post, it stopped short, rose on its hind legs and lifted its forepaws clear. Its head reared high, jaws opening towards the stars.

“Bravo!”

Cheers filled the street.

The golden lion leapt down. Its head lifted to reveal a man built like an iron tower. His bronze arms were bare, a yellow silk sash wrapped his waist, and sweat drenched his head. He grinned, showing a mouthful of white teeth.

“Neighbours! It’s the fifteenth, and our lion-dance troupe is here to perform the lion-awakening ceremony for the Rain Pavilion! Once these eyes are dotted, anyone who sits down at a sworn match of honour here will play with an honest heart and honest hands. Not one crooked tile will pass!”

Someone called, “Master Jin! Why didn’t you come and dot them yesterday? There was a crooked tile in the Rain Pavilion then!”

The street burst into laughter. Jinshi scratched the back of his head and grinned a little sheepishly.

◆

In the second-floor storytelling room, Qinglan stood at the window watching the lions below.

The notary’s people would not arrive until the day after tomorrow. The wooden box sealing the two Red Dragons still sat on the table near at hand in the storytelling hall. Yuening kept watch beside it, Xiao Cheng’an sat by the door, and neither touched it alone.

“What were you doing at the dye works this afternoon?” she asked, moving beads on her abacus.

“Seeing how Miss Luo’s hand was.”

“What did she say?”

“She said…” Qinglan paused. “‘What are you apologising for? You weren’t the one who pricked me.’”

Yuening glanced at him and asked no more.

The drums started again below. She tipped her chin towards the towering man. “That’s Jinshi, the lion-dance master. Strong as an ox, loud as a drum, and everyone likes him. Weddings, funerals, whatever the occasion, the neighbours always ask him to bring his lions. As for mahjong…”

She considered, then pronounced judgment. “Worst player in the city.”

“How bad?”

“If he can pung, he pungs. If he can kong, he kongs. Never calculates a thing. Last year he played three sworn matches of honour for the neighbours and lost two. The last one was over water rights to the old well in the east of the city. He broke up a pair of Three of Characters tiles just so he could pung on someone’s Five of Characters.”

At the table, Zhumo was carefully drawing the two Red Dragons from the previous day in a little notebook. He spoke without looking up. “I recorded that hand too. Breaking a pair to call pung was a bad move. But after he did it, the player opposite panicked, thinking he was building a big hand, and made two bad discards in a row. In the end, Jinshi won with All Pungs. The well was saved.”

“That was luck.” Yuening pursed her lips.

“At the mahjong table,” Zhumo said, setting down his brush, “sometimes the sheer conviction that nothing can stop you is a kind of luck.”

Qinglan said nothing. His eyes remained on the man below, his brow slightly furrowed, as though he were struggling to remember something.

◆

Below, the lion-awakening ceremony began.

Jinshi took a cinnabar-dipped brush from an apprentice and approached the new golden lion head with great solemnity. First the eyes, so the lion could see. Then the nose, so it could smell. Last the forehead, to give it spirit. Only after the eyes were dotted would this lion come to life.

He dotted the left eye, then the right. Straightening to wipe his brow, he happened to glance up at the second floor.

His eyes found Qinglan at the window.

And stopped.

Cinnabar dripped from his brush and struck the flagstones with a soft pat.

The drums fell silent. The apprentices looked at one another. The neighbours quietened too, unsure what had happened to their lion-dance master.

Then the towering man thrust the brush into an apprentice’s hand, spun round and charged inside. His feet thundered up the staircase like an ox climbing to the second floor.

The storytelling-room door burst open.

Jinshi stood in the doorway, panting, looking Qinglan up and down, from head to foot and back again.

“Xiaolan!”

He surged forward, thrust his great hands under Qinglan’s arms and hoisted him overhead like a chick. Then he began to spin.

“Thud!”

Qinglan’s head struck a roof beam squarely.

Zhumo hugged his bamboo basket and shrank into a corner. Jinshi’s elbow swept the teapot off the table; Yuening caught it in one hand, clamped the other over the wooden box and shrieked:

“Master Jin! Put him down! He’s twenty-four!”

Jinshi came to his senses and set Qinglan back on the floor. He even straightened the collar he had pulled askew, chuckling all the while. “So tall now, and still so thin! When you were little, I could lift you onto my shoulders with one hand and spin you round your courtyard. You laughed so hard you drooled!”

Qinglan held the top of his head and stared at him.

A blurred figure rose from the depths of memory: a boy, five or six years older than he was, always grinning, frighteningly strong. He would carry Qinglan on his shoulders and turn in circles in his father’s courtyard until Qinglan giggled helplessly. The boy spent two years listening to his father explain mahjong theory but never learned to calculate tile possibilities. His father had sighed and told him, “Ashi, your heart burns too hot. You cannot sit still at the mahjong table. Go and learn the lion dance. They can use that fire there.”

The boy had left in tears. Before he went, he knelt in the courtyard and bowed to Qinglan’s father three times, his forehead striking the ground.

“…My senior fellow disciple?”

“Hey!” Jinshi slapped his back hard enough to make him cough. “Yesterday’s hand is all over town! Ready in five turns, passed up a dangerous discard, self-draw in nine—and you made back those three points in one hand! I said it, didn’t I? Master’s son couldn’t possibly—”

He broke off abruptly.

“The Master…” Jinshi’s voice grew quiet. “Has he been well, all these years?”

Qinglan was silent for a moment.

“He died nine years ago.”

Jinshi asked blankly, “And after you left Tiandu?”

“We lived in a little town for less than a year. Father rented a shop and taught children to read. Later, when I was on my own, I went farther away.” Qinglan paused. “After Father left, someone found me and taught me for four years. I studied records and positions, practised footwork and gathering my breath, but never sat at a table again.” He lowered his eyes to his hands. “I knew I was steadier than before, and I knew what I still couldn’t do. But if I’d stepped back again yesterday, perhaps I’d never have sat down at all.”

◆

The storytelling room fell still.

Little by little, the smile froze on Jinshi’s face. Little by little, it broke apart.

He opened his mouth, but no sound emerged. Then the towering man fell to his knees with a thump.

“Xiaolan.” His voice no longer boomed. He sounded like a child who had done something wrong. “The day it happened, I was downstairs at Wangjiang Teahouse, performing the opening lion dance. The drums were deafening. I danced all day. All that was happening upstairs—the Master being framed, his tiles smashed—and I was right below him… I heard nothing. I saw nothing…”

He lifted a fist and struck the floorboards with a heavy, muffled blow.

“Later, I went to Tiandu to find you. The house had been sold. Nobody knew where you’d gone. For ten years, I’ve felt I owed the Master a life.”

Yuening turned her face away. Zhumo lowered his head, his furry hands tightening on the straps of his basket.

Qinglan bent down. It took all his strength to haul his mountain of a senior fellow disciple to his feet.

“Brother.” Looking Jinshi in the eye, he spoke each word clearly. “You owe him nothing.”

“The people who owe him are not here.”

◆

Just then, a scream rose from below.

Tables and chairs overturned. Crockery shattered. Women and children screamed, and a rough voice roared:

“Out of the way! Hand over the Red Dragon!”

Yuening rushed to the window, her face changing. “They’re after the Red Dragons!”

More than a dozen black-clad thugs carrying wooden clubs had scattered the spectators. The sugar seller’s stall lay overturned amid broken sweets. At their head was a one-eyed brute with a scar running from brow to mouth. He swung his club and brought it down on the golden lion head whose eyes had just been dotted.

With a crack, one brass-bell eye broke free, struck the ground and rolled away, ringing and clattering.

“The Red Dragon is sealed evidence under mahjong arbitration!” Xiao Cheng’an stood at the stairway, his white beard trembling with rage. “Lay a hand on it, and you make yourselves enemies of the entire Way of Mahjong!”

“The Way of Mahjong?” The one-eyed man threw back his head and laughed. “Old man, after tonight, Yunchuan won’t have a Way of Mahjong! Search the place!”

◆

Jinshi’s eyes reddened.

He himself had dotted that brass-bell eye now rolling across the ground.

Without a word, he reached the window in three strides and, to everyone’s cries of alarm, leapt straight out of the second floor.

“Brother!”

Jinshi dropped like a stone, turned in mid-air and landed firmly beside the damaged lion head. Even the flagstones seemed to shudder. With one hand he lifted the heavy head, weighing dozens of pounds, and pulled it over his own.

“Lion-dance troupe! Raise the lions!”

The drums sounded again. These were not the cheerful rhythms of the lion-awakening ceremony, but the troupe’s battle drums, held in reserve for desperate moments: urgent, dense, relentless, each beat hard on the last, like a downpour striking roof tiles.

The apprentices huddled by the wall grew red-eyed one by one. One shouldered the red lion, another seized a long staff, another grabbed gongs and cymbals. With fierce cries, they charged.

This was no dance. A real beast was defending the entrance to its den.

The golden lion rolled through the crowd. A toss of its head sent two thugs flying; a sweep of its tail knocked down three more. A club swung from behind. The lion performed Turning to Gaze at the Moon, and the blow missed, landing on another thug’s shoulder. The red lion followed at its side, snapping at ankles. A young apprentice clapped his brass cymbals about a thug’s head with a clang. The man crouched with both hands over his ears and could not get up for a long time.

But there were too many of them. Three clubs closed in from three directions. The golden lion lunged left and right, slowly forced towards a corner.

At the second-floor window, Qinglan had never taken his eyes off the one-eyed man.

The man did not charge with the others. He circled outside them. His toes pointed left, but his shoulder dipped right. Before every swing of his club, he licked his lips.

Just as a player’s finger always lingered on a tile before discarding it.

“Brother!” Qinglan gripped the window lattice and shouted. “The left is a feint! He’s coming from the right!”

Without a moment’s hesitation, the golden lion swung its head right.

The one-eyed man’s club came down from that very side and landed in the lion’s open jaws. The lion “bit” down, then hurled man and club more than ten feet away.

“Good!” Jinshi roared from inside the head. “Keep calling, Xiaolan!”

“The one behind you! He’s going for your legs!”

The lion sprang straight up. The sweeping club passed beneath its feet, and the thug, unable to stop his own momentum, fell flat on his face.

At the stairs, a thug took advantage of the confusion to creep up. Without a word, Xiao Cheng’an snatched the purple-clay teapot from his storytelling stage and struck him across the forehead. The pot shattered. The man went down.

The old storyteller brushed tea leaves from his sleeve. “I’ve told stories all my life. Today, this old man gets to do something.”

The one-eyed man scrambled up, seized the thickest wooden club and swung it down at the golden lion head.

Jinshi neither dodged nor ducked. The lion raised its head and took the blow squarely on its forehead.

With a crack, the club broke in two. A long split opened in the gold paint across the lion’s brow, exposing its bamboo frame.

From inside came Jinshi’s deep, muffled laughter.

“Is that all you’ve got? Come on! Let’s clash again!”

At the upstairs window, Zhumo murmured, “If he can pung, he pungs. If he can kong, he kongs. Exactly how he plays.”

The beads of Yuening’s abacus were flying beneath her fingers.

“What are you counting?” Qinglan asked.

“The attackers. Seventeen came in. Master Jin knocked down seven, Red Lion three, Master Xiao one with a teapot. Six still standing.” She paused as a tile clattered at the rear window. Turning, she saw two dark shapes rising over the sill.

“Back window!” Qinglan had seen them too.

Yuening shot to her feet. “Two more from behind the building!”

◆

Under cover of darkness, two men in black had climbed from the alley to the storytelling-room windowsill. They reached for the wooden box on the table.

Qinglan pulled Yuening behind the table and stepped half a pace sideways, shielding her with his shoulder and back while leaving himself room to retreat. His left palm guarded the table edge. With his right hand he caught up the teapot, aiming its spout at the wrist reaching for the box, about to force that hand back—

A yellow-and-black shadow moved faster.

It dropped upside down from the eaves, silent as a cat. Two sharp snaps sounded, like fingers clicking twice. Clutching their wrists and screaming, the two men rolled off the sill into the firewood stacked in the alley. They did not get up for a long time.

The shadow flipped lightly over, settled into a crouch on the windowsill and tilted its head at the people inside.

A young man with the head of an orange-striped cat. Short, tousled fur. Close-fitting black clothes edged in gold. A short blade at his waist. In the lamplight, the pupils of his golden eyes had narrowed to slits. Behind him, a furry orange tail marked with rings swung idly to and fro.

One of the catfolk. A cat in human form.

“Good evening.” He grinned, exposing two sharp little canines. “Lu Zhaoye. Just passing. Enjoying the show.”

Somehow, the wooden box was already in his hand.

“Put that down!” Yuening leaned out from behind Qinglan. “That is sealed evidence under mahjong arbitration!”

“I know.”

Lu Zhaoye drew his short blade. A flick of the tip severed the sealing strip. He opened the box, glanced inside and lifted the yellowed old Red Dragon between two fingers. After turning it under the lamp, he tucked it into his clothes.

Then he tossed the box into Yuening’s arms.

“You keep the new one. That’s what Hengchang’s men want. Take it to the Arbitration Office. It’ll give their proprietor plenty to choke on.” He patted his chest. “The old one, I’m borrowing.”

“That strip had my fingerprint and Master Xiao’s on it!” Yuening clutched the box, her face red with fury. “You’ve broken the seal! How is this evidence supposed to stand now?”

“Whether it stands,” Lu Zhaoye said with a smile, “depends on whether you, our scorekeeper, keep a record.”

Yuening gripped the box and called Xiao Cheng’an over. The old storyteller did not conceal what Lu Zhaoye had done; first, he checked the newer Red Dragon, now alone in the box.

She wrote: “At a quarter to ten at night, one catfolk male, calling himself Lu Zhaoye, broke the seal without permission and removed one old Red Dragon. One newer Red Dragon remains in the box. Verified in person by the scorekeeper and Master Xiao.”

“This entry goes to the notary too.” She looked up. “Recording it doesn’t mean you had permission to take it.”

Lu Zhaoye burst out laughing.

“What do you want it for?” Qinglan stared at him.

The smile slowly faded from Lu Zhaoye’s face. His golden cat’s eyes flashed in the lamplight, carrying an indefinable chill.

“Someone left this tile deliberately. He’s waiting for you to follow it, step by step, to wherever he wants you to go,” he said. “I’ve been hunting the man in the woven hat for three years. Whatever he wants, I mean to deny him.”

“Who are you?”

“Someone with a grudge against Ghost Hand,” Lu Zhaoye said. “Like you.”

He gathered himself to leap through the window, then looked back.

“Tomorrow night. The Zi watch. The old stone archway south of the city.”

“Bring your father’s White Dragon.”

Before the words had faded, he was gone into the night. A few tiles rustled softly on the roof.

◆

Below, the lion-dance troupe had beaten the thugs into disarray. Seeing that the attempt at the rear had failed too, they scattered, swearing. The one-eyed man clutched his head, swollen where the lion head had rammed it, and vanished into the depths of an alley.

Crossroads Street was a wreck. Lanterns littered the ground. The old sugar seller sat among the debris, sorrowfully gathering broken sweets.

Jinshi removed the lion head. A gash marked his temple, and blood mingled with sweat, dripping from his chin. But when he looked up and saw Qinglan at the second-floor window, he grinned like a child.

“Xiaolan! Didn’t let them upstairs!”

Yuening hugged the wooden box and muttered, “One got up. A cat.”

Qinglan could not quite stop the corner of his mouth from lifting.

Below, his bloodied senior fellow disciple carefully cradled the golden lion head, its forehead split and one eye missing, as though it were a wounded old friend.

Qinglan’s eyes grew hot.

◆

Yuening touched his arm lightly. “How does that cat know you keep a White Dragon on you?”

Qinglan reached inside his robe and took out a red cloth wrapping. The cloth was washed pale, its corners worn fuzzy. He unfolded it, layer by layer.

Inside lay a White Dragon. A clean, bone-white face. Nothing on it.

“I never told him,” Qinglan said softly.

Until last night, even Qinglan had not known the tile held anything else.

Last night, his eyes had followed the hand on its face so closely that he had never thought to turn it over.

Now Lu Zhaoye’s words reminded him of the matching incisions on the backs of the two Red Dragons.

Zhumo put down his brush and came over. Below, Jinshi lifted his face, the lion head in his arms.

Qinglan turned the White Dragon over and brought it near the lamp.

At the lower right of its back was an exceedingly faint incision, fine as a hair. Under the slanting lamplight, it cast a pale shadow.

Five fingers, spread wide.

Xiao Cheng’an came closer. In front of him, Yuening lifted the snow-white new Red Dragon from the box, whose seal was already broken, turned it over and set it beside the White Dragon.

The brush slipped from Zhumo’s fingers and struck the floor.

Two carved marks. The same five spread fingers.

Qinglan’s fingers slowly tightened around the White Dragon. He remembered his father’s eyes as he pressed the tile into his hand. What he had seen there was neither despair nor humiliation, but something he had not understood then.

“So,” Qinglan said softly, “Father had already seen it.”

Outside, the full moon had risen to the heart of the sky, round and bright. It shone on the wreckage in the street, and on the golden lion below with one eye missing.

◆

It was late, and people were gradually leaving the street. Jinshi was still downstairs, gathering scattered sweets for the frightened children and sending an apprentice to collect the broken post ropes.

Upstairs, the wooden box stood empty, the new Red Dragon still beside Yuening’s hand. Qinglan laid the White Dragon on the red cloth. Across from them, Zhumo listened as the two described the scene at the box’s rim from the night before, from beginning to end.

“I didn’t see that scene,” Zhumo said. “But from what you tell me, reaching to look at a tile box is not the same as putting something into it. If you haven’t seen what follows, you cannot write a line to stand for it.”

Qinglan placed the White Dragon by the window. Starlight fell on it, and a hint of blue-green like spring water showed through its edge.

He tried moving the tile into the shadow beside the lamp, away from the starlight coming through the gap in the window. The hint of green faded. Back by the window, a fine line appeared again. He showed Zhumo the positions he had tried.

Xiao Cheng’an checked the empty box, then looked at the sole remaining new Red Dragon on the table. The old one Lu Zhaoye had taken had not returned.

“Last night, it touched the old one,” Yuening said.

She moved the new Red Dragon over.

The White Dragon still held a trace of blue-green. But his father’s hand did not appear. There was no tile box, no faint click.

They waited a long time. Zhumo said nothing either.

“Both Red Dragons. Both with Ghost Hand marks.” Yuening looked at the two objects. “But this one wasn’t there ten years ago.”

Qinglan’s fingers stopped at the edge of the red cloth.

“Does it recognise things from the past?”

“We can’t be sure yet,” Zhumo said. “The Heavenly Tiles in the legends each have their uses. Perhaps yours has retained something. We need to find the next thing that can make it speak before we know whether our guess is right.”

Jinshi finished gathering the post ropes downstairs and came up with the lion head. Hearing this, he set it by the door and untied a small bundle of old cloth from his belt.

“I’ve come across something like that.”

Inside was a dull grey stone tile. He did not bring it towards the White Dragon, but laid it in his own palm and gathered his breath. A line of light rose along its edge, then markings emerged on its face. When Jinshi withdrew that force, the markings slowly receded, leaving the same old grey stone tile in his palm.

“Dug it out from under old bricks when we were repairing the lion yard years ago. At first I used it as a paperweight for the accounts. Later, when I gathered my breath, it answered. That was when I knew what I’d found.” Jinshi wrapped it again. “With a burst of strength through it, I can work out how to send the next step as soon as my front foot lands on a post. Less chance of the lion head pulling me off line. But no matter how steady your mind is, your feet need training. Overdo it and your legs still go weak. I’ve never staked spirit energy with it.”

Qinglan looked at him, then back at the White Dragon by the window.

“Those markings aren’t usually there?” he asked.

Jinshi turned the tile over to show its original stone grain. “Only when I use it. When I train up a stage, it changes with me. Draw back the energy, and it looks the same as ever.”

Qinglan’s hand paused beside the spread red cloth. He did not follow Jinshi in drawing energy into the tile.

“Has someone identified yours too?”

“Had an old tile-bearer look at it, and tested it at a spirit-binding ground.” Jinshi patted the wrapped stone tile. “The first man said I was passing off a broken stone. The second wouldn’t let me in. Later someone agreed to look, and I spent half a month fixing his collapsed training shed. He taught me how to try. It took another winter’s practice before I could keep even that little light.”

He looked down and laughed softly.

“Finding it took one reach of the hand. Using it took much more. All I’ve managed with it over the years is keeping steady on the posts. Never seen it reveal anything from the past.”

“Then why not draw more from it?” Qinglan asked.

Jinshi reached out and rested a hand on the lion head by the door, rubbing its cracked eye socket with his thumb.

“Once I got impatient, my legs gave out coming off a post. Nearly brought an apprentice down with me. It may answer, but I still have to bear it.”

Zhumo turned to a copied game compact in his notebook. A line in the margin recorded receipts and payments, with different figures before and after; beside it were the names of three suit aspects.

Qinglan’s eyes stopped on “Flow and Growth.”

“The master who taught me later called it that.”

“Bamboo: Flow and Growth.” Zhumo flattened the paper’s edge. “Dots: Condensation and Defence. Characters: Evolution and Change. I’ve copied accounts of someone holding up half a falling wall, and someone changing course before a move was finished. Only a few words on paper. In practice, each person’s mastery differs.”

Jinshi glanced at the lion head by the door. “To understand it, you need to see whether they can sustain it.”

Qinglan moved the White Dragon a little closer to the window. The line of green remained. Only tonight had he learned how often his senior brother had sought help for a stone tile. His own had been hidden close to his father’s body and handed to him on that terrible night.

He smoothed the corner of the spread red cloth.

Qinglan asked, “Does the higher-ranked player always win?”

“Higher rank brings a better starting hand and more favourable draws,” Zhumo said. “Heaven grants that; the scorekeeper can’t stop it. But once the tiles are in hand, what to keep and discard is still your own decision. A good hand can be played badly. No one can write ‘win’ for you in advance.”

Jinshi snorted. “On the high posts, I can judge where to land, and my feet can follow. At a tile table, when several paths clash in my head, I still pick the wrong one easily.”

Qinglan looked at him, but his senior brother was not smiling. The hand wrapped around the stone tile still rested beside the lion head.

Zhumo closed the notebook. “We don’t yet know what your White Dragon can do. Don’t read a few lines of old compacts and then take it into someone else’s game.”

Qinglan drew his hand away from the red cloth.

Yuening returned the newer Red Dragon to the wooden box, recorded the inspection they had witnessed, replaced the seal, and added a fresh fingerprint beside Xiao Cheng’an’s. Zhumo recorded only what he had seen in his notebook, without writing himself into last night’s events as an eyewitness.

After hearing Qinglan fill in the events of the two nights, Jinshi lowered his head to study the White Dragon. He reached out one thick finger, almost touched it, then drew back.

“Keep your things safe,” he said. “People came for the Red Dragon today. That cat had his eye on the White Dragon instead. What if more people come?”

Qinglan wrapped the red cloth around it, layer by layer, and tucked it into his robe.

“Then we’ll go and get some answers,” he said. “The old stone archway. Tomorrow night, at the Zi watch.”

(End of Chapter Three)
