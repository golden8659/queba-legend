Chapter 2The Abacus and the Scroll

Qinglan stood by the door and unfolded the slip he had just been gripping.

The Red Dragon in his palm had yellowed bone and red paint faded to dark brown. Beneath the final downward stroke of the character was a chip the size of a grain of rice. It differed from the new tile Master Xiao guarded, and had come from somewhere else.

There was only one line, written in a neat, precise hand, like that of a clerk copying official documents:

Nine Mahjong Jade tokens. Meet me at the Hall of the Mahjong God. I will see that hand from ten years ago through to the end with you.

There was no signature. In one corner of the paper was a tiny drawing of a hand. Five fingers, spread wide.

Qinglan clenched the paper in his palm, crushing it into a ball. He glanced back inside. Everyone was still crowded around the square wooden table, arguing loudly. No one was looking towards the door.

He tucked the crumpled paper inside his robe, beside the White Dragon.

◆

“We lost him!”

Several waiters pushed back inside, streaming with sweat. Hu Shan had burst through the crowd onto Crossroads Street and plunged into the market, overturning two vegetable carriers and a cage of chickens. By the time the waiters had chased him past two junctions, he was long gone.

The leading waiter held up half a sleeve. “All we got was this.”

Inside the cuff, a little hand was embroidered in black thread. Five fingers, spread wide.

Inside, Qian Youqing in the West seat went from ashen to pale, then flushed red. He braced himself on the table and rose, forgetting his prayer beads as he pounded the tabletop.

“This—this has nothing to do with Hengchang! Hu Shan was a player we hired. If his hands weren't clean, he deceived us too! Hengchang has been in Yunchuan for thirty years—”

“Thirty years?” Luo Jinke cradled her swollen, purple-red wrist and gave a cold laugh. “Thirty years of honest dealings, and yet for these past two years, at the northern apothecary, at the academy's rear hill, it's always been Hu Shan at the table?”

The steward turned dark purple.

“Jinke.” Tao Lingtao gave her a gentle tug.

Tao Lingtao’s eyes were still red and dust remained on her knees, but her voice had steadied. “If Manager Qian says he knew nothing, then so be it. How today’s match should be judged, I leave to our scorekeeper and Master Xiao.”

Every eye in the room turned to Yuening.

◆

Yuening did not hurry to speak.

Holding the extra Red Dragon between her fingers, she walked to the west window.

The sun was sinking westward. Light slanted through the lattice and fell across her hand. She turned the tile over, rotating it little by little. Seen straight on, its back was smooth as jade, without a mark. At one particular angle, a hair-fine incision suddenly cast a faint shadow.

Five fingers, spread wide.

A little bone dust still clung to the incision's edge.

“Bone dust still clings to the edge of the carving,” Yuening said. “This part wasn’t cleaned properly.”

Qinglan joined her and handed over the Red Dragon from the doorway. “And this one?”

Yuening turned it in the same shaft of light. The same ghostly hand. The same five spread fingers. But the edges of this incision had worn round and blunt, and a line of black grime lay ingrained in the groove.

Her fingers slowly tightened.

“This one has worn for a long time.” She raised her head. “To know what year it was carved, we need the old records.”

◆

The two Red Dragons lay side by side on the square wooden table. One snow-white. One yellowed with age.

Someone in the crowd whispered the name.

“Ghost Hand.”

The room fell silent at once.

No name had inspired stranger rumours in the past two years. There was said to be a band of people who had turned cheating into a sinister art, tampering with sworn matches of honour wherever they went, stealing land, property and reputations for their clients. They never showed themselves. In each match they rigged, they left only a Red Dragon engraved with the Ghost Hand mark.

Until now, people had thought it a storyteller's invention to frighten children.

Now Ghost Hand lay on the table. And one of its hands had stood in the doorway only moments before.

Xiao Cheng’an watched the two Red Dragons on the table and said nothing for a long time.

Qian Youqing spoke first.

“His hand hasn’t been scored yet.”

His gaze avoided the two Red Dragons and settled on the abacus beside Yuening.

She looked up at him.

“The hand’s been checked. We can make the score clear too.”

She spread Qinglan’s fourteen tiles out again.

“One-Two-Three of Characters, Five-Six-Seven of Bamboo, a pung of Three of Dots, Six-Seven of Dots with Red Dragon as Eight of Dots, and a pair of North Winds. Four groups and a pair, fourteen tiles. Closed-hand self-draw.”

The abacus clicked.

“Closed hand: two points. Each of the other three pays two. The two Tao seats cancel one another’s payment and receipt; the two Hengchang seats each pay two, so Tao gains four net. Three down after the first three hands: normal scoring for this hand would put Tao one point ahead over the four hands.”

The manager’s fingers stopped on his beads.

“Even by the score at the start of the match, Tao did not lose,” Yuening said.

Tao Lingtao’s tense shoulders slowly lowered at last.

But Yuening did not stamp the result. She set the abacus aside and opened another page.

“Today’s whole match cannot be settled on that one point alone.”

Her brush tip touched the entry recording Hushan pulling out the Red Dragon.

“Before the shuffle: one hundred and thirty-six tiles, four Red Dragons. East began with fourteen and had thirteen after his first discard. Normal draws and discards then balanced. After his final discard he drew no more, yet held fourteen. Master Xiao saw him take this tile from his hand and move it into his sleeve. Counted separately, the other one hundred and thirty-six tiles already include four Red Dragons. This one set apart is a fifth. The original record and discard river have also been checked against each other.”

The manager’s lips moved.

“Hushan was the player you brought to the table.” Yuening looked at him. “Hengchang Trading House forfeits this match. The western waterway remains with the Tao family dye works.”

She entered the one-point calculation in a supplementary column, adding “Hand verification; no separate score settlement.” In the main column she wrote only:

Forfeit.

Xiao Cheng’an took the seal and pressed it into the main column.

The manager stared at the square red impression.

“What about the matches for the northern apothecary and the academy’s rear hill?” Luo Jinke asked.

“Today’s evidence goes to the Arbitration Office, and those two matches will be re-examined separately,” Yuening said. “This tile decides today’s match. The old matches need their own evidence.”

Qian Youqing said no more. He closed his beads in his palm and left with his staff.

Only when his footsteps had passed through the door did the young embroiderers on the stairs dare to sob aloud. They rushed down and surrounded Tao Lingtao.

◆

Xiao Cheng'an took a small brocade box from his robe.

Inside lay a thumb-sized jade tablet, a clear, deep green, like a pool of spring water held still. He engraved the day's date on its back with a small knife, then offered it to Qinglan in both hands.

“Young Master Qing, you formed a proper winning hand, pointed out the concealed tile and asked the three parties to verify it together. That is how we have this evidence today.” Xiao Cheng'an held the brocade box a little further towards him. “By the rules of the Way of Mahjong, you are awarded one Mahjong Jade. Your first.”

Qinglan accepted it. The jade was warm.

In his father's study, there had once hung a string of Mahjong Jade tokens. Twenty-seven in all. On the day of the tile-breaking sentence, they had been confiscated before everyone and smashed, one after another, against the flagstones.

“Nine to enter the Tianyuan Mahjong Assembly,” Yuening said softly behind him. “Eight more to go.”

At last Tao Lingtao emerged from the knot of embroiderers and approached, red-eyed, presenting a brocade pouch in both hands. Part of a Dipper gold coin showed at its mouth.

Qinglan did not count them. One coin meant ten taels of silver, a thousand copper coins. His bowl of noodles cost only three coppers.

“You have saved the livelihoods of more than twenty people at the dye works and workshop. Please accept this small token…”

“This isn’t money staked under the agreement. It’s our thanks,” Tao Lingtao said.

“I understand.” Qinglan gently pushed the pouch back. “But before Miss Luo touched the tea tray, I could have warned her. I said nothing. I didn’t take my seat today to collect a reward. Keep it for her hand, and for a few more embroidery frames for the children.”

“If you won’t take the gold,” Tao Lingtao said, “you mustn’t give back that robe. You wore it to play for the Tao family today. Keep it. The workshop can hurry out another for Master Wan’s son.”

Tao Lingtao was about to speak again when a young embroiderer squeezed past her. Blushing, she pressed something into Qinglan's palm: a tiny handkerchief, with a crooked sprig of green bamboo embroidered in one corner.

“I made it,” she said. “I did it wrong.”

Qinglan looked down at the bamboo for a long time.

“No,” he said. “You didn't.”

◆

Under the rules of the Way of Mahjong, the scorekeeper and witness had to seal the evidence together until someone arrived from the Arbitration Office.

Yuening placed both Red Dragons in a small wooden box. Xiao Cheng'an affixed a sealing strip. Each added a fingerprint.

The strip had barely been secured when a gentle, unhurried voice spoke behind Qinglan.

“Young master. A word, if I may.”

Qinglan turned.

Behind him stood a round figure with sharply contrasting black-and-white fur and two glossy black eye patches. He wore a somewhat worn bamboo-patterned long robe, a brush with thinning bristles hung at his waist, and a bamboo basket half a person's height was strapped to his back. Scrolls of every length crowded the basket, some still tied with faded red cord.

A panda. No—a chronicler.

Across the land of Tianyuan, the historians who copied the game records of famous matches were called chroniclers. Most were elders among the spirit beasts who had taken bodily form: long-lived, with good memories and the patience to sit beside a mahjong table all day, recording every play exactly as it happened.

“My name is Zhumo.” The panda clasped his hands. His sleeves were too short, exposing a length of furry forearm. “I heard that sworn matches of honour in Yunchuan have been plagued by strange happenings these past two years, so I came to make a record. I did not expect to record such a fine hand: ready to win in five turns, no pung, no kong, passing on a discard win, then a self-draw with a concealed hand.”

“You are too kind.”

“Many people can play.” Zhumo watched him, his voice softening. “But when the wild tile was revealed, you looked first at whose side of the table that stretch of wall stood on, not at the tile itself. Before discarding, you looked around every other player's discard pool. And when you drew your winning tile, you passed your thumb across its face. You knew what it was without looking.”

He paused.

“Only one man in the world had all three habits. You are Qing Yuanshan's son.”

Qinglan did not deny it.

Zhumo's gaze travelled past him to the newly sealed wooden box on the table.

“That old Red Dragon.” Suddenly the panda's voice sounded dry. “May I see it again?”

◆

The storytelling room on the second floor of the Rain Pavilion was empty now.

Xiao Cheng'an shut the door and brewed a pot of tea himself. Yuening followed them upstairs, carrying the ledger and wooden box.

Zhumo did not drink. He took off his bamboo basket, rummaged for a long time and drew out a yellowed scroll. Its red cord had faded to pale pink. He needed several attempts to untie it.

A small heading stood at the top.

Tiandu. Wangjiang Teahouse. Sworn match of honour.

Dispute: Tiandu's mahjong tax.

East seat: Qing Yuanshan (Tuidao School). North seat: Xuanshu.

“I copied this,” Zhumo said. “I was the chronicler at that match.”

Qinglan stared at the name Xuanshu. His hands slowly clenched on his knees.

Ten years ago. Tiandu. Wangjiang Teahouse.

That, too, had been a crowded teahouse, with a square wooden table. His father sat in the East seat, wearing his best dark-blue gown, his back perfectly straight. The dispute concerned Tiandu's mahjong tax and the livelihoods of hundreds of small mahjong houses across the land. Dozens of those houses had asked his father to represent them, to win them justice.

Fourteen-year-old Qinglan stood behind him, holding his tea bowl in palms wet with sweat.

In the North seat, immediately before his father in the order of play, sat a man in blue-grey official robes. His face was kindly, his long fingers pale and clean. He always spoke calmly, without haste. He represented the authorities.

At the end of that hand, his father laid down his tiles. Self-draw. The room cheered.

The man in blue-grey official robes applauded softly, twice. Then he rose with a slight smile and reached out to open the stretch of wall in front of Qinglan's father.

Inside it lay an extra Red Dragon.

The cheering stopped dead. The tea bowl fell from Qinglan's hands and shattered. To this day, he remembered its sharp, brittle sound.

Zhumo unrolled the scroll to its very end. After the game record, he had drawn a tile in meticulous detail, with a note beside it.

Evidence: one extra Red Dragon found in the East seat's wall. A rice-grain-sized chip beneath the final vertical stroke of its central character. Delivered to the Tiandu Mahjong Arbitration Office for sealed storage.

Yuening peeled back the sealing strip, lifted the yellowed Red Dragon from the box and placed it beside the drawing.

Beneath the last vertical stroke, a chip the size of a grain of rice.

Exactly the same.

Qinglan looked back and forth twice between the drawing and the tile. The chip recorded ten years ago was before him now. But the scroll did not say how the tile had left the storeroom, or into whose hands it had passed.

The storytelling room was so quiet that they could hear the street vendors calling below.

“Back then…” Zhumo murmured. “No one turned it over.”

“This tile was locked in the Tiandu Arbitration Office's storeroom ten years ago.” Xiao Cheng'an's voice had changed. “Whoever could take it out of there…”

He did not finish. Yuening did it for him.

“Anyone who can take things from the Arbitration Office's storeroom has no reason to fear the Arbitration Office.”

Qinglan looked at the old Red Dragon.

Suddenly, Qinglan saw that nod from the afternoon again.

The man knew about his father. But had he been part of the match then, or acquired the tile afterwards? And how had the old tile left the storehouse?

Qinglan clenched the hand on his knee, then slowly released it.

“Record the newer tile and the old one’s provenance separately,” he said. “We can’t write him a confession on the strength of a nod.”

Yuening returned the old Red Dragon to the box, checked it and the new one individually, and entered this examination in her ledger. Xiao Cheng'an replaced the sealing strip. Both added fresh fingerprints, and the wooden box remained before their eyes.

◆

Yuening had already bent over the game record.

She read game records faster than books. Her finger slid down the scroll line by line as she murmured, “First turn, East discards North Wind. Second turn, North calls pung on Three of Bamboo… Fifth turn, East is ready to win…”

Her fingertip stopped.

“Third turn. South discards a Two of Dots.” She pointed farther down. “Sixth turn. West discards Two of Dots. East calls pung and exposes three on the table.”

Her finger moved to the ninth turn.

“Ninth turn. North discards a Two of Dots.”

She looked up, her eyes fiercely bright. “One, plus three, plus one. A set has only four Two of Dots tiles. How could North have a fifth?”

No one answered.

Zhumo lowered his head and looked at his furry hands. A long time passed before he spoke.

“I noticed it then too.”

Yuening's finger did not move away. All five Two of Dots tiles remained there on the scroll.

His voice was almost inaudible. “That night, at the inn, I was making a clean copy. I reached the ninth turn and saw it. But the tile-breaking sentence had been passed on the spot. The very next day, the man in the North seat was promoted to a position at court. I was only a copyist.”

He gave a bitter smile.

“I told myself that a chronicler records. He does not judge. I buried the scroll at the bottom of my basket and never took it out again for ten years. Every spring, when the dead are remembered, I dream of your father standing beside that table, looking down at the broken tiles.”

Yuening sprang to her feet. “You—”

Her hand still pressed on the record. The extra Two of Dots, the Red Dragon with the matching chip: both were right there. Her voice tightened. “All of this needs investigating. That night, did you seek out no one at all?”

Zhumo offered no defence. He simply looked at Qinglan and waited.

Qinglan said nothing.

He wanted to ask: Did you ever see my father again afterwards? But as the words reached his lips, he thought instead of that noon.

The noodle stall in the back alley. Noodles clumped in the bowl. The words drifting through the woodshed door: “It will be on the rim of the tea tray for the third hand.”

He had heard. He had said nothing. He had eaten every strand in his bowl. Then he had stood at the edge of the crowd and watched Luo Jinke reach for the tray.

He had been silent for half a day. Luo Jinke's hand had been swollen for half a day.

Zhumo had been silent for ten years.

Qinglan reached out and rested a hand lightly on the scroll, over the name Qing Yuanshan.

He said nothing. Neither forgiveness nor blame.

◆

Slowly, Yuening sat down again.

Her eyes settled on the very end of the scroll. After the drawing of the evidence, after Zhumo's square, careful signature, there was another small line. The handwriting was graceful, as though someone else had added it later.

This hand is suspect.

Scorekeeper: Shen Suyun.

Yuening's fingers slowly traced the words. Her fingertips trembled.

“That is… my mother's writing.”

She traced the word “doubt” again. Its final stroke ended in a hurry, just as it had in the copybooks her mother wrote for her. She smoothed the corner of the paper, reluctant to move her hand away.

Qinglan and Zhumo both turned towards her.

“My mother was a scorekeeper too. Ten years ago, she went to Tiandu to officiate at a sworn match of honour. After she came home, she couldn't sleep, night after night. She sat by the lamp with her abacus, working through a ledger. I asked what she was calculating. She wouldn't say.” Yuening paused. “Then one day, she said she had a document to deliver to the Arbitration Office. She stroked my head and said she would be home before supper.”

“She never came back.”

Zhumo's expression changed. “Miss Shen… I remember her. She kept watching the North seat's wall throughout that hand.”

Yuening set the small ebony abacus on the table. Its frame was worn glossy. On the first bead of the lowest rod was a tiny engraved character: Yun.

“The very first day she taught me to use an abacus, she said a scorekeeper answers to numbers, never to people.”

She looked up at Qinglan. Her eyes were red, but the corners of her mouth remained stubbornly lifted.

“And now the numbers are telling me your father was wronged.”

◆

Three gentle knocks sounded at the door.

The visitor was a young man in his early twenties, his cuffs tied back and a faded indigo apron about his waist. He carried a three-tier red-lacquer food carrier.

“Pei Zhiwei, head cook at the Rain Pavilion.” He set the carrier down and opened it tier by tier: a dish of prawn dumplings, a dish of turnip cakes, a bowl of chicken soup with red dates, and a basket of steamed buns filled with tender bamboo shoots, fresh from the steamer. As he lifted the lid, warmth billowed into the room.

Zhumo's nose twitched involuntarily.

“Proprietor Tao asked me to make these.” Pei Zhiwei laid out chopsticks, glanced at Qinglan and smiled. “You had noodles in the back alley at noon, young master. They had stuck together, but you didn't leave a strand. I thought you ought at least to have a hot supper.”

Qinglan stared.

Yuening burst out laughing. Zhumo was already reaching for a second bamboo-shoot bun. The tension that had held the storytelling hall all afternoon eased at last.

Pei Zhiwei packed away the food box and first told Xiao Cheng’an, “The records here still haven’t been checked in full. The proprietor’s agreed that Master Qing can stay tonight, in the little room where you usually rest.”

“Good,” Xiao Cheng’an said. “Tonight I have to guard the box, so let him rest.”

Pei Zhiwei acknowledged him, then added unhurriedly:

“Also, before Hushan took his seat, he changed clothes in the kitchen woodshed and left this behind.”

From his sleeve he took a palm-sized wooden token, blackened as though with smoke.

On the front was an open hand. On the back, a name:

Nanchao.

Nanchao, the greatest seaport in the land. Pinghe Hall had its headquarters there.

“Ghost Hand's next destination,” Zhumo said softly.

“Or their nest,” said Xiao Cheng'an.

◆

“You really haven’t sat at a table for ten years?” Yuening asked.

Qinglan looked at the old ink on the paper.

“No. Later, someone taught me that without touching the tiles, I could still learn from a game others had finished.” He rubbed the wrist that had tensed while pushing his tiles forward. “I practised footwork and gathering my breath too. Before I sat down today, I used what he taught me to keep my hands from shaking.”

“What did he teach?”

“First, separate what you guess from what you’ve seen yourself. Only when your hands are steady and your breathing connects may you learn more.”

He looked at his palm. When he first reached this exercise, his fingertips stayed numb for a long while. Now the energy connected and that numbness seldom came. Whenever his master asked how far he had advanced, he had to demonstrate it himself.

After the last test, his master said he had only just steadied himself at the early Two of Bamboo stage and must not rush higher. He remembered gathering his breath then, the two broken currents in his chest finally joining. His master called this path Flow and Growth. Qinglan had practised it, and knew he was far from mastering it.

He did not say who that master was. Yuening did not press him. She checked the provenance of the two pieces of evidence, old and new, once more.

“Master Zhumo.” Qinglan raised his head. “May I borrow that game record to make a copy?”

“What do you intend to do?”

Qinglan looked out of the window. The sun was setting. On Crossroads Street, the old sugar-figure seller had set up his stall again. A few children surrounded him, clamouring for a golden lion.

Qinglan did not answer. Yuening answered for him.

“Next year's Tianyuan Mahjong Assembly.” She slapped her abacus onto the table, beads clacking. “He's going. So am I.”

Her fingers flew over the abacus, beads clattering. When she finished, she looked up. “The Starwatch Pavilion calculated the date long ago. From today, there are one hundred and ninety-seven days until the hall's doors open.”

One hundred and ninety-seven days. Nine Mahjong Jade tokens. He had only one.

Qinglan looked at that column of beads for a long time.

“I want to clear my father's name.”

Ten years. This was the first time he had spoken those words aloud.

“Miss Yuening, this is dangerous—”

“My mother has been missing for ten years. I spent five years at the scorekeepers' table to find out what that one line meant.” Yuening glared at him. “Besides, you're a scholar who hasn't touched a tile in ten years. When you get to the Assembly, who will keep your score? Who will check the tiles? Who will count exactly how many Red Dragons are on the table?”

Qinglan opened his mouth. He could not find a single word. Xiao Cheng'an stroked his beard and laughed.

Zhumo rolled up the game record, tied it carefully with the faded red cord and offered it to Qinglan in both hands.

“No need to copy it,” the panda said. “I will come with you. There are words I have owed for ten years. It is time I spoke them before the world.”

Qinglan accepted the scroll.

He glanced at Zhumo without speaking, picked up the teapot, poured away the tea that had sat cold before him all afternoon, and filled his bowl again.

“Those Heavenly Tiles Master Xiao spoke of,” Qinglan watched the steam, “have you copied accounts of them?”

Zhumo did not nod at once.

“I’ve copied game compacts left by tile-bearers, and their receipts and payments.” He turned the roll back to that afternoon’s hand. “One tile-bearer said that when his energy connected steadily, he could consider several lines of play together, less likely to lose one while watching another. Martial artists also use it to sense an attack’s approach and sustain their strength. They call that power spirit energy.”

Qinglan studied the draws and discards on the paper. His fingertip stopped beside Hushan’s last entry.

“Can it tell you even the tiles that haven’t been shown?”

Zhumo covered several lines on the roll with his hand.

“If I don’t let you see these, can thinking faster tell you what I haven’t written?”

Qinglan did not answer. He withdrew his finger.

Zhumo lifted his palm. “What you see and what you guess remain different. As for the Heavenly Tiles’ own divine powers, the records I’ve copied differ. One tile can’t speak for them all.”

He gathered the roll. “Some pledge spirit energy in a compact and really pay it to the winner if they lose. A few gold coins can’t stand in for it. I’ve copied that account; I haven’t seen each person’s abilities myself.”

“Someone called Hushan a Mahjong Adept today,” Qinglan said. “Is that the same as the ranks you mean?”

“That’s what people call him.” Zhumo glanced at the wrist Qinglan had rubbed. “Your hand has been trained. But I haven’t actually seen you use your abilities. I can’t give you a title at random.”

Qinglan nodded. He knew his own early Two of Bamboo stage; Zhumo had not seen it, and could not attest to it for him. He did not reach for the White Dragon inside his robe.

“So it’s like today’s match?”

“No. Today decided the waterway. The points in the ledger are neither gold nor spirit energy.” Zhumo set his cup down steadily. “The Tianyuan Mahjong Assembly is what people call the Assembly. There is only that one assembly. Your mahjong jade is a credential recognized by the Arbitration Office. Don’t confuse the Heavenly Tiles’ legendary divine power with the jade used for entry.”

Qinglan touched the small brocade box holding his mahjong jade and asked no more. The storyteller’s wonders had seemed to belong only to a story. Now Zhumo said he had seen the written compacts, and Qinglan wanted more than the tale.

◆

That night, the upstairs windows of the Rain Pavilion stood open.

Yuening stayed by the wooden box. Qinglan wanted to compare the old Red Dragon’s carving and first asked both her and Xiao Cheng’an. She recorded the inspection time, removed the seal, and counted the two Red Dragons separately, one old and one new. A fresh sealing strip lay by Xiao Cheng’an; the removed one rested on the ledger, its ink not yet dry.

Qinglan spread the red cloth, revealing the White Dragon his father had left him.

“You said you’d only compare the markings,” Yuening said, watching him.

“I know.”

The two Red Dragons remained before her eyes, one snow-white, one yellowed. Qinglan lifted the old one and brought the White Dragon’s edge against it.

To the north, a few stars cleared the eaves.

A hint of green seeped from the White Dragon. Then a hand appeared on its face.

Qinglan’s breath stopped.

He remembered that hand. When he was little and could not hold a brush, his father had pressed a thumb over his forefinger just so, guiding him through his first character for “upright.”

But this time his father’s hand reached towards a tile box.

A Red Dragon rested against its rim. From the other side, a length of blue-grey sleeve pressed over, blocking his father’s hand. The tile struck the edge of the box.

Click.

Beneath the final downward stroke of the red character, a white chip the size of a grain of rice broke open.

Qinglan jerked back. The White Dragon and old Red Dragon parted, and the hand vanished.

Yuening’s abacus dropped onto the table. Xiao Cheng’an’s folded fan closed inch by inch.

Qinglan looked down at the old Red Dragon. That tiny chip lay beside his fingertip.

“Put it back,” Yuening said softly.

He did.

The same hand, the same sleeve, the same soft click. Then it began again at the same point.

The image did not show what was inside the box. No one told him what his father had been trying to see.

Qinglan separated the two objects, but his hand remained suspended.

He had not seen his father for nine years.

Yet his father’s hand had just been before his eyes.

Yuening moved the lamp closer, then farther away. The light and shadow changed, but the fragment remained the same. None of them saw what happened next.

“Ten years ago.” Only when he spoke did Qinglan realise his voice was hoarse. “I was standing behind Father then. But before play began, I was holding his tea. I wasn't watching his hands all the time.”

Xiao Cheng'an's folding fan lay on his knees. He never opened it.

“For now, record only what you saw,” he said. “We don't yet know what that hand meant to do.”

Yuening wrote what they had seen in her ledger, then looked at Qinglan. “Don't take this half a scene and admit your father did something he never did. And don't use it to excuse the hand opposite him either.”

Qinglan nodded. He moved the White Dragon back onto the red cloth.

Yuening checked the old Red Dragon against the new one in the box and returned it to its place. The old sealing strip went into the examination record. Xiao Cheng'an fitted another fresh strip; both added fresh fingerprints, and the time of resealing was recorded.

Only after the inspection was entered did Qinglan go to the little room Xiao Cheng’an had yielded to him. The old storyteller moved his own chair to the hall’s doorway and brought the lamp over. The box stood on the table between the door and the bench, seal facing out, where both guards could see it.

“Rest a while. I’ll watch,” Xiao Cheng’an said, settling into his chair. “Any inspection still needs the two of us to record it together. Neither opens it alone.”

Yuening did not ask for the little room. She was the scorekeeper; tonight she had to stay beside the box with Master Xiao. The bench was right by the table, the seal in view whenever she rose. She lay down fully dressed, but could not sleep.

Whenever she closed her eyes, she saw the scroll. Turn by turn, tile by tile, she went through it again in her mind: the Two of Dots on the third turn, the pung on the sixth, the tile on the ninth that should not have existed. Not a single number was wrong.

She thought of her mother sitting by the lamp. The abacus had clicked through one night after another. Had this been the account she was trying to settle?

Yuening took the abacus from inside her jacket. Her thumb found the lowest bead engraved with Yun, and gently moved it.

Click.

“Mother,” she whispered to the dark roof beams, “wherever you got to, I'll carry on from there.”

There were no lights in the back alley.

Two figures stood close against the wall, heads tilted back, counting the second-floor windows of the Rain Pavilion one by one.

“That one.”

“Tonight?”

“Too many people. Too many eyes.” The other voice was low. “Tomorrow night, the fifteenth, the lion-dance troupe will perform the lion-awakening ceremony outside. Gongs, drums, people filling the street. No one will hear anything on the second floor then.”

“What does the proprietor say?”

“If that new Red Dragon reaches the Arbitration Office, Hengchang will have to give it all back: the northern apothecary, the academy's rear hill. The proprietor says we must recover it.”

“And the old one?”

The man fell silent for a moment. Then his voice dropped lower still.

“The old one is not to be touched. The man in the woven hat said that one is for the Qing boy.”

Behind the paper of the second-floor window, not a candle glowed.

In the wooden box, the two Red Dragons rested quietly against one another. One snow-white. One yellowed with age.

(End of Chapter Two)
