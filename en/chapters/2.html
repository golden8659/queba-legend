Chapter 2Hush Money

Not one of the Rain Pavilion's two hundred-odd people had left.

They were waiting for a thief.

Qinglan stepped over the threshold behind Yuening. Every eye turned towards him, settling on his left hand. The yellowed old Red Dragon was still gripped between his fingers, rain dripping from its corner.

Only then did he understand. The thief they awaited was him.

A moment earlier, in the rear alley, he had answered Yuening's question.

“A White Dragon.” He pressed the red wrapping back against his heart. Rain had soaked the green-and-white robe through; the gold-thread bamboo leaves lay wet and cold against his neck. “This very tile. He put it in my hand himself that night.”

Water streamed from the rim of Yuening's umbrella. “Today Hu Shan said you were carrying the hundred and thirty-seventh tile too. I counted. Yours wasn't one of the hundred and thirty-six.”

“And ten years ago?” Qinglan looked up. “Did anyone count that time?”

Yuening opened her mouth. No answer came.

Just then a shriek rose from the hall, followed by a clamour. “Scorekeeper! Where's the scorekeeper?”

Yuening turned and ran. Qinglan followed, gripping the old Red Dragon, his numb right arm unable to swing, as though he dragged someone else's limb behind him.

The overturned table of justice had been set upright. Its four copper plates remained dark beside its legs.

Qian Youqing stood next to it, the great string of prayer beads arranged neatly across his chest. Gone was the man who had cowered beneath a table shrieking. Now his face wore an expression of wounded indignation. Seven or eight brown-clad Hengchang servants stood behind him, ringing half the table.

“He's back!” Qian Youqing pointed. “Look closely, everyone! What is that in his hand?”

The crowd erupted.

“Another Red Dragon!”

“His hands were empty when he ran out. Now he's brought back another!”

“What happened to the one with the hand mark?”

Yuening ignored them. She crouched and searched once more through the tiles she had protected. All four branded Red Dragons were there. The unbranded one was not. She had known before re-entering the hall, but she needed to search again before witnesses.

Qian Youqing had been waiting for this.

“Hu Shan ran. Hengchang accepts that we chose the wrong man.” His voice climbed, high and shrill. “But two matters must be settled today. First: where is this supposed extra tile now? Without the evidence, how can Hengchang be judged to have betrayed the Way? Second—” He turned halfway round and pointed at Qinglan. “Tao's substitute player came to the table with a tile in his robe! Two hundred pairs of eyes saw it! And you've all heard how his father cheated, haven't you?”

“Qing family spawn!” someone echoed.

“Search him!” Qian Youqing's voice rose again as he fingered his beads.

Several brown-clad servants pushed forward. One reached for Qinglan's sleeve, catching the green-and-white robe. Its broken red cord swung, knocking the jade pendant against his waist.

“Let go.”

Tao Lingtao stepped before him. She had just helped Luo Jinke sit down. One of her twin buns had come loose, the flower at her temple hung crooked, and a strand of hair lay in the hollow of her throat, rising and falling with her breath. She had no time to tidy it.

“This robe belongs to the Tao family,” she said. “Lay hands on me first.”

The servant's hand froze in midair. He looked back at Qian Youqing.

Crack.

A storyteller's block struck the table. Xiao Cheng'an rose with his walking stick. There was not a trace of amusement on that usually smiling old face.

“Let us settle the matter of the carried tile first.” He shook the agreement out of his sleeve. “Clause five: those taking a seat must declare what they carry. An undeclared object brought into play means forfeiture. If it does not enter play, a fault is recorded, but no forfeiture.”

The hall fell silent to listen.

“Before the match began, I witnessed Hu Shan's search. Sleeves, robe, boot shafts: all clear.” Xiao Cheng'an paused. “Young Master Qing joined as an emergency substitute for the fourth hand. Steward Qian said, ‘Sit.’ Scorekeeper Shen replaced the tiles and renewed the incense. The whole hall urged us to begin. He was not searched.”

He added, “One other matter. After the third hand, Hu Shan went to the Hengchang party for a towel. When he returned to his seat, I did not search him again.”

He laid down the agreement. “Record both faults against the witness. I was remiss.”

For a moment no one spoke. Few in Yunchuan had ever seen a witness acknowledge his own errors before two hundred people.

“As for whether it entered play—” Xiao Cheng'an turned towards the counter. “Chronicler.”

Zhumo stood, cradling his scroll. His voice was soft, but every word landed firmly. “South began the fourth hand with thirteen tiles, drew nine and discarded eight, winning with fourteen. Scorekeeper Shen counted them before everyone. Every one was branded. South's hand never once reached inside his robe.”

“I counted his fourteen winning tiles too,” said Yuening. “The White Dragon fell onto the table only when Hu Shan pulled open his robe.”

Xiao Cheng'an nodded and turned to Qinglan. “Undeclared, but never brought into play. One fault recorded, no forfeiture. Young Master Qing, do you accept?”

“I do,” said Qinglan.

The ready admission briefly silenced Qian Youqing. Then his eyes shifted and he returned to the attack. “Very well, a fault for the substitute. What about my first question? The evidence? He ran out empty-handed and came back with a Red Dragon. Who knows whether he switched the one on the table?”

Yuening held out her hand. Qinglan placed the old Red Dragon in her palm.

She lifted it to the lamp, turned it over and back again. “Yellowed bone, darkened red paint. Paint more than ten years old. No Rain Pavilion brand on the back. A little hand engraved at the lower right, all five fingers spread, the cuts filled with black grime. Every finger intact. Today's unbranded tile was snow-white bone, freshly engraved.” She returned it to him. “They are not the same tile. I have noted this one's provenance. I will not discuss it here.”

“Then where's the snow-white one?” Qian Youqing pressed. “Where?”

No one could answer.

Qinglan looked down. The White Dragon inside his robe was not cold at all.

He understood what that absence meant. The tile that had drawn his breath whenever it came near was no longer in the building. It had gone far away, taking with it the one piece of evidence they could present to the Arbitration Office.

The White Dragon could tell him only that much. It could not say who had taken the tile. Even if it could, no one would believe it.

“I'll count the tiles first.”

Yuening stood. She had the waiters collect every bone tile from beneath the table, around the pillars and among the broken tea bowls, gathering them onto the tabletop.

“Master Xiao. Master Zhu. Please watch me count.”

She counted swiftly, her small ebony abacus in her left hand, her right turning the tiles. For each complete type she moved a bead. Thirty-six Characters, thirty-six Bamboo, thirty-six Dots. Four each of East, South, West and North. Four each of Red, Green and White Dragons. She turned every tile over, showing its brand to the nearest spectators.

“One hundred and thirty-six.” The final bead clicked into place. “Before the fourth hand, we poured one hundred and thirty-six tiles from the sealed box and counted them before both parties. After the hand, there are still one hundred and thirty-six. Wherever today's unbranded Red Dragon has gone, it is not part of this set.”

“So what?” Qian Youqing sneered. “Master Xiao pinned a hand. A tile fell. Where is that tile? Without it, the verdict cannot be sealed. The agreement says ‘proved on the spot’. Within three days, Hengchang will petition the Arbitration Office for a replay review, with someone clean in that seat!”

He put great weight on “clean”.

Xiao Cheng'an's face darkened. Zhumo was about to speak, game record in his hands, but Qian Youqing cut in first. “A chronicler records. He does not judge. That is the rule of the Way of Mahjong.”

For a moment, no one had an answer.

But Qinglan bent down.

He crawled beneath the table of justice and, with his left hand, dragged a red-lacquer tea tray out of a pool of broken crockery. The very tray delivered to Luo Jinke in the third hand.

He turned it over and raised it to the lamp.

A small lump of wax clung beneath the rim, an embroidery needle set slantwise in it, point outward. Smaller than a grain of rice, the tip flashed in the light. A trace of blackened medicine stained its blunt end. On the tray's back, two characters had been branded: Hengchang.

“The Rain Pavilion's tea trays are all plain-lacquered,” said Qinglan. “Hengchang brought this one themselves.”

Luo Jinke stood, supporting her purple-black swollen wrist. Strands of red-brown curls had escaped her blue headscarf and stuck to the cold sweat on her forehead. She stared at the needle, teeth clenched. “When I took the tray, it pricked me here, in the web of my thumb.”

She turned her hand over. A black dot the size of a pinhole marked the flesh.

The crowd stirred again. This time the abuse changed direction.

Qian Youqing paled, then stamped his foot. “Which of you did this? Step forward! You've ruined Hengchang's thirty-year reputation!”

The brown-clad servants looked at one another. None moved. Qinglan scanned them. The man who had carried the tray by the side door was not among them.

“Steward Qian,” he said, “in the woodshed, you asked, ‘The tea tray?’”

“Who can testify to what you heard in a woodshed?” Qian Youqing squeezed his prayer beads until they creaked. “Can a rat's word go into a verdict?”

He was right. Qinglan looked at Yuening; she met his eyes and shook her head faintly. Something heard alone would not suffice.

Just then, the crowd at the entrance parted of its own accord.

A small, thin old man entered, back stooped, a blue strip of cloth binding his greying topknot. His wet goatee clung together, and two broad patches darkened the shoulders of his indigo robe. He carried an oilpaper packet. Between the fingers of his right hand, the old bamboo-backed tile still gleamed from long handling.

As he crossed the threshold, the White Dragon against Qinglan's heart gave a brief chill.

Just once, like a fingertip touching him through his clothes.

“Miss Luo's hand mustn't wait.” Zheng Shouzhuo set the packet before Luo Jinke. “I went to the apothecary on Crossroads Street for a poultice to ease the swelling. The proprietor says to apply it first, then call a doctor.”

Quiet praise rippled through the crowd.

“Good old Master Zheng.”

“All this chaos, and he's the only one thinking of the girl's hand.”

Tao Lingtao rose to thank him, her eyes red. Zheng Shouzhuo waved her aside and took two steps towards Qinglan, still smiling kindly. “Young fellow, your robe is hanging open, and Steward Qian keeps shouting for a search. It looks unseemly. Let me straighten it for you, so nothing else falls out.”

He reached with his left hand towards Qinglan's heart.

Before the hand came within a foot, the White Dragon turned bitterly cold. Not the passing touch of a moment earlier: a sudden stabbing, like an ice pick driven against his heart. A fine whiteness formed again on the gold-thread bamboo leaves at his collar.

Qinglan stepped back.

“Master Zheng,” he said, “please don't touch me yet.”

Everyone stared. Old Zheng offered to straighten a young man's clothes, and the young man recoiled. In Yunchuan, such behaviour invited contempt. Someone was already cursing. “Spits on a man's kindness!”

Zheng Shouzhuo's hand stopped in midair. He smiled and withdrew it.

Qinglan was not watching his hand. He was looking at the medicine.

The oilpaper was dry. Even the hemp cord binding it was dry. Yet rain was falling outside, and the old man's shoulders were soaked.

He looked next at the old man's hem and shoes. Grey-black mud spattered the bottom of the indigo robe and the sides of his cloth shoes, furnace ash mixed through it. Crossroads Street was stone-paved, washed clean by the rain. Only the rear alley was spread with cinders dumped from the kitchen.

His thumb was still numb. His eyes were not.

“Master Zheng, the road here from Crossroads Street is paved all the way,” he said. “Where did the furnace ash on your shoes come from?”

“Old age. Unsteady feet.” Zheng Shouzhuo smiled. “I took a short cut part of the way.”

“The short cut is the rear alley. There's no apothecary there,” said Qinglan. “This medicine came through the rain without getting wet because you carried it inside your sleeve.”

He turned to Zhumo. “Master Zhu, after the third hand, choosing the substitute, fetching the chest and changing the tiles kept everyone busy for the time it takes to drink a cup of tea. Did East leave his seat?”

Zhumo bent over his record. A claw tip stopped at a line of small writing. “End of the third hand: East left his seat, received a towel behind the Hengchang party, wiped his hands and returned.”

“And when Master Zheng yielded the seat,” Qinglan asked, “where did he withdraw to?”

Someone in the crowd answered softly. “Behind the Hengchang servants… I saw him.”

The hall went still.

Qinglan said no more. He turned only to the witness. “Master Xiao, I would not presume to search Master Zheng. I ask only that you invite him to turn out his left cuff for everyone to see.”

Zheng Shouzhuo had lived in Yunchuan for decades. Half the city's children had touched their first mahjong tile in his hall. To turn out his sleeve before two hundred people was to stake half a lifetime's reputation—and the life of this stranger of a scholar.

Xiao Cheng'an looked at Qinglan, then at the old man.

“Brother Zheng,” he said at last. “Forgive me.”

Zheng Shouzhuo did not resist. He tucked the old bamboo-backed tile into his right palm and slowly turned out his own left cuff.

A fine white powder coated the sleeve's lining, as though someone had ground bone inside it.

Yuening stepped forward, took a little on her fingertip and rubbed it. “Bone dust. A freshly engraved design on a tile back, not wiped clean afterwards.”

As the sleeve turned, something slipped out, rang against the stone floor and rolled to Qinglan's feet.

A copper coin.

Qinglan bent to pick it up. From his own sleeve he took the other coin, the one the man in the woven hat had dropped before his nose in the woodshed. He laid them side by side on the table.

Both edges had been filed with a narrow mark, like half a finger.

Exactly alike.

Yuening did not look at the coins. She reached into the upturned sleeve pocket, felt inside a hidden layer and brought out an object, which she laid on the table.

A Red Dragon. Snow-white bone, no brand on the back, a little hand with five spread fingers engraved at the lower right.

Someone in the crowd sucked in a breath.

“When I turned the tiles over earlier,” Yuening said steadily, “the little finger on the tile Hu Shan put down was missing a stroke. The engraving knife had slipped.” With her brush handle, she turned the tile to expose the lower right corner. “This one is missing it too.”

Qinglan stared at that missing stroke. Cold ran down his back.

In the rear alley, through the rain, he had seen only the red symbol on the tile the man in the woven hat waved between two fingers. He had assumed it was this one. It was not. This one had been in Zheng Shouzhuo's sleeve all along.

The man had more than one.

The White Dragon's chill tightened. Steam had been rising straight from the cup nearest the Red Dragon. Now it clung to the rim and streamed towards the tile, wisp after wisp, as though drawn by something.

Yuening glanced at Qinglan without speaking. She asked a waiter for a copper washbasin, turned it upside down and covered the Red Dragon.

The steam rose straight again. The ice pick at Qinglan's heart withdrew at once.

Zheng Shouzhuo looked down at the basin and the two coins. After a while, he sighed, without the least trace of panic. More like a lifelong player watching the man opposite lay down a good tile.

“The young are formidable,” he said. “A pity I was one step too late. That tile was supposed to be found inside your robe.”

So the hand that had reached for his heart had not meant to straighten his clothes.

“Who told you to take it?” Qinglan asked.

The old man smiled and gave no answer, saying only, “Young Qing, you can count tiles. Can you reckon with people?”

He slowly smoothed his cuff, found a bench and sat down. The old bamboo-backed tile returned to his fingers, turning ceaselessly. He closed his eyes and said no more.

This time Qian Youqing truly panicked.

He retreated half a step, gripping the prayer beads against his chest so tightly that the cord snapped. Large dark-brown beads scattered across the stone, striking one after another, rolling under tables and around pillars to the spectators' shoes.

“I don't know him!” His shrill voice broke. “Hengchang has never had a single copper's dealings with the Zheng mahjong house—”

“Steward Qian,” Yuening interrupted. “The Red Dragon is back. But today's case does not rest on it alone.”

She opened the ledger and, pointing with her brush, read item by item:

“First: before the fourth hand, one hundred and thirty-six tiles were counted, every one branded. After his ninth-turn discard, Hu Shan held thirteen, the correct number. But one bore no brand, and its little-finger engraving lacked a stroke. Remove it, and he had only twelve. The missing West Wind, branded, fell from his right sleeve before the whole hall. On the seventh turn, the tile in his sleeve entered his standing hand, and a tile from his hand entered his sleeve. The unbranded tile has now come from Master Zheng's sleeve, its missing stroke still there. Second: a needle was concealed beneath a tray brought by Hengchang; Luo Jinke's puncture wound was inflicted today. Third: Hu Shan, the player Hengchang hired, overturned the table and fled.”

She paused. “Those three points are the reckoning. The judgment is not mine.”

She turned the ledger and pushed it towards Xiao Cheng'an.

“As for who passed the tile to Hu Shan, who took it away, whom they meant to plant it on, and who hid the needle in the tray—” She glanced at the old man sitting with closed eyes. “Those are criminal matters of tile trickery, beyond the match. Arrests and questioning belong to the Bureau of Balance.”

Xiao Cheng'an unfolded the agreement again and read clause three. “‘Where either party employs tile trickery in play and it is proved on the spot, the witness shall rule that party defeated.’ Steward Qian, you put your handprint to this too.”

Qian Youqing opened his mouth. No sound came.

“Hu Shan concealed a foreign tile in his sleeve and introduced a hundred and thirty-seventh tile into play. Proved on the spot.” Xiao Cheng'an closed his fan. “Hengchang is ruled defeated. The western waterway remains with the Tao family dye works.”

Only then did Yuening take up her brush and write the verdict in the main column.

Ruled defeated.

“Fourth hand: Tao wins by concealed self-draw, for a net victory of one point across all four hands. That point is recorded as earned,” she said. “The ruling is the witness's and goes in the verdict. The point was won at the table and goes in my ledger. Both stand.”

Xiao Cheng'an took out the witness's copper seal and pressed it onto the words.

Only now did the girls from the embroidery workshop on the stairs dare to weep. They rushed down, surrounding Tao Lingtao and Luo Jinke. The youngest wriggled through fastest, flinging herself into Tao Lingtao's arms and sobbing until she hiccupped.

Tao Lingtao held her with one arm and reached to open the medicine with the other. “Jinke, let's put this on first—”

“Proprietor Tao,” said Qinglan. “Don't use that packet.”

Her hand stopped. She looked at the dry medicine packet, then at the old man on the bench. Her face slowly paled.

Without a word, Yuening sealed the packet, hemp cord and all, with a sealing strip and set it beside the tray. Tao Lingtao turned to two of the older girls. “Go east. Fetch Doctor Lan. Run.”

The girls returned within the time it took to drink a cup of tea, panting. Doctor Lan had gone into the hills to gather herbs and would not return until the day after tomorrow. The apothecary's assistant would advise only this: soak it in well water, do not rub it. Luo Jinke lowered her hand into a basin of well water and clenched her teeth without a word.

Xiao Cheng'an took a small brocade box from his robe. An Arbitration Office seal covered its opening. He broke it before everyone. Inside lay several small rectangular pieces of green jade, each narrower than a bone tile, a cloud carved in relief on the front and a hole for a cord at the top.

“One piece of Mahjong Jade is awarded for victory in an official sworn match of honour.” He selected one and used a small knife to engrave the date on its back. “Proprietor Tao, you played North for all four hands. This is yours.”

Tao Lingtao received it with both hands.

Xiao Cheng'an engraved another. “South was originally Miss Luo's seat. A substitute's victory is credited to the original player.”

Luo Jinke's injured hand could not take it. Tao Lingtao accepted it for her and tied it to the handle of her dyeing brush. Luo Jinke turned away, eyes red, and muttered, “I lost over my three hands. That thing burns to hold.”

“Keep it,” said Tao Lingtao. “Your hand took that needle for the Tao family.”

The whole hall looked towards the scholar. A substitute received no jade. Everyone knew the rule.

But Xiao Cheng'an selected a third piece.

“There is another old custom of the Way,” he said. “Where someone exposes tile trickery before witnesses and secures a ruling of defeat through solid evidence, the witness may award an additional piece.” He engraved the date and laid it in Qinglan's palm. “This is not for your winning hand. It is for saying, ‘His right hand.’”

The jade was warm.

“Nine are needed to enter the Tianyuan Mahjong Assembly,” Xiao Cheng'an added. “Mahjong Jade records how many matches you have won, not the extent of your skill. Grades are the Arbitration Office's concern. The two are separate.”

Qinglan held the jade and remembered the string that had hung at his father's waist. Twenty-seven pieces. On the day of the tile-breaking sentence, every one had been smashed on Wangjiang Teahouse's stone floor.

Only as dusk fell did the Rain Pavilion's crowd begin to disperse.

Xiao Cheng'an wrote a notice, stamped it with the witness's copper seal and sent a waiter to the Bureau of Balance's northern branch that night. The waiter returned empty-handed within the time it took to eat a meal. Only two clerks were there, unable to handle a tile-trickery case. The branch had received word from Tiandu days earlier: a Captain of the Bureau was inspecting a neighbouring county and would soon pass through Yunchuan. They had sent someone that night to notify him. He would arrive early the day after tomorrow. Until then, the place that had detained the suspect must hold him.

The Rain Pavilion's proprietor sighed. He assigned his two strongest waiters to flank Zheng Shouzhuo and cleared the rear storeroom for him, setting a lamp outside its door. Whoever caught a thief must guard him until officials arrived. That was an old rule of Dayu.

Another notice went to the Arbitration Office's eastern branch. Someone had to escort the evidence away: two Red Dragons, Hengchang's tea tray, the medicine and Yuening's ledger. But the branch had locked up at nightfall. The porter accepted the notice and said they would answer in the morning.

“Then we won't seal it tonight.” Yuening put away her ledger. “Once the box is sealed, even a look inside would require breaking it. We'll leave it unsealed, watched by Master Xiao and me. Every use gets an entry. Tomorrow, when the branch replies, we'll seal it together.” She paused. “An evidence courier handles only the objects. The prisoner must still wait for the Bureau of Balance.”

Qian Youqing crouched, collecting his beads one by one. After finding more than twenty, he was still several short. At last he slunk away with his servants. At the door, he glanced back at Qinglan. There was no hatred in that look. Only fear.

Tao Lingtao brought a brocade pouch, the edge of a Dipper gold coin showing at its mouth. Qinglan pushed it back. “Keep it for Miss Luo's hand. Besides, I should have shouted before she took the tray. I was too late.”

Tao Lingtao did not insist. Instead she looked at the robe he wore.

Rain had darkened its deep green. A few drops of blood stained the fresh white lapels. The red cord was broken in two, one end still hanging, the other lost somewhere. Yet the jade pendant, despite striking the table edge, had not suffered even a hairline crack.

Just then two young servants in short jackets appeared at the door, calling as they entered, “Proprietor Tao, where is our young master's robe? Our master said it would be delivered after today's match!”

Tao Lingtao stepped between them and Qinglan and gave them a bow. “Tell Master Wan the Tao family will make a new one and deliver it within half a month, free of charge. As for this one—” She glanced back at Qinglan. “It has sat at the table for us. It stays with the man who took our seat.”

The servants began to object, then looked at the still-wet red seal on the table and the blood on the robe. They scratched their heads and left.

An embroiderer squeezed down the stairs: pink top, pink skirt, a white apron over them, a small flower in her side bun. She was only eighteen or nineteen, still slender with youth. Her apron strings knotted behind her waist drew it fine; as she descended, her skirt swung gently to reveal a pink shoe tip, then hid it again. Tao Lingtao called her Xingyu. Blushing, she crouched beside Qinglan and pulled a length of red hair cord from beneath her bun. Half her hair loosened, held only precariously by the little flower, but she paid it no mind. Bending her head, she used the cord to join the robe's broken ties, made a firm knot and leaned in to bite off the surplus thread.

The fingertips holding the thread were covered in needle pricks.

The join made a little lump. The red cord hung down again, its tassel still attached.

“It isn't a pretty mend,” she whispered.

Then she pressed a plain ivory silk handkerchief into his palm, folded into a small square. Unfolded, it showed a sprig of blue-green bamboo embroidered in one corner. The joints were crooked; two stitches had been skipped, and loose thread remained on the back.

“I spoiled this one too.”

Qinglan looked at the crooked bamboo. “It isn't spoiled.”

Xingyu flushed to the tips of her ears. Clutching her apron, she stood and hurried away, nearly colliding with a waiter collecting bowls at the foot of the stairs. Two steps up, she glanced back—not at him, but at the little knot at his waist.

Once everyone had gone, the waiters took brooms to the broken bowls strewn across the floor.

Passing the counter, Qinglan kicked something. He looked down. A copper coin lay at the counter's foot, tea staining half its surface.

His own. The last coin he had placed on the tile box beneath the shrine of the God of Mahjong the night before. When Hu Shan overturned the table and shook the hall, it had been jolted from the lid.

He bent to pick it up.

Click.

The sound came from beneath the shrine. Soft, as though a fingertip had gently pushed over a tile, or the tile-box lid had lifted a fraction and fallen back.

Qinglan's hand stopped in midair.

The White Dragon was inside his robe; the ten-year-old Red Dragon was in his sleeve. Two Heavenly Tiles, passing right before the old table.

The lamp flame dipped. In that blink of time, he saw a grey-white fingertip resting at the box's opening, half transparent, like smoke made solid, slowly drawing back inside.

A waiter holding a broom hunched his shoulders and muttered, “At it again.” He saw nothing. He had only heard.

“That is a passage from one of my stories,” someone said behind him. “For another day.”

Xiao Cheng'an stood behind him with his stick, not looking at the old table, and yet seeming to have watched it all along. With the ribs of his closed fan he nudged the coin to his feet, bent to pick it up, and set it squarely in the centre of the box lid. Neat and practised, as though he had done it many times.

No further sound came from beneath the shrine.

“You can hear it too, Master Xiao?” Qinglan asked.

“A storyteller's ears serve him better than his eyes.” The old man straightened and tapped his fan against his palm. “The year they moved this table from the middle of the hall, I still told my stories downstairs. My voice carried better then.”

He said no more, turning towards the stairs. Watching his back, Qinglan remembered the hand holding a fan at the third watch the night before, its ribs laid across the eastern hand.

He put the thought aside. Every storyteller had a fan. Storytellers all over the world held them that way.

A furry hand settled on his arm.

“Young master,” said Zhumo, “a word upstairs, if you please.”

The panda carried his half-man-high bamboo basket, his eyes never leaving Qinglan's left sleeve. The old Red Dragon lay inside it.

The upstairs storytelling room was empty. Xiao Cheng'an closed the door. Yuening set the inverted copper basin on the long table by the entrance, the unbranded Red Dragon beneath it, then moved the oil lamp to the centre of the main table.

Zhumo took off his basket and drew a yellowed scroll from its very bottom. Its red cord had faded almost to white. He tried several times before undoing it.

Tiandu. Wangjiang Teahouse. Sworn match of honour.

Matter at issue: Tiandu's mahjong tax.

East: Qing Yuanshan, Tuidao School. South: Hechen, Red Dragon Society.

West: Luo Hanzhang, Pinghe Hall. North: Xuanshu, Revenue Directorate.

“I copied this scroll,” the panda said. “I was the chronicler of that match.”

Qinglan's gaze settled on the name Xuanshu. He remembered the man: blue-grey official robes, long, clean white fingers, an unhurried voice. When his father laid down a self-drawn winning hand and the hall cheered, that man had clapped twice too. Then he rose, smiling, and turned over the section of wall before his father.

Zhumo unrolled the scroll to its end. After the game record was a carefully drawn Red Dragon, with a line of small writing beside it:

Evidence: one extra Red Dragon found in East's wall. A chip the size of a rice grain beneath the symbol's final vertical stroke. A small hand engraved at the lower right of the back, matching the engraving on the White Dragon declared by East. Delivered to the Tiandu Mahjong Arbitration Office for sealing and safekeeping.

Qinglan laid the old Red Dragon beside the drawing, then took the two half-finger coins from his sleeve and set them side by side beneath the lamp.

The rice-grain chip matched exactly in the lamplight.

The picture included a rubbing of the back: a small open hand at the lower right, five fingers spread. Qinglan did not need to turn his own tile over. His White Dragon had the same little hand in the same place. Ten years earlier, everyone in the hall had seen those two hands and concluded that Qing Yuanshan himself was Ghost Hand.

“Something sealed away,” Yuening said tightly, “has seen daylight again today.”

No one answered.

Yuening leaned over the game record. She bent so low that the flowers in her twin buns almost brushed the paper. A curve of startlingly white nape showed above her crossed collar, edged warm yellow by the lamp. Behind her, Qinglan caught the faint soap-pod fragrance in her hair and stepped half a pace back. She read the record faster than a book, her finger sliding line by line. At the seventh turn, the paper was a little thicker, as though lined with an extra layer. Zhumo pressed a paw there briefly, then moved it away. Yuening's finger continued without stopping until it reached the ninth turn.

“Third turn: South discards Two of Dots. Sixth turn: West discards Two of Dots. East calls pung, exposing three. Ninth turn: North discards another Two of Dots.” She looked up. “One, three, one. Five Twos of Dots.”

“I saw it then too,” Zhumo said softly.

“Leave then aside for now.” Yuening pressed a finger to the record. “The final count was one hundred and thirty-seven tiles, correct? The extra tile was the Red Dragon in the wall.”

“Yes.”

“Then something is wrong,” she said. “An extra Two of Dots and an extra Red Dragon, but only one extra tile in the count. The Red Dragon was added. The Two of Dots was exchanged for something else, keeping the number unchanged.” She looked at Zhumo. “Which tile is missing from the record?”

Zhumo stared. “I don't know. I recorded only the tiles played, not those kept concealed.”

“To find it, we need the complete count of the set before play began that day.” Yuening slowly straightened, the curve of her back unfolding, the green trim of her dress settling in a soft line along her body. “The scorekeeper's count.”

Her gaze returned to the end of the scroll. After Zhumo's signature was another small line in elegant handwriting, as though someone else had added it later:

This hand is suspect.

Scorekeeper: Shen Suyun

Yuening's fingertip stopped on the word “suspect” and began to tremble.

“That is my mother's writing.”

She did not cry. She pressed the paper's corner flat, but could not bring herself to move her hand. “After Mother came home that day, she spent night after night at the lamp with her abacus. Then one day she said she was taking a document to the Arbitration Office. She would be back before supper.”

She paused.

“She never came back.”

The storytelling room was so quiet they could hear the waiters collecting bowls downstairs.

But Qinglan was looking at the beginning of the scroll. Beneath the four seats should have followed the opening rules, searches and tile count. A section was missing. A neat cut remained, as though a narrow strip had been sliced away with a knife.

“What was written here?”

Zhumo pressed a paw to the cut. After a long silence, he said only:

“Wait until night.”

Three knocks sounded at the door.

The young head cook, Pei Zhiwei, entered, a cloth wrapped about his head and a deep-blue apron at his waist. He carried a large tray: shrimp dumplings, turnip cake, a bowl of chicken soup with red dates, and a basket of freshly steamed bamboo-shoot buns.

“Proprietor Tao asked me to make these.” He set out the chopsticks and glanced at Qinglan. “A night in the woodshed, then a soaking in the rear alley. You need something hot.”

Zhumo's nose twitched. He was first to reach for the food.

Pei Zhiwei gathered his tray, then took a black-lacquered wooden tag, half a palm in size, from his apron pocket. A cord hole pierced the top. He laid it on the table. “Hu Shan changed clothes in the woodshed before playing and left this behind. The proprietor asked me to give it to the scorekeeper.”

An open hand was cut into the front, all five fingers spread, with a line of old gold paint beside it. On the back was one name: Nanchao.

“Nanchao.” Xiao Cheng'an frowned. “Pinghe Hall's headquarters are there.”

“So are next year's Tianyuan Mahjong Assembly qualifiers.” Yuening's abacus clicked. “From today, there are two hundred and ninety-seven days until the Hall of the Mahjong God opens. Nine pieces of Mahjong Jade required. You have one.”

Qinglan looked at the wooden tag.

“I'm going,” he said. “To clear my father's name.”

Ten years. This was the first time he had spoken those words aloud.

“You can't even move one thumb.” Yuening glared at him. “Who will keep your score at the Assembly? Who will count the Red Dragons on your table?” She tucked the abacus into her robe. “I'm going too.”

The rain stopped in the second watch. When the Dipper climbed above the eaves, Zhumo opened the storytelling-room window.

“Heavenly Tiles are most tractable beneath the Dipper,” the panda said. “If you want one to speak, this is the time.”

There was no copper boundary in this room. No sworn matches were played at this table. No copper was set beneath it, nor any copper coin laid on top.

Qinglan unfolded the red cloth and placed the White Dragon on the table. The blue-green line on its back was scarcely more than the shadow of a hair.

“You used it once this afternoon.” Zhumo watched the line. “Use it again, and you fill the gap with your own breath.”

Yuening leaned closer. “Was that line engraved on the tile to begin with?”

“It is his.” Zhumo's claw hovered over the line without touching it. “A Heavenly Tile shows not itself, but its bearer. When the breath a bearer gathers forms a strand, a mark appears on the back. Its shape depends on the affinity that person was born with. Those whose thoughts settle on people belong to Characters: each mark is a number, One or Two, like the numeral on a Character tile. Those drawn to plants and their connections belong to Bamboo: one stalk per strand. Those drawn to the workings of heaven and to objects belong to Dots: one circle per strand.”

He paused, then qualified it. “Ordinary Heavenly Tiles work that way.”

He glanced at Qinglan. “Yours is upright, thin as a bamboo stalk. Bamboo affinity. When your father presented this tile during the search, it showed bamboo on its back too.”

“And now?” Yuening asked.

“One stalk, not fully grown,” said Zhumo. “Burn it away, and there will be none.”

“I know,” said Qinglan.

Starlight entered through the window.

Unlike lamplight, which shone on things, this light seemed to have weight. A film of water, impossibly thin, overflowed the sill, ran slowly across the table to the White Dragon, circled halfway round it and seeped inside thread by thread. Nourished by it, the hair-fine blue-green line brightened.

Yuening could not see that layer of light. She saw only Qinglan staring unblinkingly at the table. She opened her ledger and raised her brush.

“I have a rule. Say what you see. One sentence from you, one entry from me. I won't ask what you think it means. Don't guess for me either.”

“All right.”

Xiao Cheng'an sat by the door, his closed fan on his knee.

Qinglan picked up the old Red Dragon and touched its edge to the White Dragon.

Cold flooded from his palm up his arm. This time it did not overwhelm him. He clenched his teeth and let himself sink into it, as though descending a well.

A square wooden table. A dark-blue long robe. A hand reaching for a tile box, callused at the knuckles.

“East. Right hand reaching for the tile box,” he said. Yuening's brush whispered across the paper.

A blue-grey cuff pressed in from the side, blocking that hand. The Red Dragon inside the sleeve struck the box's edge.

Click.

“A sleeve from the north. Blue-grey. Red Dragon strikes the box edge. Chip.”

He had seen all this in the afternoon. He forced himself to look away from the cuff, towards other things, things he had had no time to see before.

His view was from his father's chest. He could not see his father's face, only his hands.

At the instant the sleeve pressed in, his father's left hand pressed against his own chest.

Hard.

As that hand pressed down, Qinglan felt cold pour towards him from ten years ago. Another man's cold, crossing ten years to overlap the chill in his own chest.

He knew that feeling. On the seventh turn that afternoon, he too had been so cold he wanted to press his chest, but dared not move.

“East. Left hand against his chest.” His voice rasped. “He was cold too.”

Yuening's brush paused, then resumed.

Qinglan wanted to see further. After that cuff withdrew, what had his father's hand done? Where had the Red Dragon gone? He pushed with all his strength into the cold.

The scene would not move on.

It whitened, dissolving at the edges like paper soaked to pulp, unable to hold anything. In the whiteness remained only that click, again and again.

The starlight on the table seemed to be drained in one gulp. It rushed back to the sill and out of the window.

Someone was pulling his arm.

“Let go!” Zhumo's voice. “Young Master Qing, let go!”

Qinglan opened his fingers. The old Red Dragon fell onto the table and rolled halfway over.

He heard something dripping onto the ledger. He looked down. His own nosebleed, drop after drop, blurring Yuening's freshly written “He was cold too” into red.

His right arm was numb from fingertips to shoulder. He could not move a single finger.

“The White Dragon.” Xiao Cheng'an suddenly stood.

The hair-fine blue-green line was gone from the White Dragon's back. The surface was bare, leaving only the little engraved hand and a few indistinct dots beneath its heel.

Like the most ordinary bone tile.

Yuening turned pale. “Is it… broken?”

“No.” Zhumo moved it beneath the window, facing the Dipper. “It is as though someone shut a door on this tile, leaving only a crack. One key opens one door. He insisted on looking beyond it and burned through his own strand of breath.” The panda's voice softened. “Leave it under the Dipper overnight. Some may return. Or it may not.”

No one spoke again.

Yuening lowered her head and wrote another line beside the bloodstain, her hand perfectly steady:

“Vision ends at the chip. Nothing beyond it visible. What was seen awaits corroboration; it is not evidence.”

She laid down the brush, took out her own handkerchief and pressed it beneath his nose. Hard, as though pinning down a ledger entry that might escape.

“Tilt your head back,” she said.

He did. She kept her hand there until the bleeding stopped, then removed it. The bloodied handkerchief remained clenched in her palm. She neither gave it to him nor threw it away.

Late into the night, Yuening fell asleep beside the table, the ebony abacus still beneath her hand. Her cheek rested on her forearm. Her lashes cast a small shadow beneath her eyes; her lips were parted a little, her breathing even and soft. By the door, Xiao Cheng'an snored like a broken bellows.

The night grew cold. Her pale yellow dress had been soaked in the alley, and its back was still damp. She drew in her shoulders in her sleep. The crossed collar loosened a finger's width and slipped aside, exposing a slender mauve undergarment tie below her collarbone.

Qinglan looked only once, and heat rushed to his ears. He turned away so quickly that his chair leg scraped the floor.

She did not wake.

An old felt blanket lay folded in the corner, used by storytellers to cover their knees in winter. With his still-working left hand, he shook it out and, face averted, laid it over her shoulders without touching a single hair.

He straightened and should have left, but lingered another breath. Asleep, she looked different. The determined crease between her brows had eased. Each warm breath brushed the back of her own hand. Suddenly he wondered how that breath would feel on someone else's hand. The thought startled him, and he quickly turned his face away.

The oil lamp stood too close to her face. Its flame flickered, lighting her eyelids. He moved it to the other end of the table, shielding the flame with the back of his hand so the light would not disturb her.

Her lips moved in her sleep. Indistinctly, she murmured, “One hundred and thirty-six.”

Qinglan stood beside the table a while before returning to the window.

He could not sleep. He sat beneath the window, watching the White Dragon.

In the third watch, the Dipper had moved halfway past the roof corner. Starlight overflowed the sill again, this time slowly, like bamboo roots searching the earth for water. Finding the White Dragon, it drew inside thread by thread. Beneath the heel of the little engraved hand, a blue-green line finer than spider silk slowly emerged.

He let out a long breath.

“It's back.” At some point Zhumo had sat beside him. “Thinner than this afternoon. From now on, use it sparingly.”

Qinglan nodded. His fingers were still numb, but now they could bend a little.

The panda did not continue. He sat holding a cold tea bowl for a long time, then reached to the very bottom of his basket and took out a small, flat cloth pouch. Its mouth had been sewn shut; the thread had blackened. He bit through it and tipped out a narrow strip of paper.

The very strip cut from the beginning of the scroll.

“I cut it,” said Zhumo. “That night ten years ago, at the inn. With my own knife.”

He spread it on Qinglan's knee. His handwriting from ten years earlier lay square and orderly on the paper:

Opening search: East, Qing Yuanshan, one White Dragon inside robe, declared as a Heavenly Tile, excluded from tile count, permitted by match supervisor. North, Xuanshu, inside robe—

Nothing followed. Beneath “inside robe” lay a streak of ink where the brush had stopped. Another piece of paper had once continued below the ink. Now only a ragged tear remained, with half a vermilion seal straddling its edge.

Qinglan stared at the two lines.

“East. One White Dragon inside robe. Declared as a Heavenly Tile. Excluded from tile count.” He read it softly. “Father declared his White Dragon before play began.”

The rule Xiao Cheng'an had read to the hall today—objects inside one's robe must be declared—his father had obeyed ten years ago. Obeyed it, and still been accused of carrying the hundred and thirty-seventh tile.

“He declared it,” said Zhumo. “The supervisor allowed it. I recorded it. That tile was never part of the hundred and thirty-six.”

“And North? What was inside his robe?”

“North refused the search,” said Zhumo. “He sat down and said only that what he carried was not to be shown. When the supervisor insisted, someone stood among the northern party and produced an order from the Scorekeepers' Institute permitting him to play. My brush stopped at ‘inside robe’. I wrote no further.”

“What was the torn-off piece?”

“The match supervisor's sealed endorsement,” said Zhumo. “After the match, Supervisor Gan added several lines beneath my entry, folded them over and sealed the join. A chronicler must preserve a supervisor's sealed endorsement, never open it. I never did. I never saw a word of what it said.”

“Then why cut it away?”

“That night, while I was making the fair copy at the inn, someone came.” The panda's voice grew hoarse. “He wanted one sentence left out of the fair copy. As for this opening strip, either it must say, ‘North searched; no concealed objects,’ or it must not be there.”

“Which sentence did he want left out?”

Zhumo did not answer that. His paw pressed against his knee, as though holding something down.

“I would not alter it. A chronicler's hand does not write lies,” he said. “So before his eyes, I cut away the whole strip. He tore off the sealed endorsement beneath it, put it into his robe without even looking, and laid something on my table. Hush money, he called it.”

Zhumo extended his paw and placed a copper coin beside the paper.

A thin mark had been filed into its edge, like half a finger.

Exactly like the two on the table.

Blood rushed to Qinglan's head. “Who was he? What did he look like?”

Zhumo opened his mouth.

At that moment, cold touched Qinglan's heart.

The White Dragon beneath the window, its blue-green line only just restored, glazed over with frost. Frost crept from the tile onto the table, towards the sill, thin and brittle, like the first crack across a winter river.

The inverted copper basin on the long table by the door did not stir. It was not the tile beneath it.

The thing was outside the window.

On the eaves directly above their heads.

(End of Chapter Two)
