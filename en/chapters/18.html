Chapter 18Stitches

Early the next morning, two things lay on the inn’s breakfast table: a pair of black leather gloves and an ink rubbing.

The gloves belonged to the man in the woven hat. Lu Zhaoye had torn one off on the night of Nanchao’s great tide. The other had been left on the challenge platform when the man escaped. Inside the right glove, a tiny “Star” was embroidered in silver thread.

The rubbing showed the seven white Dipper marks on the back of the White Dragon, the one Zhumo had taken at the old memorial arch and Yuening had handed to Qinglan after the great tide. The tile itself was back inside Qinglan’s robe. By daylight it was only an old bone tile; to show it to anyone, he still needed the rubbing.

“We split up,” Qinglan said. “Yuening and Zhumo, go to the Scorekeepers’ Institute and look into Scorekeeper Shen’s old files. Lu Zhaoye and I will go to the Starwatch Pavilion.”

“Why the Starwatch Pavilion?” Jinshi asked through a mouthful of bun.

“The rubbing shows the Dipper.” Lu Zhaoye picked up the right glove and pointed to the silver character in its lining. “And in here, they stitched a star.”

Qinglan tucked the rubbing back inside his robe. Yuening watched him put it away, her hand settling on the small ebony abacus at her waist.

“You have your father’s White Dragon,” she said. “I have my mother’s abacus. We mustn’t lose either.”

Qinglan nodded. “Five this evening. Whatever we find, we come back here.”

Jinshi swallowed his bun and looked from one to the other. “What about me?”

“You’re with me,” Lu Zhaoye said. “My chest hasn’t healed. I’ll need carrying up the stairs.”

◆

The Scorekeepers’ Institute stood in the west of the city, a large compound of blue-grey brick. No xiezhi. No Guards of Balance. Only a black plaque with white lettering above the entrance.

Before they went in, they heard abacuses.

Not one. Dozens, hundreds, rattling from row upon row of side rooms like a relentless downpour. Yuening stood at the entrance and listened for a long time. This was the sound she had heard as a child, falling asleep against her mother’s table.

A young clerk received them. Seventeen or eighteen, tall and thin, sleeves and fingers covered in ink. He spoke as fast as an abacus clicked.

“Scorekeeper Shen Suyun’s file?” His eyes lit up. “I’m Youhuang. I’ve worked in the archives for two years. Everyone here knows about Scorekeeper Shen! Ten years ago, she checked over three hundred hands in a single year. Never got one tile wrong. On their first day, every new clerk has to memorise her Ten Rules for Checking Tiles.”

Walking, he recited them on his fingers. “First rule: never let the tiles out of your sight. Second: never leave a count until tomorrow. Third: better one point wrong than one tile…” At the fifth rule, something struck him. He looked back at Yuening. “Is your family name Shen too, miss?”

“Yes.”

“Oh,” Youhuang said. He asked nothing more, but slowed his steps and stayed at Yuening’s side, as though afraid she might lose her way among the rows of rooms.

He led Yuening and Zhumo through three courtyards into a tall, dark archive room. Row upon row of wooden shelves filled it, stacked with bundles of game records and account books. The air smelled of old paper and camphor wood.

Zhumo stood at the threshold a moment before stepping inside.

“Ten years ago, it was in this room that I handed over the record of that match,” he said quietly. “Afterwards, I stood outside for a long time, wanting to go back and change one word. In the end, I didn’t.”

Yuening turned to him. But he had already lowered his head and moved behind a row of shelves, as though unwilling to let anyone see his face.

Youhuang brought a ladder, climbed to the highest shelf and searched for some time before coming down with a dusty wooden box.

“Ten years ago. Tiandu. Wangjiang Teahouse. Sworn match of honour.” He read the label. “Scorekeeper: Shen Suyun.”

Yuening opened the box.

Inside lay a thin ledger with Mother’s name on the cover. She turned page after page: who drew each turn, who discarded what, who punged or konged, how the scores were calculated. On the last page, in the end-of-hand count, stood several careful words:

Tile count: one hundred and thirty-six. Verified. Scorekeeper: Shen Suyun

Behind her, Zhumo drew a small, sharp breath.

Yuening said nothing. She pressed a finger to the “Yun” in the name.

The writing was alike. Every stroke, so alike even Zhumo could not distinguish it. But whenever Mother wrote Yun, the final falling stroke flicked lightly towards the upper right, like an abacus bead sent upwards. This one finished flat.

“That stroke isn’t how Mother usually wrote it. We have to find out first who swapped this book,” she said.

Youhuang stared. “But it’s an official file. It bears the Institute’s seal—”

“Official files are written by people too.”

A voice suddenly came from the archive doorway. “The access register has no record of this file being checked out in ten years.”

A woman official in her forties stood there. Her blue-green robe was washed almost white, her hair pinned without a strand out of place. Her face was gentle, her voice soft, as though afraid of waking someone in the room.

“Madam Tang.” Youhuang hastily straightened.

The woman came in, glanced at Yuening, then at the ledger. She reached out, closed it and returned it to the box.

“Do you wish to check tiles for someone?” she asked gently. “I have a doubt in an old record I’d like you to examine.”

She drew a record from her sleeve and laid it before Yuening. “There’s one error in this hand’s record. Find it, and I’ll ask you to discuss something from the past.”

Yuening looked down at the record.

A Tuidao School hand. Four players. Fourteen turns. She began at the first turn, her finger tapping softly at the table’s edge. Once. Twice. Three times. At the ninth turn, it stopped.

“Two of Characters,” she said. “On the third turn, East discarded one. West punged it, exposing three. On the sixth turn, South discarded another. All four Twos of Characters had appeared. But on the ninth turn, the record says North punged Twos of Characters.”

“That pung would take a fifth Two of Characters.” She looked up. “Whether it was copied wrong or there really was an extra tile on the table, we’ll only know by checking the original record.”

Tangxi watched her fingers for a long time.

She slipped the record back into her sleeve, lifted the wooden box and turned to leave. At the doorway, she stopped with her back to Yuening and spoke as though to herself.

“The last stroke in Scorekeeper Shen’s name flicked upwards.”

Yuening’s head snapped up.

“I finish work at five today.” Tangxi’s voice remained soft. “The Qu family tailor shop, in the south of the city. I have an old official robe there for alteration.”

She left. In the archive room remained only shelf upon shelf of old paper, and Youhuang with his mouth hanging open.

◆

The Starwatch Pavilion had nine storeys. The highest stood thirty feet above the city wall.

By the seventh floor, Jinshi was panting under Lu Zhaoye’s weight. At the ninth, he set him down and collapsed beside him. “This—this is higher than the plum-blossom posts!”

An open terrace crowned the tower, the bronze instrument mounted at its centre. Rings nested within rings, taller than a man. Someone in a deep-blue robe stood with their back to them, bent over one of the rings. Gold-thread stars covered the robe in clusters, as though the wearer had put on the night sky.

Lu Zhaoye’s hand flew to the short blade behind his waist.

He had seen too many stars on the way up. “Star” on the Pavilion’s plaque. Stars carved into the stair rail. Even a bronze star on the young doorkeeper’s cap. His palm was wet around the knife hilt.

The figure turned. A woman, a little over thirty, with a lean face and bright eyes, as though accustomed to watching stars at night. She glanced at Lu Zhaoye’s hand on his blade, then at Qinglan, entirely untroubled.

“Pei Xingheng, starwatcher of the Starwatch Pavilion,” she said. “Few people come to look at stars by daylight.”

Qinglan spread the rubbing on the stone stand beneath the instrument.

Pei Xingheng glanced down. Her expression changed. She bent until her face almost touched the rubbing and went over it dot by dot before straightening.

“Seven dots like the Dipper,” she said. “The rubbing also holds fine lines from the tile’s back. It may not be a casual drawing of the sky. But seven dots alone can’t tell us where, or on which night, someone looked at the sky.”

“What do you mean?”

Pei Xingheng pointed overhead. The sky was pale. Not a star could be seen.

“Stars are in the sky. To recognize a place, people need other references,” she said.

She brought an old star chart and showed Qinglan. Beside a northern ridge were seven dots too, with half an old palm-like mark beneath them, but no Hall name beside it. The shapes were similar, but there were plenty of gaps too.

“Beneath the Dipper,” Qinglan said softly.

“You need to find the maker, or the stretch of road they left unfinished.” Pei Xingheng pushed the old chart towards Qinglan.

“The Hall of the Mahjong God,” Qinglan said.

Jinshi scratched his head beside them. “Right underneath the Dipper? How cold would that be?”

“Cold.” To their surprise, Pei Xingheng nodded in earnest. “The world’s farthest north is snow-covered mountains. On the Pavilion’s old star charts, there is only the outline of a mountain there. Nothing is written on it.”

“Not a single word?”

“The chart’s maker probably never went up either,” Pei Xingheng said. “Nothing recorded on the chart doesn’t mean no one ever went.”

Pei Xingheng never took up the name of the Hall. She only looked at Qinglan. “What did you take this rubbing from?”

“The back of a White Dragon.”

“Where is it?”

“On me. By daylight, it’s just an old bone tile.”

After a silence, Pei Xingheng said, “A little over ten days ago, someone else came asking with a similarly shaped rubbing.”

The wind across the terrace suddenly strengthened.

“Who?” Lu Zhaoye asked.

“He gave no name,” Pei Xingheng said. “He wore gloves.”

“What did he ask?”

“The same things as you.” Pei Xingheng looked at Qinglan. “But one more question. On the mountain directly beneath the Dipper, which path could he climb at night and keep the Dipper in sight?”

“Did you tell him?”

“I told him I didn’t know,” Pei Xingheng said. “It was the truth.”

Lu Zhaoye pulled the right glove from inside his robe and slapped it onto the stone stand. “This pair?”

Pei Xingheng picked it up, turned it over and examined the silver-thread “Star” inside.

She looked at that character for a long time and did not say whom it brought to mind. She only shook her head.

“Starwatch Pavilion members embroider stars in gold thread—points of stars, never written characters,” she said. “This is a character in silver, return stitches looped through one another, so dense you can’t see the ends. I’ve worn Qu clothes and seen similar work. Ask her to identify it first.”

She handed the glove back. “In the south of the city. The Qu family tailor shop.”

◆

The Qu family tailor shop occupied a small frontage in a narrow southern alley. Strings of cloth samples hung outside, fluttering in a riot of colour whenever the wind blew.

When Qinglan’s group arrived, it was just five in the evening. Yuening and Zhumo already stood at the door. The two groups met eyes and knew without speaking that both paths had reached the same place.

Qinglan said only one thing. “A little over ten days ago, someone took a similar rubbing to the Starwatch Pavilion.” Yuening’s face changed. “Ours has been inside your robe the whole time.”

The shop was crammed with clothes. Stage costumes hung to the left, embroidered with phoenixes and clouds, their sleeves trailing to the floor. To the right, official robes hung in straight rows, deep blue-green xiezhi robes among them. At a large cutting table in the middle, a woman in her thirties cut cloth with a row of needles held between her lips. Snip, snip went her scissors. She did not look up.

“Three days for measurements. Half a month to make the clothes. No rush orders,” she said around the needles.

Jinshi was dazzled by the costumes as soon as he entered. He reached for a robe embroidered with a golden dragon. Without looking up, Qu Manrong said, “One copper coin per touch.”

Jinshi snatched his hand back.

Only then did she glance up and look him over. “A long robe for a man your size would take three feet more cloth than anyone else’s.”

“What of it?”

“Three feet would dress a young actor from head to toe.”

Lu Zhaoye laid the gloves on the table.

The scissors stopped.

The woman took the needles from her mouth, lifted the right glove and turned it over. One glance at the “Star” in its lining, and she put it down.

“My stitches,” she said. “Qu Manrong. This is my shop.”

“Who ordered them?” Lu Zhaoye’s voice was tight.

“The Qu shop’s rule.” Qu Manrong picked up her scissors again. “Only the clothes know whose they are.”

“I tore this glove from that man’s hand. He admitted the fire. Of eleven in the Lu family, only I remain!”

The scissors paused again.

Qu Manrong looked up at Lu Zhaoye. A long look. Then she lowered her head and resumed cutting.

“I’ll say one thing,” she said. “The right glove is half an inch larger than the left. Those were the measurements when they were ordered.”

“The size may have been altered after burns,” Qinglan said.

Qu Manrong said nothing. Her scissors snipped through a loose thread.

She set them down, turned to Yuening and took a folded costume sleeve from beneath the table. A fine border of abacus beads was embroidered around its cuff. The costume Shen Yansheng had worn onstage.

“Master Shen brought this in this morning to have the cuff altered,” she said. “She said that if a young woman surnamed Shen ever came looking for me, I was to pass on a message for her. Don’t go back to Wansheng Theatre.”

Yuening clenched her hands. “What else did she say?”

“Only that,” Qu Manrong said. “I make clothes. Passing messages is incidental. I carry words for both sides. I ask neither side about its business.”

The door curtain lifted. Tangxi came in.

She had changed out of her official robe into plain clothes and looked thinner still. She nodded to Qu Manrong. Without a question, Qu Manrong went into the back room and returned with a neatly folded old official robe. The blue-green robe of the Scorekeepers’ Institute. Its collar worn white, its cuffs patched twice.

“Ten years,” Qu Manrong said. “Every year Madam Tang brings it to be aired and mended. I’ve never asked why.”

“Why keep it here?” Qinglan asked.

“The Bureau of Balance wears Qu clothes. So do the theatre troupes. So does the Scorekeepers’ Institute,” Tangxi said. “Anyone in Tiandu might turn the pages of a ledger. But no one thinks to turn out the lining of an old official robe.”

She took the robe, picked up a small pair of scissors from Qu Manrong’s sewing basket and unpicked the lining, stitch by stitch.

A thin booklet was sewn inside.

“After the old case, the night before your mother left Tiandu, she came to find me at the Scorekeepers’ Institute.” Tangxi took out the book and held it. “She said the official file would be replaced. This one, she asked me to keep. When her daughter came, I was to give it to her.”

“I asked, what if your daughter never comes?”

Tangxi placed the booklet in Yuening’s hands.

“She said, ‘She will. Wherever she has counted to, I’ll count from there again.’”

Yuening’s tears fell at once.

For ten years, she had said those words to the rafters more times than she could count. She had always thought they were hers, spoken to her mother. Her mother had said them first. To her.

“That night, she sat opposite me,” Tangxi said very quietly. “As she spoke, her ring finger tapped the table’s edge. Once. Twice. Three times. We’d worked as scorekeepers in the same room for six years. I had never seen her so frightened. But her hand never faltered once.”

Yuening’s hands shook so badly she could scarcely hold the booklet.

“The next day, the official file I saw was no longer this one,” Tangxi said. “Who replaced it, I’ve investigated ten years without finding. Access to the archive has to pass the Institute’s guards and seals. There must be handlers inside who need investigating. So I dared tell no one, and dared leave even the clothes only with the Qu family.”

Yuening opened the booklet.

Her mother’s writing. Every final stroke of “Yun” flicked lightly upwards.

She turned the pages one by one, very slowly. On the last, in the space for the final tile count, her mother’s writing was heavier than anywhere else. Every stroke seemed carved into the paper:

Tile count: one hundred and thirty-seven.

Below it was another line in smaller writing, the ink paler, as though added later:

Before the extra tile entered the wall, it passed through the Scorekeepers’ Institute.

The shop went still. By the door, a string of fabric swatches fluttered in the breeze. No one made a sound.

Yuening looked up at Tangxi. The woman’s face had gone white. Her lips pressed tightly together.

“Ten years,” Tangxi said softly. “I thought I was hiding the wrong done to your mother. All this time, I was hiding the Institute’s own ghost.”

At the door, Zhumo gripped his brush until its shaft creaked.

Qinglan looked at that small line of writing for a long time without speaking. Ten years ago, everyone had taken the Red Dragon found in his father’s wall as irrefutable proof. But no one had asked where that tile came from. Whose hands it had passed through before entering the wall.

Only one person had asked. After asking, she had never come home.

Yuening closed the booklet and hugged it tightly to her chest.

“Madam Tang,” she said. “Thank you. For all these ten years.”

Tangxi shook her head, looking at her. At last her eyes reddened. “You look like her. And your hand, when you count tiles. That too.”

◆

That night, after they had gone, Qu Manrong shut her shop and lit a small lamp.

She took a newly finished set of xiezhi robes from a rack. Deep blue-green, with the single-horned creature embroidered in silver thread on chest and back. Early tomorrow, it would go to the Bureau of Balance.

She picked up her needle and sewed a slip of paper, folded to the size of a fingernail, inside the collar.

The stitches were so close no thread ends showed.

She bit through the thread, folded the robe and blew out the lamp.

Only the clothes knew whose they were.
