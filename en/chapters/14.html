Chapter 14The Challenge Platform

Pinghe Hall held its qualifiers in the square by the docks, not inside the hall.

A challenge platform stood in the middle of the square, three feet high and forty feet on each side. Red felt covered it, with four mahjong tables set on top. At each corner stood a great flag embroidered with the name Pinghe, snapping in the sea wind.

A sea of people crowded below. Every second- and third-floor window of the arcaded buildings stood open, heads packed along the sills. Even the masts of the moored ships were full of children who had climbed up to watch.

“Pinghe Hall’s custom. Once every twelve years.” Pei Zhiwei stood beside Qinglan, speaking quietly. “They have four places at the Tianyuan Mahjong Assembly. Whoever wins a place by holding a seat can register without the Jade requirement. Their own players defend those places. Anyone who objects can go up and play. Pick a seat. Play one hand. Win, and the place is yours. Lose, and step down. Whoever holds those four tables at the end of three days represents Pinghe Hall at the Assembly.”

“Anyone can challenge?”

“Anyone can challenge,” Pei Zhiwei said. “That’s how Pinghe Hall earned its name. In thirty years, it has never lost all four seats in one contest.”

Qinglan looked up.

The defenders were already in place. At the first table sat an old master with white hair, a junior fellow disciple of their teacher, who brought every tile he drew up close to his eyes for a look. At the second, Liuxia. At the third, Meng Chuan. The fourth was empty. Behind it stood a broad wooden armchair with a little purple-clay teapot on its seat. That was the defending champion’s place. Jinghe would not occupy it until the third day.

In the most conspicuous part of the front row sat a group in deep-blue silk robes, each with a little silver whale embroidered on the chest.

Sea Whale Trading House.

Three gong strokes. Jinghe slowly mounted the platform with his walking stick.

He did not take the armchair. He stood in the centre and saluted the crowd with clasped hands.

“Pinghe Hall has held this platform for thirty years.” His voice was not loud, but the crowd fell silent. “In those thirty years, some have taken one place from us. Some have taken two. No one has taken all four. This year will be no different.”

He paused.

“Pinghe Hall’s play is quick. But quick means not keeping people waiting. It does not mean keeping them from seeing clearly.” He looked at the row of silver whales and spoke unhurriedly. “Anyone whose play is too quick to see clearly will be asked to leave. That is our rule.”

The crowd cheered. Sea Whale’s men sat smiling, perfectly still.

Two accompanying disciples went to the seat-holder to wait for the challenger. The supervisor spread the register and addressed the crowd. “You must win before this seat’s defender to take this seat. The accompanying players each play their own game and help neither side. If one of them wins, or the hand is drawn, the game is replayed. Seats only—no money or spirit energy on the table.”

He asked those pressing too close to step back. “Pinghe Hall play: no wild indicator. Chow from your preceding player, pungs and kongs allowed, four sets and a pair or seven pairs. Anyone with a question calls stop. Keep the tiles on the table. No scattering them before the check is complete.”

◆

Qinglan did not go up.

He, Zhumo and Yuening crowded into a second-floor window directly facing the platform. Zhumo spread out paper and brush. Yuening set her abacus on the sill. Jinshi and Huo Xiao kept watch below. Lu Zhaoye had not recovered from his injuries, and Jinshi had forced him to stay in bed at the inn. As they left, he had clung to the doorframe, teeth clenched. “Watch their hands. Come back and tell me about every one.”

Qinglan had not closed his eyes all night. Last night on the boat, he had held his heart down by gritting his teeth. Today, with thousands of people below, it would not stay down.

“You really aren’t playing?” Yuening asked.

“It isn’t that I can’t,” Qinglan said. “The Hall Master has invited me. I want to watch how they play first, and what they’re still hiding today.”

He looked at the row of silver whales below the platform. “Sitting in the audience won’t make them forget me. But I needn’t put these eyes opposite them too early.”

Zhumo had already laid out paper and filled his worn brush with ink. “I’ve copied this sort of arena three times. Twelve years ago Pinghe Hall held all four seats. Hall Master Jinghe alone sat through nine games and won all nine. Nanchao still tells it.”

“Was he quick then?”

“So quick I couldn’t keep up.” Zhumo narrowed his eyes, remembering. “But in the last hand, he suddenly slowed. Held one tile for ages, just looking at it. I didn’t understand then.”

Qinglan made no reply. His gaze swept the silver whales below, row by row.

The man at their centre was Sea Whale’s chief steward. About fifty, round-faced, his eyes narrowing to slits when he smiled. His hands rested on his knees, all ten fingers interlaced. Now and then, the fingertips moved as though counting something.

The attendant on his left held a brocade box throughout. A small one. Just large enough for an incense burner.

◆

At nine, the first challenger mounted the platform.

A young player from out of town. He chose the old master’s seat. Custom required a challenger to offer incense to the defender first: a small copper burner placed beside the defender’s hand, a single stick of incense lit within it.

“The incense honours Pinghe Hall’s thirty-year reputation,” Pei Zhiwei explained. “Once a defender accepts it, he must play to the full extent of his skill.”

A slender thread of smoke curled upwards.

The four rolled dice in turn and took their seats. East began with fourteen and discarded first; the other three held thirteen waiting to draw. The accompanying disciples watched their own hands and did not speak for the defender.

The hand went quickly. The old master won on the seventh turn. The young challenger saluted and stepped down. The crowd cheered.

But Qinglan had missed it. The cheering, a child crying beneath the window, surged up towards them wave after wave. He kept his eyes on the old master’s hands, yet the corner of his eye was forever dragged off to the row of silver whales below; by the time he pulled it back, the old master had already laid down his hand. He had not seen which tile won it.

He did not grit his teeth again. He laid his hands flat on the sill. If the voices wanted to surge, let them surge. Each tile that fell, he noted; whatever he could not hold, he let go by.

The second and third challengers stepped down too.

By the third game, he missed not a single tile. Who chowed which tile, who rearranged their hand on which turn—one by one, it all settled into his mind. The voices were still there, as if through a layer of water.

Somewhere deep in his chest, beneath those two currents, a strand of something very fine stirred once, like weed at the bottom of water. It was out of reach, and it would not join. He did not reach for it.

Past ten, someone challenged Meng Chuan’s seat.

When Meng Chuan took the platform, a small figure below waved frantically from Changfeng’s shoulders. Xiaohe.

He saw her. For the first time in half a month, a smile appeared on his fine-featured face.

He played that hand with astonishing speed. Chow on the second turn. Pung on the fourth. Ready on the sixth. Self-draw on the eighth. No pause at the final step. Not one wrong tile. Not one.

As he laid down his hand, Xiaohe screamed with joy. Changfeng nearly lost his hold on her.

Meng Chuan did not look at his challenger, nor heed the applause below. He saw only the little girl perched on someone else’s shoulders. Slowly, his eyes reddened.

At the next table, Liuxia lifted her chin at him, the corners of her mouth turned up.

Zhumo’s brush had been chasing Meng Chuan’s table. Halfway through, it stopped, and he looked up. “On the fifth turn, what did Meng Chuan discard? I was busy watching the little girl—”

“Nine of Characters.” Qinglan’s eyes did not leave the window. “The challenger discarded South Wind on the same turn. Of the two accompanying disciples, one discarded Three of Dots, the other Four of Bamboo.”

Zhumo bent to check his own sheet. South Wind, Three of Dots, Four of Bamboo—he had written them all down, not one wrong. He looked up at Qinglan again. “Weren’t you watching that row of silver whales below the whole time?”

“Them too.”

Zhumo’s ear twitched. He asked nothing more, and took up his brush to add “Nine of Characters.” Yuening’s abacus beads had stopped halfway through a sum. She glanced at Qinglan, and did not ask either.

At noon, Sea Whale’s people made their move.

◆

A stout man rose.

Qinglan recognised him. The North-seat player from Chaosheng Teahouse, who had sat opposite Meng Chuan and smiled and saluted towards the stairs after every win.

He chose Liuxia’s seat.

He carried up a polished copper incense burner himself, lit the incense and respectfully set it beside her right hand. “Miss Liuxia. Please.”

Still chewing peanuts, Liuxia glanced at him. “Enough with ‘please’. Sit.”

◆

Qinglan watched the burner.

The same copper vessel as the earlier ones. The same incense. But the smoke rose differently. From the earlier burners, it had climbed in a straight, thin thread. Here it spread low across the tabletop, slowly, like a light mist, creeping over the back of Liuxia’s hand.

“Master Zhumo,” Qinglan said. “Can you smell it?”

Zhumo sniffed and shook his head. “Too far.”

Yuening’s abacus stopped. “What are you watching?”

“Her hand.”

◆

On the first turn, Liuxia’s hands were as quick as ever. Draw when due; after a chow, discard first. One continuous movement.

On the third, she chowed a Six of Bamboo. Reaching for it, her fingers slipped.

Only once. She took no notice, picked up the tile and played on.

On the fifth turn, she drew. As her fingertips touched the tile, they paused half a beat.

On the seventh, she called “Pung!” to add to her pair of East Winds. Her hand reached out, but failed to lift the tile at once. Her fingers stiffened in midair as though they belonged to someone else.

A puzzled sound came from the crowd.

Liuxia herself froze. She looked down at her right hand, spread all five fingers and tried to close them. They would not grip.

The stout man sat smiling, arranging his tiles into a perfectly neat row.

He was in no hurry.

He knew the incense had to burn halfway down before the drug took effect. By the seventh turn, the girl’s hand would no longer be hers. All he had to do was play steadily. No dealing in. No risks. Wait, turn by turn.

Pinghe Hall’s people were the fastest. But when the fastest hands slowed, their owners panicked more than anyone.

He watched Liuxia bend over her fingers, trying to move them with her other hand. His smile all but swallowed his eyes.

“He hasn’t discarded a single fresh tile,” Qinglan said. Yuening’s fingers ran once across her abacus: from the fourth turn on, every tile the stout man had discarded had already appeared in the river.

“He’s waiting on her hands.” Qinglan’s voice lowered. “The same as with Miss Luo.”

◆

Liuxia lost that hand.

She was ready by the ninth turn. But her hand was so stiff she could not even arrange her tiles neatly. She had to force them into place one by one. One more turn passed, and the stout man called self-draw.

The crowd erupted. Pinghe Hall had defended this platform for thirty years. On the first day, it had already lost a seat.

When Liuxia stood, her right arm hung at her side. Not one finger would move. Biting her lip, she said nothing, saluted the stout man with her left hand and stepped down.

The row of silver whales applauded in unison.

Upstairs, Yuening slapped her abacus onto the sill. “They’re cheating! We have to tell—”

“Tell the supervisor to preserve the record from that moment first,” Qinglan said. “Offering incense is custom; harming people is not.”

He looked at the platform. The stout man had personally carried down the copper burner and handed it to the attendant behind him. The attendant turned and slipped into the crowd.

Below, Jinshi and Huo Xiao moved almost together. One from the left, one from the right, they pushed after him.

Too many people. The attendant wriggled through the gaps like an eel. Jinshi stretched out an arm and at last seized the back of his collar, lifting him out of the crush.

Only an empty brocade box remained in his hands.

“Where’s the burner?” Jinshi roared.

Trembling, the attendant pointed aside. A great fire-fighting vat stood there, full of seawater. The bronze burner had long since sunk to the bottom; the incense ash had spread through the water and turned the whole vat a cloudy white. Huo Xiao threw out an arm to stop those about to scoop water. “Don’t touch it! Get the keepers over here to look.”

Huo Xiao looked into the jar and punched its rim. “Slippery as fish.”

As Jinshi turned to call the keepers, his grip slackened. The attendant hunched his shoulders sharply, slipped out of his outer robe and plunged headfirst into the crowd. Jinshi was left holding an empty garment. Huo Xiao chased past two rows of people and still lost him.

◆

In the shelter behind the platform, Liuxia cradled her right hand. Pain covered her forehead with sweat, but she would not cry.

“I’m fine.” The first thing she said when she saw Qinglan. “It’ll be better tomorrow.”

“Tomorrow, Meng Chuan has to play,” Qinglan said. “The day after, Jinghe.”

Her face went white.

The shelter’s curtain lifted. Jinghe entered with his walking stick. He looked at Liuxia’s hand, then at Qinglan. Asked nothing.

“Hall Master,” Liuxia said through clenched teeth. “They cheated! Put something in the incense!”

“I know something’s wrong with that hand,” Jinghe said.

“Then make him leave the platform!”

“This seat goes on record as suspect. The incense stops. The burner gets traced,” Jinghe said, watching her. “But whose hands the incense came from, how it reached your table—until every step is checked, Pinghe Hall can’t say he poisoned you. Your hand went stiff. That’s real. One hand alone can’t convict anyone.”

Liuxia had no answer.

Jinghe turned to Qinglan. “Tuidao. You’ve watched from upstairs all morning. You saw more than incense, didn’t you?”

“Yes,” Qinglan said. “They used incense today. They won’t use it tomorrow.”

Jinghe nodded and spoke slowly. “Then keep watching. Pinghe Hall has held this platform for thirty years. This year, we’ll borrow your eyes.”

Pei Zhiwei crouched beside Liuxia, lifted her stiff hand to his nose and sniffed for a long time.

“Like bone-numbing herb, mixed with sandalwood, agarwood, and one more…” He frowned. “I can’t identify it. It’s faint, masking the herb’s bitterness so the poison can’t be smelled.”

“I know someone who can.”

Changfeng. Somehow the boy had squeezed inside, his head wet with sweat. “An incense maker at the night market. Xu Zhifan. There’s no scent in Nanchao she can’t recognise.”

That afternoon, Jinghe issued an order. From that moment, Pinghe Hall would light challengers’ incense itself and provide its own burners.

The silver whales said nothing. Two more challengers went up that afternoon, both from Sea Whale, both choosing the old master’s seat. Both played honestly. Both lost.

“They’re in no hurry,” Qinglan said at the window. “Two losses, to make us think they’ve run out of tricks.”

Zhumo’s brush paused. “And their next trick?”

“Saved for tomorrow.”

◆

The night market filled a narrow lane behind the docks. At dusk, lanterns lit its length. Fish-ball sellers, sweet-soup stalls, old books, fortune-tellers. Stall beside stall.

Xu Zhifan’s incense stall stood at the far end. A small table. Dozens of little porcelain bottles. One oil lamp.

She was in her twenties, dressed in a long embroidered dress in alternating shades of dark and light purple. Small eyes, a remarkably straight nose. There were no customers, but she called to no one, only weighed pinch after pinch of incense powder on a small scale.

“What do you want?” She did not look up. “Five copper coins to calm the mind. Three to keep mosquitoes away. Directions are free. Anything else depends on my mood.” Pei Zhiwei offered the little ash he had scraped from Liuxia’s cuff. She bent to sniff it once. Her expression changed.

“Something from the sea,” she said. “Bone-numbing herb is one thing. The ingredient masking its bitterness is Deepwhale Ambergris. In Nanchao, I know of only one incense shop that’s brought it in. I know goods, not people. Whether the proprietor knew—check the books yourselves.”

“Which one?”

Xu Zhifan was silent a moment.

“Jialan Incense Shop,” she said. “The proprietor is Ruan Jianing.”

Pei Zhiwei went to pay one gold coin, but Xu Zhifan pushed it back without even looking.

“Bone-numbing herb: a pinch calms, a handful harms,” she said. “Telling you puts me in a good mood.”

◆

Jialan Incense Shop stood on a quiet arcaded street. A modest frontage. Inside, fragrance filled the room.

Ruan Jianing was in her early thirties, a Siamese cat in humanlike form: a creamy-white face, dark brown on her ears and the tip of her nose, and a pair of ice-blue eyes. In a pale-purple blouse, a dark-brown tail behind her, she was blending incense behind the counter. When she heard why they had come, the little copper spoon in her hand clattered onto the wood.

“Impossible,” she said. “For ten years, this shop has sold only calming incense, mosquito incense and incense for offerings. I’ve never touched anything like Deepwhale Ambergris.”

Yuening stepped up to the counter, ledger in her arms. “Proprietor Ruan. May I see your accounts?”

Ruan Jianing hesitated, then handed over a thick ledger.

Yuening turned the pages rapidly. At the entries for the past three months, her finger stopped.

“These.” She pointed to several lines. “‘Twenty catties of calming incense. Ordered by Sea Whale Trading House.’ ‘Thirty catties of mosquito incense. Ordered by Sea Whale Trading House’… Proprietor Ruan, does your shop sell fifty catties of incense in a month?”

Ruan Jianing stared. “Sea Whale’s orders… The new assistant took those. I… I only blend the incense. He kept the books…”

She gripped the counter, white as paper. “My late husband left me this shop. He said incense was something to quiet people’s hearts. For ten years, I’ve kept faith with those words…”

Xu Zhifan watched her. Her cold expression softened a little.

“Then someone has been using your shop to move goods,” Xu Zhifan said. “Formula, ingredients, everyone who handled it—check them one by one. Don’t just look at whose name is in the books.”

◆

The new assistant had left his job three days earlier.

But Yuening did not stop. She turned back through the ledger, page by page. Three months. Six. The handwriting changed. The new assistant’s strokes were straight and square, each one seeming carved into the page.

“Here.” Her finger stopped in a corner of the last page.

A tiny line, tucked between two spice accounts, as though jotted down in passing:

Three catties of bone-numbing herb. Half a tael of Deepwhale Ambergris. Send to Tiandu, the street behind the Scorekeepers’ Institute.

Yuening’s hand trembled.

The Scorekeepers’ Institute.

In Tiandu.

Ten years ago, when her mother went to Tiandu to keep score for someone, that was where she had gone.

◆

At the inn, after hearing about the day, Lu Zhaoye rolled over in bed, his tail thumping irritably against the boards.

“The burner went into a water jar?” he said through gritted teeth. “If I’d been there, that attendant wouldn’t have got near it.”

“You’re staying in bed.” Jinshi brought a bowl of medicine to his mouth. “Tomorrow too.”

“I’m going tomorrow!”

“If you go tomorrow,” Yuening said without looking up, “I’ll change ‘one catfolk gentleman’ back to ‘one catfolk male’.”

Lu Zhaoye opened his mouth, then drained the medicine in one gulp.

◆

That night, Ruan Jianing turned out everything the assistant had left behind: an empty chest, half an unburned incense cake, a torn-up note.

Yuening fitted the scraps together one by one. It took an hour.

The reassembled note bore a single line:

On the second day of the preliminaries, change the table.

“Change the table?” Jinshi scratched his head. “What’s that mean?”

Qinglan studied the words for a long time without speaking.

“They used incense today,” he said at last. “Pinghe Hall will be watching the incense tomorrow. So tomorrow, they won’t use incense.”

“What will they use?”

“Look at the tables,” Qinglan said. “All four. One at a time.”

Outside, the night-market lanterns went out one by one. The sea wind carried a strange saltiness.

Yuening sat beneath the lamp, gripping the ledger, staring at the line “Send to Tiandu, the street behind the Scorekeepers’ Institute.” She did not sleep all night.

She remembered her mother on the day she left. A hand on her head. A promise to return before supper. She remembered her mother sitting under a lamp, working the abacus night after night, calculating accounts Yuening could not see.

The street behind the Scorekeepers’ Institute. That was where her mother had stayed during that year’s visit to Tiandu.

Qinglan did not return to his room. He sat opposite Yuening without speaking, only trimmed the lamp wick to make it brighter.

As dawn approached, Yuening finally spoke, her voice hoarse. “When we’re finished in Nanchao, I’m going to Tiandu.”

“We’ll go together,” Qinglan said. The window paper had already turned white. The day on the note had come.
