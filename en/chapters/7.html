Chapter 7The Patient in the Flower Garden

Frost had formed on Xingyu’s eyelashes.

It was the seventeenth day of the fifth month, just past dawn. The brazier in the flower garden’s side room glowed red, its heat yellowing the window paper, yet not a trace of white had melted from her lashes.

That was the first thing Qinglan saw when he stood beside her bed. He had seen frost on the rim of a well in winter, seen the cold coating the back of the White Dragon. He had never seen frost on a living person’s eyelashes in the fifth month.

“Don’t touch her.” Lan Ruoheng did not turn. “Your hands are cold. She cannot bear another degree of chill.”

He withdrew the left hand he had reached out and clenched it inside his sleeve.

Xingyu lay in the same pink clothes, her white apron still tied on, her side bun half undone and its little flower crooked beside the pillow. Her lips were blue; so were her fingertips. Her chest rose and fell so faintly that he had to watch the edge of the quilt to see it. She was slender, the neck emerging from her pink collar so fine he scarcely dared touch it. All her usual girlish sweetness had drained away, leaving a small face so white it seemed translucent.

They had brought her over from the embroidery workshop shortly before dawn.

A young embroiderer had hammered on the Rain Pavilion’s back door with the news. Qinglan had run at once, Jinshi chasing after him with the lion head on his shoulder. By the time they reached the workshop at the mouth of the rear lane, Tao Lingtao had already sent for a doctor. Xingyu lay on a bamboo couch beside the embroidery frame, still clutching a needle threaded with blue-green silk.

Lan Ruoheng came quickly. She had treated Luo Jinke’s hand only yesterday and spent the night at her own shop in the east of the city. At the waiter’s knock, she came out carrying her brass-cornered elmwood medicine box, still in her dark-blue robe, thick braid over her right shoulder, the blue tassel on the small white flower at her temple swinging wildly.

One look beneath Xingyu’s eyelid darkened her face.

“Take her to Tang Yingtang’s flower garden,” she said. “She grows snowwatch herb there, and we can keep medicine on the stove day and night. Wait here while I go and make it up, and the girl will be cold through before I return.”

That was how they had come to the Tang family’s side room.

Lan Ruoheng raised one of Xingyu’s eyelids and told Qinglan to look.

Several exquisitely fine blue-green threads crept across the white of the eye, like the first trails of ink dropped into water before they disperse, reaching from the corner towards the pupil.

“Chill Cicada Powder,” said Lan Ruoheng. “A southern recipe. The cast shells of cicadas that refuse to die even into autumn, mixed with herbs, dried over heat and ground to powder. First the hands and feet grow cold. Then comes sleep. Once asleep, the victim cannot wake. These blue-green threads advance a fraction every two hours. When they reach the pupil, she freezes to death.”

“How long?”

Lan Ruoheng measured the distance between the threads and the pupil with her fingers, then glanced at the sky outside. “She won’t last past the dog hour.”

Qinglan counted silently. The end of the hare hour until the dog hour. Twelve hours at most.

“Can you cure it?”

“I cannot.” Lan Ruoheng’s voice was level. “The antidote must be made to match the poisoner’s recipe. There are several recipes. Get one ingredient wrong and you have another poison. All I can do is hold it down with snowwatch herb, slow its advance and buy you another two hours, or four.”

The door curtain lifted. Tang Yingtang came in carrying a great bundle of herbs.

She owned the flower garden in the east of the city. Barely into her twenties, sleeves rolled to her elbows, she was drenched in sweat. The leaves in her arms were long and slender, their undersides coated with white down as though dusted with snow. She had been cutting snowwatch herb in her beds since before dawn; dew and mud clung to her skirt. This was where Lan Ruoheng always came when she needed it.

“Enough?” she asked Lan Ruoheng.

“Boil half for now. Pound the rest and apply it over her heart,” said Lan Ruoheng. “Watch the pot. It mustn’t come to the boil more than three times.”

“I’ll watch it.” Tang Yingtang turned towards the kitchen. At the door, she glanced back at Qinglan, then at Xingyu on the bed, and said nothing.

Qinglan sat on the low stool beside the bed.

His right arm was still numb. Last night, in the Rain Pavilion’s rear courtyard, he had touched the thumbprint on his father’s letter. Half a breath had escaped, and from fingertip to shoulder his arm had ceased to belong to him. Red bumps like chilblains had risen on his fingertips. Even now, everything he touched felt as though it lay beyond a layer of thick cloth.

He looked inward at the two bamboo stalks.

The slender stalk of his first strand still held its last leaf at the tip, looking ready to fall at a touch of wind. The shoot of his second strand had only awakened last night. Its green tip had faded to white and had yet to recover.

One thread. That was all he had.

What one thread could do, he did not yet know.

“What did she eat last night?” he asked.

Tao Lingtao had stood by the door all this time. Now she spoke. “Supper with everyone at the workshop. None of the others fell ill.” She paused. “Later, someone brought a box of osmanthus cakes and said they were for her. She ate two on her own and put the rest in her sewing basket.”

“Who brought them?”

“A Hengchang food box,” Tao Lingtao said. “There was a visiting card tucked into the lid.”

She took it from her sleeve and laid it on the table.

The card was in Hengchang’s style, red with a gold border. On it, in a neat inscription: “With Mochuan’s compliments.”

Qinglan stared at the words. He remembered Qian Youqing’s egg-sized prayer beads at the Rain Pavilion three days before, and the Hengchang servant bringing Luo Jinke her tea on a tray. The proprietor of Hengchang, sending osmanthus cakes to a young embroiderer.

“Why,” he asked, “would Mochuan send her cakes?”

Tao Lingtao did not answer at once. She looked at Xingyu on the bed as though asking the sleeping girl for leave to speak.

“Because she is Mochuan’s daughter,” she said. “His only daughter.”

Silence fell over the room.

Jinshi lowered the lion head from his arms to the floor. His mouth opened, then closed again.

“She grew up in Tiandu,” said Tao Lingtao. “Two years ago, when she was sixteen, Mochuan brought her here from their old home to learn the accounts at Hengchang’s Yunchuan branch. Before three months were out, she ran out of Hengchang’s back door with nothing but the clothes on her back and a bundle of sewing things. She said the accounts in her father’s shops were false, the tiles were false, even the tea they served their customers was false. She wanted no more part in Hengchang. She knelt outside my workshop and said she could embroider, she could work, she needed no wages—only please not to send her back.”

“Did Mochuan come looking?”

“Once,” said Tao Lingtao. “He stood at the mouth of the lane without coming in. Watched for a long time, then left. After that, at festivals and New Year, a Hengchang servant would leave a box of cakes outside the workshop. Never a message. Never waited. Xingyu never ate any. Gave them all to the other girls.”

“This time she ate them.”

“This time there was a card in the lid,” said Tao Lingtao. “The first time in two years her father had put his name to a gift. She sat looking at that card for half the night. When I checked on her before bed, she was still at the embroidery frame, with the card tucked beneath her handkerchief.”

Qinglan looked down at the visiting card.

The gold border was genuine, the red background genuine. The inscription was upright and orderly, every stroke made with a right hand, its endings pressed down and to the right. A hand accustomed to accounts.

But he remembered the hand on the stone table last night.

“Someone knew she would refuse a gift from anyone else,” he said, “so they borrowed her father’s name.”

The sewing basket stood at the foot of the bed. Tao Lingtao brought it over. Three osmanthus cakes remained beneath a pile of scraps. On top lay a silk handkerchief stretched on a little hoop, ivory white, a sprig of blue-green bamboo half embroidered in one corner.

The bamboo was straight.

Qinglan looked once, then turned his eyes away. Another handkerchief lay inside his robe, its bamboo crooked, stitches skipped, thread ends left loose. A few days ago at the Rain Pavilion, she had pressed it into his hand with a blushing face.

On this one, she had got the bamboo straight.

Halfway through the dragon hour, someone burst through the garden’s front gate.

“Xingyu! Where is Xingyu—”

The man was in his forties. His brocade robe was of the finest cloth, but a great smear of mud stained its front, one sleeve had torn at the seam, and his hat was gone. No servants followed him. No sedan chair. He was alone, gasping as though he had run all the way from the west of the city.

The garden hands could not stop him. He blundered into the side room, saw the girl on the bed and almost buckled. Only the doorframe kept him from falling to his knees.

Tao Lingtao stood between him and the bed. “Master Mo.”

Mochuan did not look at her. His eyes were fixed on Xingyu’s face, on the frost. For a long time his lips trembled before he managed, “I didn’t send… I never sent her…”

“Your card was in the lid.”

“The cards are from my shop!” His voice shot up, then collapsed. “Qian Youqing was taken into the Bureau of Balance yesterday. Hengchang was found guilty of betraying the Way, there and then. Half the shops are sealed, half the servants have run. Anyone could get at the cards in the storeroom… For two years I haven’t dared put my name on anything. How could I…”

Unable to go on, he fumbled a piece of paper from inside his robe and held it out to Qinglan.

“Someone pushed this under my door before dawn.”

Ordinary bamboo paper, folded twice. Few words:

“Your daughter has taken Chill Cicada Powder. Before the dog hour, go to the Bureau of Balance and lay charges against Qinglan: on the fourteenth day of the fifth month, at the Rain Pavilion, he secretly brought a Heavenly Tile to the table and used breath to interfere with the match. Make the accusation and the antidote will be delivered. Refuse, and collect her body at the dog hour.”

Qinglan studied the writing.

It leaned left. The stroke endings dragged down and left; the horizontals began on the right and finished on the left. The same habits as the hand that had added words at the foot of the agreement on the stone table beneath the old archway last night.

Left-handed writing.

“Were you going to accuse me?” he asked.

Mochuan stared at him, eyes bloodshot. He gave no answer. His face had already answered.

Jinshi sprang up. “You dare—”

Qinglan lifted his left hand a little. Jinshi sat down again.

“If you accuse me,” Qinglan told Mochuan, “the Bureau of Balance has to investigate. To investigate me, they have to reopen the Rain Pavilion match. How long will that take? Three days? Five? Those days are what they want.” He paused. “And the antidote? After you’ve accused me, what makes them send it? How will you demand it?”

Mochuan’s lips moved.

“You can’t,” said Qinglan. “They already have you on a string. Let them pull it again, and you’re still on the string.”

Before the words had faded, something struck the window frame with a sharp thunk.

A bone tile had buried itself half an inch in the wood. Its protruding end still hummed.

Jinshi reached the window first and threw it open. Outside lay the garden’s rows of snowwatch herb. Beyond the fence, a narrow lane stood empty. Only a yellow dog fled with its tail between its legs.

The bone tile was a Two of Bamboo. Its back was smooth and unbranded. A slip of paper lay pinned beneath it against the frame.

Qinglan pulled the tile free and unfolded the paper.

The same left-handed script:

“At noon, the abandoned dye works west of the city. One hand under Tuidao School rules. East wins, the antidote is yours. East loses, the White Dragon is mine. North builds the wall. Not one word of the agreement may be changed. Bring any witnesses you please. Let anyone from the Bureau of Balance enter the dye works, and the medicine goes into a dye vat.”

He turned it over. On the back was another line, in fresher ink:

“Master Mo’s road remains open to you as well. Two roads. Choose one.”

Qi Tingjun had come in close on Mochuan’s heels.

He had spent the night leaning against the door in the Rain Pavilion’s rear courtyard, keeping watch. At dawn, the evidence courier came for the new wooden box. Only after seeing it onto the Arbitration Office’s cart, bound for Vault One in Tiandu, had he set out for the flower garden. The ebony waist tag hung at his belt. Dark circles ringed his eyes.

He looked first at the girl in the bed, then at Mochuan, and finally at the two slips in Qinglan’s hand.

“My men chased Hu Shan through the eastern bamboo grove all night. They didn’t catch him.” That was all he said.

Qinglan handed him both pieces of paper.

After reading them, Qi Tingjun turned the bone tile between his fingers and examined its back. “Unbranded again.” He returned the papers. “Two roads. One sends him to accuse you; the other sends you to play. Whichever you take, that hand profits.”

“Then I’ll take neither,” Qinglan said.

Qi Tingjun raised his eyes.

Qinglan turned to Mochuan. “Master Mo, go to the Bureau of Balance.”

Mochuan stared.

“Lay charges,” said Qinglan. “True ones. How Hengchang keeps its accounts. Where the bone-numbing herb comes from. Who took the box for the osmanthus cakes from your storeroom. How many dealings you’ve had with the man who uses his left hand. Tell them everything. Once you do, you’re no longer a string for them to pull. They have you because there are things you dare not say. Say them all, and they lose their grip.”

“But Xingyu—”

“I’ll go for Xingyu,” said Qinglan. “I’ll take that seat at noon.”

Mochuan stared at him as though he had never seen him before.

“You… Why would you do this for her…” His voice was hoarse. “You know whose daughter she is. Her father almost went to accuse you.”

“I know,” said Qinglan. “She mended a red cord for me.”

Mochuan’s shoulders sagged.

He stood on the threshold a while, then looked back at his daughter. As he gazed, he suddenly spoke, so quietly he seemed to be speaking to himself. “Her mother and little brother are still in that small courtyard in eastern Tiandu. Her brother was only five when she left. He’s seven now. She hasn’t seen him once since.”

He wiped his face, turned and clasped his hands before Qi Tingjun. “Captain Qi. I’ll go with your men.”

Qi Tingjun stayed where he was. He called in a Guard of Balance and gave a few instructions under his breath. The guard led Mochuan out. In the front hall, Mochuan stopped to tuck the torn cuff of his brocade robe out of sight, as though he must look presentable for the person he was going to meet.

“That house in Tiandu,” Qi Tingjun said to Qinglan, watching Mochuan’s retreating back. “I’ll send word. Have the people there keep an eye on it.”

He turned back. “I won’t enter the dye works. The note says the medicine goes into a vat if anyone from the Bureau comes through the door. I’ll stay here and guard the garden. The poisoner knows she’s here. Once you leave, this room holds only a few women and a patient.”

“Thank you.”

“No need,” said Qi Tingjun. “Win, and bring the medicine back. Then I can use it to trace the recipe. Knowing which southern household made it will be worth more than a scrap of paper.”

Yuening arrived during the snake hour.

She had come from the Rain Pavilion, ledger in her arms and a sheaf of paper beneath it. Her hurried walk had brought a faint flush to both cheeks; she was still breathing hard, her chest rising and falling beneath the crossed collar. She stopped at the door and caught her breath before stepping inside. First she looked at Xingyu, for a long time. Then she turned to Qinglan. “The agreement?”

Qinglan handed her the slip.

She read it, laid it on the table and pulled the brush from beside her bun. She wrote a few small words in the blank margin, then struck them out.

“North builds the wall,” she said. “You’ve accepted that?”

“Not one word of the agreement may be changed.”

“I know,” said Yuening. “I asked whether you’d accepted it.”

“I have.”

She looked at him and asked no more. She spread out the sheaf: his game record from the old archway, written left-handed last night, crooked lines and inkblots upon inkblots. Sitting at the little table beneath the window, she opened her ledger, ground ink and began to copy.

“You’re copying it now?”

“I said I’d copy it tomorrow.” Yuening did not look up. “Today is tomorrow.”

She rolled her right sleeve halfway up her forearm to keep the cuff out of the inkstone. Her wrist was slender, its inner skin so fair that a pale blue-green vein showed beneath it. The vein stirred as she lifted her brush, as though following the words. An ink spot the size of a millet grain marked her wrist bone. She had not noticed.

Qinglan watched her writing. On his sheet it said, “Eleventh turn, North, discard Eight of Bamboo.” Beneath her brush, a neat line of small formal script emerged: “Eleventh turn: North, the man in the woven hat, discarded the Eight of Bamboo.”

At his name, her brush paused. On earlier pages she had called him “Young Master Qing”. On this page she wrote “Qinglan”. Having written it, she left it.

He moved his gaze from his name to the ink spot on her wrist, then away again, to the snowwatch herb outside.

“What are you looking at?” Yuening asked.

“The herbs.”

“What is there to look at?” She bent to her writing again. “Your record is missing a tile between the seventh and eighth turns. East’s discard that turn. You left it out. I filled it in from what you told us when you came back last night, and marked it ‘added’. When anyone checks the record later, they’ll know what you wrote and what I supplied.”

“…All right.”

“The hand at noon,” she said. “I’m coming. As no scorekeeper—this isn’t a sworn match of honour. There is no scorekeeper. I’m coming to record it.”

“You don’t have to—”

“That is my affair,” said Yuening. “The agreement says to bring any witnesses you please. Master Xiao will witness; I’ll sit beside him and write. Whoever asks about this hand in future, it will be in my ledger.”

She blew on the ink and pressed the page between the ledger’s leaves.

Before noon, they set out for the west of the city. Zhumo had also hurried over from the Rain Pavilion. He stayed at the garden to help watch the fire beneath the snowwatch pot.

Jinshi was coming to take a seat. The agreement specified one Tuidao School hand, with four players. The other side had said only that North would build the wall, not how many people they would supply. Qinglan guessed two. His side would need another player; he would not trust a stranger in the vacant seat.

“All I know is how to call pung,” Jinshi said.

“That’s enough,” said Qinglan.

The road west crossed a stone bridge. Halfway over, Qinglan stopped, prised four pebbles from beneath the parapet and arranged them on the bridge: East, South, West, North.

“North builds the wall,” he said. “We all know what a wall builder can do. A hundred and thirty-six tiles, four walls. Before play begins, it’s already fixed who draws which stack, which tile. A skilled builder can put the tiles he wants into the slots he’ll draw from. They call it ‘feeding the North slots’. He sits at North, so he feeds North. He has a good idea what we’ll draw, too.”

Jinshi stared at the four pebbles. “Then what’s the point in playing?”

“We play.” Qinglan picked up the western pebble. “The draw goes East, South, West, North. East draws, then South, then West, then North. That’s the order he’s built for.” He nudged the western pebble towards the northern one. “But if West calls pung on South’s discard, West doesn’t draw. He discards straight away. North draws next—and takes the tile meant for West. After that, East gets the one meant for North.”

Jinshi thought with his mouth open for a long time. “…I call pung once, and the tiles he’s fed himself start flowing to you?”

“Only once,” said Qinglan. “On South’s discard. One pung shifts it by one place. One place, and the whole run he’s laid out starts flowing to East. Call pung again, on North’s discard, and you shift it back.”

“What if he calls pung himself?”

“If he calls on mine, he skips South and West, and scrambles it all again,” said Qinglan. “So I won’t give him tiles he can call on.”

Beside them, Yuening wrote these words in the margin of her ledger too. When she finished, she looked up. “Which tile should he call on?”

“I don’t know,” Qinglan said. “When South discards something you have a pair of, call pung. Only the first tile you can call on.”

Jinshi closed his fist around the western pebble.

Xiao Cheng'an had followed with his stick, silent all the way. Now he coughed. “One word from an old man. That table hasn’t had its boundary activated by the Arbitration Office. I have no seal of justice to set on it. All I can do is sit beside you, watch clearly and say what I see.”

“That’s enough,” said Qinglan.

The abandoned dye works stood at the far western edge of the city, beside the river.

Half the courtyard wall had fallen. The sign above the door was long gone, leaving two nail holes. Rows of wooden drying racks filled the yard. A few faded lengths of indigo cloth still hung from them, swelling in the wind as though someone stood behind each one.

The main room was large, with more than a dozen dye vats on the floor, their rims crusted with old blue-black residue. A square table stood in the middle. Its surface was blue too, the colour of decades of indigo soaking in, layer upon layer.

“There’s a copper ring,” said Xiao Cheng'an, circling the table with his stick and bending to inspect the floor, “under this table.”

Thin copper strips were set into the grey bricks, enclosing the table and all four chairs. The copper had blackened, but its joins remained closed. There was no break.

Xiao Cheng'an tapped the ring with his stick. “It was activated once. This used to be the western guildhall’s mahjong room,” he said. “After the guildhall moved, they rented it out as a dye works. The Arbitration Office activated this boundary back then. With the joins unbroken, the ring remains closed. Let anyone inside it stir their breath, and it lights.”

“Do they know?” Jinshi asked.

“They know,” said Qinglan. “They chose this place because they want it to light.”

A man already occupied the northern chair. Thin, in his forties, with a slight limp in his left foot, but his hands were pale and long, all ten fingers spotless, nails neatly trimmed. An open tile box stood before him.

“Hou Qi,” he introduced himself in a southern accent. “Wall builder. Paid to build one hand’s worth.”

A wall builder. In games within the city and beyond, wealthy proprietors who could not afford a celebrated player hired such men to arrange the walls, paying them by the hand. Their names never appeared in the rankings. They did not want them there.

A man in black sat in the southern chair, half a black cloth covering his face, only his eyes exposed. He said nothing.

Hou Qi took a small black porcelain bottle from his robe and set it in the centre of the table. “The antidote.” Then a sheet of paper, which he slipped beneath the bottle. “The agreement. This is the full text. What you received at the garden was a few of the important clauses.”

Yuening picked it up and read aloud:

“One hand under Tuidao School rules. North builds the wall; East may count the tiles. The third tile from the tail of the wall shall be turned up to determine the wild tile and remain in the wall. Pungs and kongs permitted; no chows. Seven Pairs may win. East wins, the antidote goes to East; East loses, the White Dragon goes to North. Any party introducing an outside tile or swapping tiles betrays the Way and is ruled defeated. Tiles forming the winning hand shall be counted after play. If any falls outside the original hundred and thirty-six, the win is void and the winner is ruled defeated. One witness, whose word is final.”

At the final clause, she paused and looked up at Qinglan.

Qinglan was thinking about that clause too.

“Tiles forming the winning hand shall be counted after play.” Fair enough—but needlessly fair. In what hand could the tiles not be counted afterwards? Why single it out, and make it so precise? Not “the player who introduced the tile is ruled defeated”, but “the winner is ruled defeated”.

He kept the clause in mind and merely nodded.

“Inspect the stakes,” said Hou Qi.

Qinglan reached out with his left hand and picked up the black bottle. It was icy, far colder than ordinary porcelain, as though it held a piece of ice instead of medicine. He pulled the stopper and sniffed. A trace of sweetness beneath the bitterness, like licorice boiled too long. He replaced the stopper, ran his thumb twice over the mouth and only then set it back on the table.

“What are you rubbing?” Hou Qi asked.

“Checking the mouth for cracks,” said Qinglan.

Hou Qi looked at him and asked no more.

Qinglan had used his left hand. His right hung inside his sleeve. He had only wanted his hand to remain on that bottle a little longer.

East and North were fixed by the agreement; the others took their places. Qinglan at East, the dealer. The man in black at South. Jinshi at West. Hou Qi at North.

Xiao Cheng'an fetched a bench and sat at the southeast corner, where he could see all four players’ hands. Yuening sat beside him, her ledger open on her knees.

Hou Qi began building the wall.

Those hands were truly quick. A hundred and thirty-six tiles poured onto the blue tabletop; one sweep of his ten fingers turned them facedown and spread them out with a swish. Another sweep, and there were four walls, seventeen stacks each, two tiles to a stack, as straight as if measured with a ruler. The entire set slid back and forth beneath his hands. Every tile rubbed against that indigo surface.

Qinglan counted as the agreement allowed. He touched no tiles, only counted stacks: seventeen in the eastern wall, seventeen in the southern, seventeen in the western, seventeen in the northern. Sixty-eight stacks. A hundred and thirty-six tiles.

At the last stack, he noticed a faint blue streak on the tiles’ edges.

He checked the other stacks. Every one had it, some dark, some pale, all indigo—the decades of colour on this table had rubbed off onto the tiles.

He made a mental note.

“The count is right,” he said.

Hou Qi threw the dice, broke the wall and turned up the wild tile. The third tile from the tail was a South Wind.

“South Wind is wild,” said Yuening. “Three others remain.”

Before they dealt, water stirred in the largest dye vat in the corner.

Everyone turned.

The water was stagnant, blue-black beneath a film of oil. Now rings spread beneath that sheen, travelling from the middle to the rim and back again. No wind. No one had touched the vat. The ripples moved of their own accord.

Jinshi went pale. “Something in the water?”

“Probably the Chill Cicada Powder,” Hou Qi said mildly across the table. “Someone tipped the dregs of a batch into it this morning.”

Qinglan made no answer. He rose, went to the vat and looked down at the ripples.

They formed rings, but not true circles. Each leaned a little towards him, as though something inside had caught his scent and wanted to come closer.

What on him had a scent?

The White Dragon.

He took the copper coin from his right sleeve—the one from the old archway, its debt cancelled, its two file marks still there. With his left hand, he held it an inch above the water.

The ripples stopped.

The oily film became smooth as a mirror.

He withdrew the coin. Slowly the ripples began again, still leaning towards him.

“It isn’t the medicine,” said Qinglan. “Medicine isn’t afraid of copper. It’s the vat.”

“The vat?”

“An old vat, standing beside mahjong tables for decades,” Xiao Cheng'an said from the bench, his voice dropping as though his tale had reached a crucial point. “Stray breath from Heavenly Tiles has left it half alive. In my stories, we call it an ‘object-spirit’. An abacus bead moves by itself. A copper bell rings without wind. Water ripples in a vat. It cannot think. It can only do what it was made to do. A dye vat is made to steep things.”

“What does it want to steep?” asked Jinshi.

Qinglan glanced at his chest and did not answer. He went back to his seat. The vat stood barely five paces from the table, but the table was inside the copper ring and the vat outside. Object-spirits feared copper. It could not get in.

Hou Qi watched him. The corner of his mouth moved, but he made no sound.

He wants me distracted, Qinglan thought. He knows the vat will stir. He chose this place to make the copper ring light—and to put one more thing on my mind.

Qinglan was the dealer. He began with fourteen tiles.

He stood them up and looked: One, Two, Four, Six, Eight and Nine of Bamboo; a pair of Three of Bamboo; Two and Seven of Characters; Five and Nine of Dots; North Wind; and one South Wind, the wild tile.

Six Bamboo tiles plus the pair of Threes. Eight. A long way from a Full Flush, but the foundation was there.

He discarded the North Wind.

South, the man in black, discarded Nine of Characters. West, Jinshi, drew, studied his tiles for a long time and discarded Three of Bamboo. North, Hou Qi, discarded One of Dots.

On the second turn, Qinglan drew Five of Bamboo and discarded Nine of Dots.

The man in black drew and discarded East Wind.

Jinshi’s hand trembled.

Qinglan did not look at him directly, but from the corner of his eye he saw the hand clench and relax. The pebble had left an imprint in Jinshi’s palm. He had a pair of East Winds—Qinglan had seen him move the two tiles together at the deal, then surreptitiously press them with a finger.

“Pung!” Jinshi shouted so loudly that he flinched at his own voice.

He exposed his two East Winds, claimed the third and laid out the set. Then he discarded Two of Dots. He hesitated as he did so, turning the tile half round between his fingers.

Hou Qi’s eyes rested on Jinshi’s hand for a moment.

Only a moment. Then he drew. He turned the new tile twice in his fingers before discarding Eight of Characters.

Qinglan knew he had noticed. A wall builder counted out his favoured slots stack by stack. One pung, one place out. The tile he had just drawn was no longer the one he had put there for himself.

On the third turn, Qinglan drew Seven of Bamboo.

He discarded Two of Characters.

South discarded Four of Dots, West One of Characters, and North Six of Characters. After discarding, Hou Qi tapped the table edge once, then withdrew his finger.

On the fourth turn, Qinglan drew Two of Bamboo.

He slotted it into place and discarded Five of Dots. Now his Bamboo tiles were One, Two, Two, Three, Three, Four, Five, Six, Seven, Eight, Nine—eleven of them—alongside a Seven of Characters and a South Wind.

Hou Qi drew that turn, rolled the tile between his fingers and discarded Three of Bamboo.

There were now two Three of Bamboo in the discard pool: Jinshi’s from the first turn, and Hou Qi’s. Add the pair in Qinglan’s hand, and that made four. Qinglan kept the count in mind.

On the fifth turn, Qinglan drew Six of Bamboo.

He glanced at it and discarded Seven of Characters.

One, Two, Two, Three, Three, Four, Five, Six, Six, Seven, Eight, Nine. Twelve Bamboo tiles, plus one wild.

Ready.

He went through his waits in his head: One-Two-Three, Two-Three-Four, a pair of Sixes, Seven-Eight-Nine, leaving Five of Bamboo and the wild tile. Any Bamboo except Eight would complete a hand; another wild tile would win too. A Full Flush. A concealed hand.

Hou Qi discarded Five of Characters that turn.

The tile slid half an inch across the table and stopped directly before Jinshi.

Jinshi had a pair of Five of Characters. Qinglan guessed it when he saw the hand reach halfway out. Hou Qi needed no guesswork. He had built the wall. No one knew better what Jinshi held.

Jinshi’s hand hung halfway across the table.

“Pu—”

“Pass,” Jinshi said. He swallowed his own call, pulled his hand back and pressed it against his knee until his knuckles whitened. “Pass. No pung.”

Hou Qi’s eyelid twitched.

If West called pung on North’s discard, North would draw next. The slot that had shifted would shift back again. Hou Qi was offering Jinshi the pung of Five of Characters in exchange for the slots he had prepared for the whole hand. Jinshi’s face was crimson, as though for the first time in his life someone had held meat to his lips and forbidden him to eat.

On the sixth turn, Qinglan drew One of Dots. Hou Qi had discarded one on the first turn; he probably had no pair and could not call pung. Qinglan discarded it.

The man in black drew and, without looking, discarded Three of Bamboo.

The tile landed directly facing Qinglan.

A Full Flush. Concealed. His own deal. One, Two, Two, Three, Three, Four, Five, Six, Six, Seven, Eight, Nine, plus South Wind. Add this Three of Bamboo: One-Two-Three, Two-Three-Four, Three-South Wind-Five, Seven-Eight-Nine, and the pair of Sixes.

A winning hand.

His left hand already rested on his standing tiles.

Xiao Cheng'an leaned forward on the bench. Yuening’s brush hovered above the page.

Qinglan’s hand stopped.

Four.

There were four Three of Bamboo in a set. Two in his hand. One Jinshi had discarded on the first turn. One Hou Qi had discarded on the fourth. All four accounted for.

This was a fifth.

He took his hand off the standing tiles.

“Pass,” he said.

Jinshi nearly leapt from his chair. “You—”

Qinglan ignored him and turned to Xiao Cheng'an. “Master Xiao, would you please count the Three of Bamboo?”

Hou Qi’s expression changed. Above the black cloth, the other man’s eyes narrowed.

“East, you passed on a win,” Hou Qi said. “You cannot win on that tile again this hand. What count? If you want a count, wait until the hand is over.”

“The agreement says tiles forming the winning hand are counted after play,” Qinglan said. “I haven’t won. I’m asking the witness to count under the clause that says ‘any party introducing an outside tile or swapping tiles betrays the Way and is ruled defeated’. That clause does not say to wait until afterwards.”

Xiao Cheng'an rose and approached the table with his stick.

“East, how many Three of Bamboo do you hold?”

Qinglan tilted his standing tiles so that only Xiao Cheng'an could see their faces; their backs still faced the others. “Two.”

Xiao Cheng'an bent to look and nodded. “Two.” He looked at the discard pool. “One from West, one from North, one just discarded by South.” He straightened. “Five. A set has only four.”

For a moment the room was still.

“One of his two was brought in from outside!” Hou Qi slapped the table. “East touched the wall when he counted—”

“He did not.” Yuening’s quiet voice came from the bench. “I recorded it: ‘East counted stacks only. Did not touch the tiles.’”

“Then it must—”

“Master Xiao,” Qinglan said, “please turn over all five and look at their edges.”

Xiao Cheng'an did not hurry. First he took the two from Qinglan’s hand and turned them sideways to the window light. A faint blue streak marked each edge. Then Jinshi’s discard and Hou Qi’s. Their edges were blue too.

Last, he picked up the tile South had just discarded.

Its edges were spotless, white as freshly cut bone.

“This table,” Xiao Cheng'an said, turning to show everyone the tile, “has soaked up indigo for decades. When North built the wall, every tile rubbed against its surface. Every edge picked up colour. This one never rubbed against the table.” He set it down. “It came from a sleeve.”

The man in black drew his hand a little way into his cuff.

“South introduced an outside tile,” said Xiao Cheng'an. His voice was low, each word firm. “Betrayal of the Way. Defeat. Under the agreement, East wins. The antidote belongs to East.”

Qinglan looked at Hou Qi. “You wanted me to win on that tile. After the hand, you’d count, and the fifth copy would be in my winning hand. ‘The winner is ruled defeated.’ That’s why you wrote the clause so carefully.”

Hou Qi opened his mouth. No sound came.

The man in black moved.

He kicked a table leg. The whole table heaved towards East. Tiles flew like hail, clattering across the floor. Qinglan threw himself back, the table edge grazing his chin. His chair overturned and he fell onto the grey bricks.

Hou Qi’s hands were quicker than anyone’s. Those wall-building hands snatched the tumbling black bottle out of the air, and he was already through the broken window to the north.

“The medicine!” Jinshi roared. He chased him to the window, but the man in black seized a cloth-drying pole from the wall and struck his shoulder from behind. Jinshi staggered two steps.

Qinglan lay on the floor with only one thought.

He had rubbed his thumb twice over the bottle’s mouth.

He closed his eyes and saw the slender bamboo within him, the final leaf at its tip. He plucked that leaf, rolled it into a thread and tied it to the bottle.

The leaf was gone.

Something inside him emptied at once. Then heat rose over his heart, as though a red-hot filament had pierced his chest, passed through the broken window and pulled north.

The copper ring beneath him lit.

A faint blue-green glow shone from the strips in the floor, washing the scattered tiles with colour. The man in black looked back and froze. “He used breath—”

“The hand has already been ruled on,” said Xiao Cheng'an, standing with his stick. “And you overturned the table.”

The man cursed, dropped the pole and fled through the door.

Qinglan scrambled up and followed the thread at his heart through the broken window.

Beyond it lay the drying yard.

Rows upon rows of wooden racks hung with long lengths of indigo cloth, one beside another. The wind swelled every strip in the yard, set every strip moving. Everywhere he looked, figures seemed to stand.

Hou Qi had vanished.

But Qinglan did not need to see. When the thread at his heart tugged left, he ran left. It stretched longer and hotter, until his chest felt as though it held a live coal. He slipped between two lengths of cloth. A nail on a rack caught the right sleeve of his substitute player’s robe. With a ripping hiss, the sleeve embroidered in gold bamboo leaves tore from elbow to wrist. He did not stop.

The thread pulled towards the river.

He burst past the last row of racks and saw Hou Qi. Limping on his left foot, the man had reached the embankment and was about to jump down. A figure charged across his path—Jinshi. Instead of following through the window, he had circled round the courtyard wall. He swung the lion head into the back of Hou Qi’s knee.

Hou Qi pitched onto the embankment. The black bottle flew from his hand and rolled twice over the stones.

Qinglan lunged and clapped his hand over it.

Hou Qi turned over, glanced at Jinshi, then at Qinglan, and stopped struggling. Continuing the roll, he tumbled down the bank and plunged into the river with a splash. By the time Jinshi reached the edge, only ripples remained. Downstream, Hou Qi’s head emerged among the reeds, already swimming away.

“Let him go,” Qinglan said.

He sat on the embankment and put the bottle inside his robe, his left hand pressed over it.

The thread at his heart was still tied, burning until his vision blurred. He closed his eyes and untied it.

As it slackened, warmth filled his nose. A drop of blood fell on the back of his hand.

He wiped it away and sat a while. Only when the dancing gold specks had cleared from his sight did he rise.

The slender bamboo was bare. Not a single leaf remained at its tip. The shoot of the second strand was still white at the point.

He had not one thread left.

But the bottle lay inside his robe.

By the time they returned to the flower garden, the goat hour was more than half gone.

Yuening came through the door behind Qinglan, her ledger in her arms, sweat covering her forehead. She and Xiao Cheng'an had followed on a donkey cart delivering cloth and caught up with him at the embankment. The torn sleeve hung from his elbow to the back of his hand, snapping in the wind all the way. She had looked at it several times without speaking.

Qi Tingjun stood outside the side room. At the sight of Qinglan, his eyes went first to the front of his robe, then to the smear of blood still beneath his nose.

“I have it.” Qinglan took out the black bottle.

Lan Ruoheng received it, pulled the stopper and sniffed. Then she poured a drop onto the back of her own hand and watched it sink slowly into the flesh. She waited half the time it took to drink a cup of tea. A small patch of coolness spread across her hand; after it passed, her skin flushed faintly red.

“The antidote,” she said. “It matches the pattern of those blue-green threads in her eyes.” She poured out another drop, licked it up and tasted it. “Licorice boiled too long, hiding a bitter herb from the coast. A southern recipe.”

At the door, Qi Tingjun wrote those words on a slip of paper.

Lan Ruoheng mixed the medicine into warm water. Tang Yingtang cradled Xingyu’s head and fed it to her slowly with a little spoon. Half went down; half dribbled out. Tang Yingtang caught it with a handkerchief, letting none reach the pillow.

When they finished, nothing had changed.

Xingyu lay as before, frost still on her lashes, lips still blue.

“We must wait,” Lan Ruoheng said. “Chill Cicada Powder goes in slowly. The antidote must come out slowly too.”

She frowned at her own words, as though dissatisfied with their precision, and corrected herself. “It takes two hours for those threads to retreat one fraction.”

Qinglan sat on the low stool beside the bed.

During the monkey hour, the blue-green threads retreated a fraction.

At the start of the rooster hour, the frost on Xingyu’s lashes melted into two drops of water and ran from the corners of her eyes into her hair.

Qinglan sat there all the while.

His right arm was still numb, his left hand rested on his knee. The long rent in his right sleeve exposed his forearm. A lamp had been lit. Its wick popped.

Xingyu’s eyelids stirred.

She opened her eyes, unfocused, and gazed at the bed canopy for a long time before slowly turning towards him. Her gaze settled on his face, then moved down to the torn sleeve.

Her lips moved. Her voice was as frail as a loose thread:

“The robe… Is it torn…”

Qinglan stared for a moment, then looked down at his sleeve.

“It is,” he said. “You can mend it when you’re well.”

Xingyu looked at him. Her almond eyes, their outer corners tipped slightly upward, slowly filled with a little moisture. With her lashes wet, she was more tender to look upon than in her usual laughing moments. She said nothing more. A hand crept out from under the quilt; her fingertips touched the scrap of torn sleeve hanging over the bedside, caught it and held it. Still holding it, she fell asleep again.

This time it was true sleep. Her breathing steadied. The blue of her lips faded to a pale white.

Lan Ruoheng came over and felt her wrist. “She’s through.”

At the door, Tao Lingtao pressed her sleeve to the corner of her eye and went out.

Qinglan did not leave that night.

Xingyu kept hold of his torn sleeve. Whenever he moved, her brows pinched. So he sat still on the low stool, the second bowl of medicine Lan Ruoheng had left in his left hand. He blew it cool and waited. Whenever Xingyu woke, he gave her two mouthfuls. She drank and slept again.

Tang Yingtang tended the pot of snowwatch herb in the kitchen, coming in from time to time to add charcoal.

During the boar hour, she came in carrying a little medicine box and crouched beside him. Her skirt gathered at her knees; she leaned slightly forward, and a sweet edge of herbal bitterness drifted from her sleeves. Her features were clear and brisk. A stray lock at her temple, curled by the stove’s heat, hung against her cheek. She left it there.

“Your hand,” she said.

Qinglan did not understand.

“The right one,” said Tang Yingtang. “Chilblains. You’ve kept it tucked away ever since you came back. I noticed.”

She drew his numb right hand from his sleeve and laid it on her knee. Opening the box, she scooped up a fingertip of ointment and smoothed it over his fingertips. The salve was cool and smelled of mint mingled with snowwatch herb. She worked carefully, finishing one finger before moving to the next, pressing down along the joints with the pad of her finger, covering every red bump.

His right hand could feel neither pain nor coolness. But he could see her hands. He could see the lock falling from her temple as she bent her head, and beneath the opening of her crossed collar, the small shadow the lamp cast over her collarbone.

She bent low. Her breath fell warm against his wrist, carrying a trace of snowwatch bitterness. When she reached the web of his thumb, she cupped the back of his hand in her palm. Her palm was warm. His hand could not feel it, but his eyes could.

He looked away, towards the drawers of herbs along the wall. Each bore a red paper label: angelica, lovage, white angelica, siler. The garden grew and harvested its own. Lan Ruoheng often came for supplies when making medicines.

“Done,” said Tang Yingtang.

She put his hand back and closed the box. About to rise, she stopped.

She looked down at her palm. A thin sheen of sweat glistened there, mingled with a little ointment.

She wiped it on her skirt and raised her head. Her eyes fell directly on Xingyu’s hand upon the bed—the hand still clutching his torn sleeve, knuckles pale with the grip, not loosening even in sleep.

Tang Yingtang watched that hand for a while.

Then she pushed the medicine box into Qinglan’s left hand.

“Twice a day,” she said. “Apply it yourself.”

She rose, brushed her skirt and lifted the curtain to leave. After a while, the snip of shears came from the rear garden. Snip. Snip. Very slowly.

Qi Tingjun returned during the rat hour.

He had spent much of the night at the Bureau of Balance, and his face was grimmer than it had been that morning. Entering the side room, he glanced first at the sleeping Xingyu and lowered his voice.

“Mochuan told us everything,” he said. “Put his mark to it.”

He took a paper from his robe. He did not hand it to Qinglan, but read one sentence:

“Both Chill Cicada Powder and bone-numbing herb were shipped north by the Sea Whale Trading House of Nanchao, through shops dealing in goods from the sea.”

“Nanchao,” said Qinglan.

“One other thing,” said Qi Tingjun. “He volunteered it. We hadn’t asked. He says the Tianyuan Mahjong Assembly’s preliminaries begin in Nanchao in half a month. Before the man who uses his left hand came to Yunchuan, he had been in the south.”

He put the paper away.

“A dispatch says an order from Tiandu will arrive tomorrow morning.” He looked at Qinglan. “Most likely they’re recalling me to escort the prisoners.”

He stood beside the door a moment longer, looked once more at Qinglan’s torn sleeve and left without another word.

Gently, Qinglan eased his sleeve from Xingyu’s hand.

Her brows pinched, but she did not wake.

He went into the rear garden. A broad swathe of snowwatch herb had been cut. What remained bowed in the night breeze, the white down beneath its leaves faintly luminous against the dark earth.

The moon had just passed the southeast. The seventeenth night’s moon, a small sliver short of full, hung over the city battlements and left the rear garden half in light, half in shadow. He looked up towards the northwest. The Dipper hung low above the rooftops there, seven stars, its handle tipped upward.

He took the White Dragon from inside his robe.

On its back, below the heel of the engraved little hand, seven tiny pinholes held a faint gleam beneath the stars.

He stood motionless.

A little warmth seeped from the White Dragon into his chest and travelled slowly downward. He closed his eyes and looked inward at the two bamboo stalks.

The slender stalk of the first strand was bare. Still not a single leaf.

At the tip of the second strand’s shoot, the white had receded. A little green emerged beneath it, faint as the tip of a bud just breaking earth. But it was green.

One thread.

Two of Bamboo, newly born.

He opened his eyes.

The door curtain stirred behind him. Yuening stood in the doorway with her ledger in her arms. A needle lay between its pages, threaded with a length of blue-green silk.

“Xingyu’s sewing basket,” she said. “I saw the handkerchief while I was tidying it. She got the bamboo straight.”

She set her ledger on the stone step beneath the veranda and came to stand beside him, looking at the Dipper.

“In my ledger,” she said, “I wrote your name today.”

“I saw.”

“You saw?”

“This morning. You wrote ‘Qinglan’.”

Yuening said nothing. After a while she said, “Your game records are too ugly. From now on, I’ll write them myself.”

She turned, picked up the ledger and went inside.

Qinglan remained in the rear garden. The Dipper in the northwest. The moon in the southeast. Snowwatch leaves rustling at his feet.

(End of Chapter Seven)
