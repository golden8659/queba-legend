Chapter 7The Patient at the Apothecary

At the apothecary in the east of the city, medicine cabinets covered an entire wall. Hundreds of small drawers, each with a faded red-paper label. A bitter herbal smell filled the back room.

The girls from the workshop had carried Xingyu there before dawn. When Qinglan and the others arrived, the back room was packed with young embroiderers, red-eyed and scarcely daring to breathe. On the bed beneath the window lay a young woman of eighteen or nineteen, her face paper-white, her lips blue. Even under two quilts, she shivered.

Xingyu.

The embroiderers had brought the little bamboo basket beside her pillow. Inside lay a half-finished handkerchief. Bamboo again, but much straighter than on the one she had given Qinglan.

Lan Ruoheng was already there. Two fingers rested on Xingyu's wrist, her brow never relaxing. A long time passed before she withdrew her hand.

“Not a sudden illness,” she said. “Poison. Chill Cicada Powder. Put in food. The victim grows colder day by day. When the cold reaches the heart, they never wake again.”

She lifted Xingyu's eyelid for them to see. Fine blue threads crept across the white of her eye.

“This poison is slow. Made to drag things out.” Lan Ruoheng's voice was cold. “Whoever gave it does not want her to die quickly. He wants her to grow cold slowly. For someone to watch.”

Zhumo's ears drooped. Yuening stood at the foot of the bed with her ledger, lips pressed white.

“Can you cure it?” Tao Lingtao's voice shook.

“I don’t have an antidote formula yet.” Lan Ruoheng shook her head. “First hold back the cold, then investigate what she ate. Any medicine brought here must be identified too. We can’t trust someone just because they say it will save her.”

A woman in her early twenties entered, sleeves rolled up, mud on the hem of her skirt. In her arms was a bundle of green herbs still wet with dew.

“Tang Yingtang, from the flower garden west of the city,” Lan Ruoheng said. “She grows snowwatch herb.”

Tang Yingtang put down the herbs and quickly pounded them to juice. Lifting Xingyu, she fed it to her a little at a time. Slowly, her shaking eased.

“Snowwatch herb can hold the cold down. It cannot drive it out.” Tang Yingtang wiped her hands and looked up at them. “At most, until the Xu watch tonight.”

Yuening froze with her abacus in mid-air. “Twelve hours left.”

◆

“What did she eat?” Qinglan asked.

A young embroiderer answered between sniffles. “Yesterday evening, someone left a box of sweet osmanthus cakes at the door. They said… they said Xingyu's father had sent them.”

Everyone stared.

“Her father?” Jinshi's eyes widened. “Aren't all these girls orphans?”

All eyes turned to Tao Lingtao.

She sat beside the bed, holding Xingyu's icy hand. It was a long time before she spoke.

“Xingyu's family name is Mo. Her father is Mochuan, the proprietor of Hengchang Trading House.”

The room went dead silent.

“Two years ago, her mother died of illness. Hengchang changed too. Strangers appeared around Proprietor Mo. The trading house began winning land in sworn matches of honour, piece by piece,” Tao Lingtao said. “Xingyu was frightened. She ran away in the middle of the night and came to me. She knelt and begged me to take her in. I changed her clothes, gave her a new name and let her disappear among the other girls. She has been hidden for two years.”

“It was raining hard that night.” Tao Lingtao looked out of the window. “She was soaked through. The only thing she carried was a handkerchief her mother had embroidered with a clump of bamboo. She said her mother loved bamboo best, because it stood for integrity. She wanted to learn embroidery. Once she could do it, she would make one just like it and burn it as an offering to her mother.”

Qinglan's hand moved instinctively to his robe. The crooked bamboo-embroidered handkerchief was still there.

“She has embroidered for two years,” Tao Lingtao said softly. “It always comes out crooked. That day at the Rain Pavilion, she gave you the crookedest one of all. She said you had played for the workshop, and she had nothing else to give.”

She lowered her head. Tears fell on the back of Xingyu's hand.

“That day, in the Rain Pavilion, I knelt before everyone. I was pleading for a home for twenty-odd girls. I was pleading for her too. If the waterway went and the workshop scattered, she could no longer hide.”

Qinglan remembered Tao Lingtao's forehead against the flagstones. Remembered her words: “The workshop is their home.” That act of kneeling had carried more than he had known.

◆

The apothecary door burst open.

A man of about fifty rushed in, his brocade robe crumpled, his topknot half undone, his eyes bloodshot from lack of sleep. The moment he saw Xingyu on the bed, his knees gave way. He caught the doorframe to keep from falling.

“Yu'er…”

Mochuan.

Jinshi stepped in front of him like a wall. Mochuan did not look at him. He stared at his daughter, his lips shaking too much to speak. His fist was clenched. A corner of paper protruded from it.

Qinglan looked at the fist.

“That letter Master Mo is holding,” he said, “are they using her to make you do something?”

Mochuan shuddered.

Mochuan looked away and forced a nod.

“They want me to accuse you,” he said at last. “Say Hushan was your stand-in and the Rain Pavilion was a show you staged together. Only then will they give me the medicine.”

Qinglan’s nails dug into his palm. This time there was no need to guess what they wanted written.

Mochuan's fist opened. The paper drifted to the floor. Yuening picked it up. One glance drained her face of colour.

Before the Xu watch tonight, go to the Bureau of Balance and accuse Qinglan.

Fail to go, or get one word wrong, and your daughter need never wake.

“I… I have no other way.” Mochuan's voice rasped like sandpaper. “For two years, Hengchang hasn't been mine. They wanted land, I gave them land. People, I gave them people. I only begged them not to touch her. I thought they couldn't find her here…”

“Couldn't find her?” Jinshi seized his collar and lifted him until his toes left the ground. “Your Hengchang men smashed my lion! Poisoned Miss Luo's hand! Forced a boy at the academy to work a fan! And now you tell me all you wanted was for them to leave your daughter alone? What about everyone else's children?”

Mochuan did not struggle. He hung in Jinshi's grip like sodden clothing, looking only at his daughter on the bed, tears running down his face.

“Brother.” Qinglan rested a hand on Jinshi's arm.

Breathing heavily, Jinshi glared at Mochuan for a long time. At last he released him. Mochuan fell to the floor and did not rise.

◆

Just then, something struck the window with a thunk.

A bone tile was embedded in the lattice, its back facing outwards. A folded note was pinned beneath it.

Lu Zhaoye sprang out of the window and looked around. The alley was empty. Only wind.

Qinglan pulled out the tile and turned it over. Two of Dots.

The paper held only a few lines.

The Wu watch. The abandoned dye works west of the city. One hand under Tuidao School rules.

You win: the antidote.

You lose: the White Dragon.

No Bureau of Balance.

“A trap!” Jinshi snatched the paper. “You go, and you think they'll still give you the antidote?”

“A private game forced on someone with a patient’s life doesn’t become a sworn match of honour just because they write ‘Tuidao School’ on it,” Yuening said, biting her lip. “We can record what he does while we’re there. We can’t promise he’ll hand over the medicine.”

Qinglan put the Two of Dots and the note back on the table, without putting them into his White Dragon’s wrapping.

“Ask Master Xiao to come. Zhumo, keep the original paper and the game record. Verify what can be verified; record the coercion too,” he said. “We can’t hand over the White Dragon first. And she can’t just stay here waiting for me.”

He turned to Mochuan. “Take this letter to the captain. Say what you saw yourself. Don’t admit to things you never saw for them. Ask him to protect the shop and Xingyu too.”

Mochuan looked at him as though he were a drowning man's last hope. And as though he were mad.

◆

Before they left, Lu Zhaoye stopped Qinglan. “I'll get Qi Tingjun. Have his men lie in wait outside.”

“Go,” Qinglan said. “Have him protect the apothecary and investigate whoever delivered the cakes and the letter. The abandoned dye works may only be somewhere to draw us away.”

Lu Zhaoye stared at him. “Aren’t you afraid they’ll say you broke the agreement?”

“I never agreed not to seek help.” Qinglan looked at the paper. “What he wrote doesn’t become my promise.”

Lu Zhaoye memorized the time written there and left through the window.

In the apothecary's back room, Tang Yingtang pounded another bowl of snowwatch herb. Yuening glanced back before leaving. Water fell in the water clock, drop by drop.

The Wu watch. The abandoned dye works west of the city.

The great dye vats had long dried out, blackened indigo sediment crusting their bottoms. Half the roof had collapsed. Sunlight poured through the hole onto a square wooden table with peeling paint in the centre of the room.

But Jinshi stopped inside the doorway and looked down at the stone platform beneath the table. Old copper boundaries were set into its four corners, still unbroken.

“I’ve danced the lion here before.” His voice lowered. “Before it became a dye works, it was the western guildhall’s spirit-binding hall.”

Yuening crouched along the platform and brushed dust from the copper. An old site number appeared, matching the copy she carried from the city’s register of grounds. She checked each corner. When she rose, indigo mud coated her hand.

“The ground is still here,” she said. “But no one authorized you to force people with poison.”

Someone was already in the North seat.

Tall. Gaunt. A pallid face. Ten long, slender fingers turned a bone tile over and over.

“Scholar.” Hu Shan grinned, showing yellow teeth. “We meet again.”

A black-clad man sat in the South seat, head lowered, saying nothing.

Qinglan glanced at him. His hands rested flat on the table, all ten fingers motionless. His face held no expression. He scarcely even blinked.

Qinglan could not read a man like that. An unreadable man was ten times more dangerous than one he could read.

“Let this be clear first.” Xiao Cheng’an stood beside the table, leaning on his stick. “This is a private game forced by poison. I will not stamp it with the seal of honour. We record what you say and hand over as it happens. This hand cannot erase the old account at the Rain Pavilion.”

Hushan snorted and held the medicine bottle beside him without handing it over.

Yuening counted the one hundred and thirty-six tiles before them. “At the table we judge wins under Tuidao School rules: pungs and kongs allowed, no chows; wild tiles may replace missing tiles. One hand only. Holding the medicine doesn’t make this a sworn match of honour.”

“And the White Dragon is not a stake,” Qinglan said.

Hushan’s smile thinned. He set the ordinary bone tile from his fingers outside the table, then took another old tile from inside his robe. His palm hid its face, but a faint line of light shone between his fingers.

“I ran errands for people for years to get this one.” Hushan hid the light in his palm, staring at the red cloth inside Qinglan’s robe. “You had yours ready-made, left by your father.”

Qinglan did not loosen his grip.

“Do you know what my father paid to leave it to me?”

Hushan’s fingers stopped for a moment.

“Then let’s play as tile-bearers do,” he said. “The loser pays spirit energy. Dare you let it take part?”

Through the red cloth, Qinglan pressed the White Dragon. The medicine had not been handed over; sitting down guaranteed no rescue. But Hushan could not keep speaking for what Qinglan would do with his own tile.

“First say how much, and who pays.” He looked at Yuening. “Don’t count my senior brother as a seat that takes a loss for me.”

Hu Shan looked past Qinglan. “Where's the player opposite you?”

“Here!”

Jinshi set down the lion head and dropped into the West seat. The chair gave an agonised creak.

Hu Shan stared, then laughed aloud. “The lion-dance master? Worst player in the city? Scholar, you've come to hand over the White Dragon.”

The four rolled dice to determine the opposing axes for the two sides, then each rolled for seats within the side, ties rerolled. Hushan and the black-clad man took North and South; Qinglan and Jinshi took East and West. Only when the waiting chairs had been moved back to the table were their seats settled.

Jinshi untied a stone tile wrapped in old cloth from his belt.

He opened the cloth and brought his palm around it. The familiar suit aspect appeared on the stone.

“I know how to steady myself on the posts with it.” Jinshi looked at Yuening. “But first tell me how you set stakes here, and who pays whom. I’m on Little Lan’s side—do I pay him too?”

The black-clad man in South said nothing. He merely held another tile of his own in his palm and let its aspect rise. Yuening checked the response each person had brought forth, seat by seat, while Zhumo recorded names and positions. They did not mix those divine objects into the one hundred and thirty-six game tiles on the table.

Zhumo looked at Qinglan. “In the compacts I’ve copied, you seal your own stakes and pay your own losses. Whether your tile will answer you, I can’t say for it.”

Qinglan gathered his breath as his master had taught him, slowly guiding the strength he could usually sustain towards the White Dragon.

Coolness travelled a short way along the base of his fingers. A green line appeared along the tile’s edge, held together and slowly gathered towards its centre. Two slender Bamboo marks emerged.

Two of Bamboo.

His fingertips tightened slightly. His master had long made him test how to sustain the breath of that early stage, and he knew it himself. But for the first time, the White Dragon he had carried since childhood reflected it.

Yuening lowered her head and recorded the aspect she saw, without adding an advancement he had not made.

Qinglan slowly drew his energy back. The two Bamboo marks faded; in his palm remained the old bone-white tile, the worn marks on its corners unchanged.

After watching it a moment, he drew energy into it again. Two of Bamboo rose once more. This time he did not hurry to withdraw.

The light in Jinshi’s palm held steady. He did not hurry Qinglan, only brought the lion head a little closer to his feet.

Yuening compared the amounts each had brought forth. “The base stakes are equal. An ordinary win costs one stake; closed hand or pure suit costs two, with no stacking above that. On a discard win, the discarder pays. On self-draw, all three others pay.”

She looked at Jinshi. “You too.”

Jinshi pressed his lips together and looked down at his stone tile.

Hearing that even a teammate truly paid, he glanced at Qinglan, then still pressed the tile towards the copper boundary at his own corner.

She asked each of the four separately whether they could bear it. Jinshi looked at his stone tile and nodded, without letting Qinglan answer for him.

Zhumo added two conditions at the end of the compact: no divine tiles staked, no debt of future stakes. He laid it out for all four to read.

In turn, each drew energy and sent two stakes into the copper boundary before their seat. The faint light at the four corners closed, one after another. When the final line lit, Hushan lifted his hand, but the strand of energy did not return.

“All sealed. No one can withdraw alone now.” Yuening watched him. “A winning hand is shown and checked first, then payments follow what we just agreed. A drawn hand returns each person’s stakes.”

Hushan put his hand back on the table edge. “Deal.”

“I’ll record the payments and how this game was forced.” Yuening held down the paper’s corner. “The copper can hold these stakes. It can’t cancel what you’ve done.”

Qinglan placed the White Dragon separately on its red cloth. The gain or loss of this hand would be recorded under his name, not as some cultivation the whole side gained from nothing.

His breathing settled, but the cough from the apothecary remained in his mind. He looked around the table, first remembering all four positions. The White Dragon revealed no one’s concealed hand. What lay beneath Hushan’s fingers still had to be played, shown, or inferred from tiles already seen.

Before play began, he turned towards Jinshi and spoke loudly enough for everyone to hear.

“Brother. Today, you pung only once. The first tile you can pung, take it. After that, not a single pung.”

Jinshi's eyes widened. “Just once? That's torture!”

“Endure it.”

◆

The shuffle. The building of the wall. Hu Shan's ten fingers swept through the tiles, and the entire set obeyed. Yuening watched the stretch of wall he had built and quietly wrote in the margin of her ledger:

“North wall. Middle tight.”

She recognized the unusual tightness, but did not write “stacked wall.” What had been verified at the Rain Pavilion was an outside tile. That conclusion could not testify for this game.

Qinglan saw Hushan’s eyes move several times from that stretch of wall back to North. If he really had arranged a sequence for normal draws, the skipped draw caused by a pung would disturb it. Whether that worked still depended on how the play unfolded.

Yuening reached out and revealed the third tile from the end of the wall.

A Two of Dots.

“Two of Dots will act as a wild tile.”

East, Qinglan, began with one extra tile and had thirteen after his first discard. Each other seat held thirteen. The remaining eighty-three formed the wall; the Two of Dots indicator stayed where it was, never drawn into a hand and counted only once. Jinshi arranged his thirteen tiles, squinting for a while as his grin slowly broadened. “Well now, three pairs! Plenty I can pung…”

Halfway through, he saw Qinglan's look. His voice dwindled. “…Just once. Only once.”

The bone tile turning between Hu Shan's fingers paused.

“I thought about that hand at the Rain Pavilion for three nights.” Hushan slapped a bone tile on the table and stared at Qinglan. “Why did you stop the game just then?”

“You’d finished discarding, but still had an extra tile.” Qinglan watched his sleeve. “I didn’t see what you were thinking. The tiles just didn’t lie with you.”

Hushan’s face twitched, then he smiled again. “Count carefully today.”

◆

First turn. Second turn.

On the third turn, the black-clad man in the South seat discarded a Nine of Characters.

“Pung!”

Jinshi's great hand struck the table, making a tea bowl jump. He scooped up the Nine of Characters.

Yuening saw that West already held two Nine of Characters, which formed a pung with the one discarded by South. Jinshi skipped a draw and discarded first. Three pung tiles lay exposed; ten remained in hand, thirteen in total. Next, North, Hushan, took his normal draw.

Hu Shan reached for his tile. The instant his fingertips touched it, his face changed.

A One of Characters. He remembered perfectly. This tile should have belonged to that stupid lion in the West seat.

Wrong.

Jinshi’s pung took no tile from the wall, but North then drew the tile originally meant for West. Until another pung or kong changed things, the seats taking subsequent tiles were shifted. Tiles originally arranged for North’s turns would pass to East.

His next player was East.

Qinglan drew a tile, glanced at it and added it to his hand. Seven of Bamboo.

Another turn. Another draw. Eight of Bamboo.

Yuening’s heart pounded. On both of East’s draws, Hushan’s gaze followed closely. She recorded only the order of the draws and his looks. Before the faces were revealed, she could not write them as Seven or Eight of Bamboo, much less call the entire stretch of wall Bamboo tiles.

Hu Shan tapped the edge of the table. Once. Again. He glanced at the sunlight coming through the roof. The shadows had shifted. Eight hours until the Xu watch that evening. Eight hours until he had to report back.

For the first time, the man in the South seat lifted his head and looked at Hu Shan. The look said nothing, yet sent a chill down Hu Shan's spine.

◆

Sweat broke out on Hu Shan's brow. To restore the order, he had to call pung himself, or make someone else do it.

He held a pair of White Dragons, waiting for someone to discard the third.

Qinglan happened to have a White Dragon. He glanced at Hu Shan, kept it and discarded an East Wind.

Hu Shan gritted his teeth and tried another way. He discarded a Five of Characters.

Jinshi's eyes lit up. He held a pair of Five of Characters tiles.

“Pu—”

The word reached his lips. He forced it back down. His hand closed into a fist on the table, knuckles white, veins standing out across his brow. His whole face turned purple with the effort.

Today, you pung only once.

“…Pass.”

He squeezed the word between his teeth.

Zhumo’s brush paused. Beside the record, he added a small note: “West held back a pung.”

The black-clad man in South glanced at Hushan several times without calling pung. Qinglan could not read his expression and did not take his silence as safety. If he could avoid breaking a shape, he did. From the sixth through the tenth turn, the wall’s draw order did not change again.

◆

The eleventh turn.

Apart from the Two of Dots acting as a wild tile, Qinglan now held only Bamboo: One-Two-Three, Four-Five-Six, Seven-Eight-Nine, a pair of Three, one Seven, and the wild Two of Dots.

He reached out, drew a tile and passed his thumb lightly across its face.

“Self-draw.”

He laid down his hand. The final tile was a Seven of Bamboo.

Yuening checked the hand. “One-Two-Three, Four-Five-Six, Seven-Eight-Nine of Bamboo; a pair of Three of Bamboo; two Sevens with Two of Dots as a third Seven to form a pung. Fourteen tiles. Pure-suit, closed-hand self-draw. This game uses the single higher rate: two stakes. Each of the other three seats pays two.”

Hu Shan stared at the Seven of Bamboo.

“This tile,” Qinglan gently pushed it forward, keeping it on the tabletop where all four could see it, “I drew. Now bring out the medicine.”

◆

For a long time, no one spoke in the abandoned dye works.

Yuening checked each group in the exposed hand while Zhumo set down the winning shape and payments. The copper boundaries at the four corners reflected the face-up tiles. Three sealed streams of energy turned towards Qinglan.

Hushan clamped his old tile, trying to pull his own strand back. The copper light drew taut beneath his fingers, then still crossed along the sealed line. His smile finally vanished. South’s hand also stopped on the table edge, his eyes fixed on the White Dragon.

When Jinshi rose, the lion head he could usually lift in one breath took a pause to steady. He gritted his teeth and did not ask Yuening to strike out his payment.

Qinglan’s palm grew hot. His own two sealed stakes returned to the White Dragon first, followed closely by the other three streams. Yuening recorded six stakes; her brush paused for a moment beside Jinshi’s name, then touched down all the same.

“It’s shown itself.” Hushan suddenly smiled. “This trip wasn’t wasted.”

Qinglan’s fingers tightened. Whose purpose those words served, he did not yet know.

At last Hushan pushed forward the little black porcelain bottle he had held beside him.

“Take it back. Don’t wait till the xu watch.”

Yuening did not record that he had “kept his word.” Whether the medicine worked required an answer from the apothecary. Nor would the poisoning disappear because he handed over a bottle.

She took it first and tucked it safely inside her robe. Qinglan did not grab Hushan’s wrist; he moved between her and South.

Hushan sprang up, knocking his chair backwards. The black-clad man in South moved faster, kicking over the table and scattering tiles with a crash. Jinshi blocked the flying tiles while Qinglan shielded Yuening back to the doorway. She ran out first, clutching the bottle and heading for the apothecary. By the time Jinshi pushed past the overturned table, both men had climbed through the hole in the roof.

Lu Zhaoye chased them for two streets. When he returned, he was breathless and shaking his head. “Gone again.”

“He shouted back as he climbed onto the roof,” Lu Zhaoye said. “He said, ‘Scholar, you've beaten me twice. But remember: the one sitting at the mahjong table has never been me.’”

Qinglan fell silent. Whether the woven hat, the “master” in the letter and the people Hushan spoke of belonged to the same chain would take comparison of original papers and living witnesses, point by point. He did not supply a face for someone he had never seen.

He did not pursue. Zhumo first recorded the three actual payments, the moment the bottle was handed over and who had kicked the table, then collected and counted the scattered tiles with Master Xiao. Only after the Seven of Bamboo drawn last had been wrapped separately and recorded as a tile retained after the disrupted game did Qinglan put it inside his robe. It was an ordinary game tile, not something he could pass off as a Heavenly Tile.

Jinshi slumped in his chair, clutching his chest. It took him a long time to recover his breath.

“Xiaolan… Never been so miserable in my life.”

Qinglan looked at him and smiled. “Brother, that ‘pass’ was better than every pung you have ever called.”

◆

Yuening ran across half the city, her hair coming loose. By the time she burst into the apothecary's back room, the water in the clock had fallen a long way farther.

Lan Ruoheng received the porcelain bottle without immediately giving Xingyu any. She had the remaining osmanthus-cake crumbs and the medicine kept separately, identified the medicine first, then tried treatment according to the girl’s present pulse and condition. Qinglan asked twice; neither time did she promise certain recovery.

Only when Xingyu’s tightly clenched hand slowly relaxed and her breathing steadied did Lan Ruoheng wipe the sweat from her forehead.

“It’s working,” she said. “She still needs watching tonight. Someone who delivers medicine isn’t necessarily someone we can trust.”

The embroiderers all burst into tears. Luo Jinke, still cradling her bandaged wrist, pushed to the front, laughing and crying. Tang Yingtang leaned against a medicine cabinet and let out a long breath. Only then did she notice that her palms were wet with sweat.

◆

A quarter of an hour before the Xu watch that evening, Xingyu woke.

She opened her eyes and saw Tao Lingtao first, then Mochuan beside the bed, weeping helplessly. She stared at her father for a long time, then whispered, “Father.”

Mochuan collapsed against the bedside, his shoulders shaking like a child's.

Xingyu turned again and saw Qinglan at the door. After a moment’s thought, she took the half-embroidered handkerchief from beside her pillow and held it out to him.

“This one… is straight.”

Qinglan took it, keeping his fingertips clear of the loose thread.

“I see,” he said.

Jinshi stood by the door, struggling for a long time. At last he could no longer hold it in. He addressed Mochuan in a deep, muffled voice. “Hengchang owes me the eye from my lion.”

Mochuan nodded hurriedly. “Of course. Of course. I'll pay for a pair in gold.”

“Don't want gold.” Jinshi stiffened his neck. “Want the old one.”

Everyone laughed.

Late that night, Mochuan rose to leave. Tao Lingtao saw him to the apothecary door. They stood on opposite sides of the threshold for a long time. Neither spoke first.

“Xingyu,” Mochuan said at last. “Let her stay with you.”

“She is your daughter.”

“With you,” Mochuan said with a bitter smile, “she can be who she wants to be.”

He bowed deeply to Tao Lingtao and walked into the night.

That night Mochuan went to the Bureau of Balance again. This time he did not bring a threatening letter to seek help. He recounted, one by one, the things that had passed through his hands over the past two years.

The original paper, cake box and medicine bottle each had their own provenance. What he had seen was recorded as witnessed; what he had heard included who had told him. Who had administered the poison remained under investigation. His tears did not clear his own account.

At dawn, Qi Tingjun came to the Rain Pavilion. He laid a densely written statement before Qinglan and pointed to its final line.

Ghost Hand's next target: Pinghe Hall in Nanchao. In eighteen days, Pinghe Hall will hold the Tianyuan Mahjong Assembly Qualifiers.

“Ghost Hand moves against Pinghe Hall. Next comes the Tianyuan Mahjong Assembly.” Qi Tingjun looked at Qinglan. “Are you going to Nanchao?”

Qinglan took out the Dipper gold coin he had kept and laid it on the table with the Seven of Bamboo and the two green-bamboo handkerchiefs. Before leaving, he put the coin back inside his robe and packed the tile and handkerchiefs separately.

“Yes.”

(End of Chapter Seven)
