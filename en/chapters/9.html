Chapter 9Fast Play

Early on the eleventh morning, the boat entered Nanchao Harbour.

Pei Zhiwei shook Jinshi awake. After ten days of seasickness, his face was still green. He clung to the rail and peered over. One look, and his mouth fell open. For a long time, it would not close.

The sea.

Not Yunchuan's Three-Coloured River. Not the Great River they had travelled down. An entire sea without a visible end, grey-blue, rising and falling in the morning light like a vast beast breathing in its sleep. More vessels lay in the harbour than there were rooftops in all of Yunchuan. The largest stood three storeys high, their sails like city walls; the smallest were mere slivers, fishermen standing at their sterns to work the sculls, the creak of wood mingling all around. Mast beside mast, dense as a leafless forest.

Seabirds darted between them, crying high and clear.

“Nanchao.” Pei Zhiwei stood in the bow and drew a deep breath of salt wind. “The greatest port in the world.”

Gongs and drums were sounding on the quay as they came ashore.

A large ship newly returned from a distant voyage lay at the foremost berth. Its owner and his family stood on the gangplank while a lion-dance troupe welcomed them home. Nine plum-blossom posts stood in a row on the quay, far taller than those outside the Rain Pavilion in Yunchuan. The highest was fully twice a man's height.

A blue-green lion wheeled and leapt across them. It did not jump with the golden lion's measured, steady steps. It flew. Before it had settled on one post, the next was already beneath its feet: so fast that all one saw was a blue-green blur. At the highest post it hung upside down, opened its jaws and seized the string of red gift packets the shipowner had hung from the mast.

The whole quay erupted in cheers.

The lion head lifted. Beneath it was a young man of twenty-five or twenty-six, with sharp brows, thin lips and close-fitting blue-green clothes. Sweat beaded his forehead, but his eyes were cold. They swept the crowd and came to rest on Jinshi at the rail, holding the golden lion head with the golden scar across its brow.

The young man's mouth moved. Perhaps a smile. Perhaps not.

“A lion from out of town,” he called. “With a scar on its face.”

Jinshi straightened at once, seasickness forgotten. “What's wrong with a scar?”

“A scar means you've lost.” The young man swung the lion head onto his shoulder and turned away. “Nanchao's lions don't lose.”

“You stop right there!”

But he had already vanished into the crowd with his troupe, gongs and drums sounding as they went.

“Huo Xiao,” Pei Zhiwei said quietly. “Heir to the Huo family lion-dance tradition in Nanchao. No one in the harbour dares challenge him on the posts.”

Jinshi glared after him, fists cracking. His face was no longer green.

◆

Beyond the landing, a long flagstone road led from the quay into the city.

Zhumo went first to deliver the work to the bookshop’s branch. During the ten days aboard, he had copied diagrams by daylight, stopping to check pages when the waves rose. The final volume’s ink had dried only that morning. The proprietor sampled two books and paid the balance. Only after settling his debt to the bindery and handing Yuening the thirty-eight taels left net would he sit down for tea.

Arcaded buildings lined both sides. Thick columns supported the second and third floors, leaving a long covered walkway below, shaded from sun and sheltered from rain. Shop followed shop beneath the arcade: sea goods, spices, silks, self-striking clocks from overseas, salted dried fish. Strings of fish hung swaying in the wind.

Yuening kept turning her head as she walked, her abacus beads clicking without pause.

“What are you calculating now?” Qinglan asked.

“Prices,” Yuening said. “A jin of cured dried fish costs fifteen coppers in Yunchuan, ten here. A bolt of imported floral silk costs a gold coin in Yunchuan, only six hundred coppers here.” She paused. “Things are cheaper in Nanchao, and people walk faster. Look—no one’s moving slowly.”

Qinglan looked. She was right. People with carrying poles, handcarts, bales on their shoulders or birdcages in their hands all seemed to have wind at their heels. Even the two old men playing a board game beside the road slapped their pieces down—clack, clack, clack—as if racing each other.

“People in Nanchao do everything quickly,” Pei Zhiwei said with a smile. “The tide waits for no one. Cargo must come in with it, ships must sail out with it. A quarter of an hour late, and you wait another half-day.”

Lu Zhaoye crouched atop an arcade column, tail dangling and swaying. “What about mahjong?”

“Mahjong,” Pei Zhiwei said, “is faster still.”

◆

The first place Pei Zhiwei took them was a morning teahouse.

Chaosheng Teahouse stood at the corner where two arcaded streets met. Three storeys, a string of red lanterns at its door. Dawn had barely broken, yet it was already full. Waiters wheeled little carts between the tables, bamboo steamers piled high on each. Whenever a lid came off, steam billowed through the room.

“One pot, two dishes,” Pei Zhiwei called to a waiter with easy familiarity. “A pot of tea and two kinds of dim sum. That's the Nanchao way.”

But far more than two dishes arrived.

Prawn dumplings in translucent wrappers, the pink prawns visible inside; open-topped dumplings crowned with a dab of crab roe; chicken feet braised until the bones slipped free at a touch; rice-noodle rolls so silky the chopsticks could barely hold them; and a basket of golden, fluffy steamed sponge cake, fragrant with sweetness.

Zhumo held a prawn dumpling and examined it from every side, reluctant to bite.

Jinshi had already swallowed three. Ten days of seasickness seemed cured. “Brother Pei! These prawn dumplings are even better than yours!”

Pei Zhiwei took no offence. He picked one up, bit off half and chewed for a long time with narrowed eyes.

“Wheat starch in the wrapper. A little lard mixed in. The prawns came ashore this morning. Crushed, not chopped.” He set down his chopsticks as though entering something in a mental ledger. “Ten years making them. And all this time, the difference was that one press of the blade.”

After they ate, the waiter came to settle up: two coppers for tea, ten for each of seven dishes of dim sum. Seventy-two coppers altogether.

Yuening took coppers from the change exchanged before they came ashore and counted one by one. At seventy she stopped. “Ten each for seven makes seventy, and two for tea is seventy-two. Correct.” She pushed the coins over, then added, “But our chicken feet had two fewer than that table’s.”

The waiter stared, then went red-faced to the kitchen and brought two more.

Jinshi was awestruck. “Miss Yuening, you even count chicken feet?”

“A scorekeeper's rule.” Yuening put away her abacus. “If it's on the table, count it.”

Qinglan held his tea bowl without eating.

He was listening.

Several nearby tables had mahjong games in progress. The sound of the tiles was different. In Yunchuan, they fell slowly, one by one: clack—clack. Here it was clack-clack-clack-clack, like rain.

And there was a sound he had never heard before.

“Chow!”

◆

Pinghe Hall allowed chow.

If the previous player discarded a tile that completed a sequence with two in your hand, you could take it and lay the sequence face up on the table. No tile was revealed to determine a wild tile. No insistence on a concealed hand. Even a basic winning hand could win. Everything came down to speed.

“A Tuidao School hand generally lasts a dozen turns or so,” Zhumo explained quietly to Qinglan, swallowing his last mouthful of prawn dumpling. “At Pinghe Hall, seven or eight turns is quite usual. A quick hand ends in five. I've copied their game records until my paw ached. Sometimes, before I'd finished recording one turn, they'd already finished the next.”

Qinglan nodded without speaking.

Pinghe Hall was why they had come to Nanchao. Mochuan's confession said: Ghost Hand's next target, Pinghe Hall in Nanchao; in seventeen days, Pinghe Hall would hold the Tianyuan Mahjong Assembly Qualifiers. Three days waiting for a boat, ten days on the water. Only four remained. Before the qualifiers, they had to see Jinghe, Hall Master of Pinghe Hall, and tell him Ghost Hand was coming.

“But getting through their door isn't easy,” Pei Zhiwei said. “I've asked around. An outsider who wants to see the Hall Master must first pass an ‘entry trial’.”

“An entry trial?”

“A match. A Pinghe Hall disciple serves as dealer. Best of three hands. Win, and you're admitted. Lose—” Pei Zhiwei paused. “You go home and come back next month.”

“Next month?” Yuening's abacus fell silent. “The qualifiers are in four days!”

◆

Pinghe Hall's headquarters stood directly opposite Chaosheng Teahouse.

Its five-bay arcaded building rose above its neighbours. Over the door hung a black plaque with gold lettering: Pinghe. Two stone lions stood at the entrance, worn smooth by decades of sea wind.

Just inside was a mahjong table. A young woman sat beside it, shelling peanuts with her head down.

She was in her early twenties, in a red dress worked with gold-thread peonies, wide-sleeved and long-skirted; when she walked, she moved like a flame. Her hair was pulled high and tied with red cord. She tossed a peanut into her mouth and looked up. Her bright, quick eyes swept the arrivals and settled on Qinglan.

“From out of town?”

“Yunchuan.”

“Tuidao School?”

Qinglan blinked. “How did you know?”

“The way you walk.” She pushed the shells aside. “Tuidao people all walk slowly. As though the ground were paved with tiles and they had to read every one before stepping on it.”

Jinshi snorted with laughter. Yuening glared at him.

“I'm Liuxia. Pinghe Hall disciple. On the door today.” She stood, half a head taller than Yuening. “Want to see the Hall Master? Sit. Best of three.”

◆

The four rolled dice for seats, ties rerolled. Liuxia sat East, Qinglan North—immediately before her in turn order.

The other two players were young Pinghe Hall disciples, one stout, one thin. They grinned at Liuxia as they sat down.

“Pinghe Hall’s rules.” Liuxia pushed the tiles into the centre and all four shuffled. “One hundred and thirty-six tiles, no wild indicator. Chow from the player before you; pungs and kongs allowed. Even a basic hand counts. In every hand, East begins with an extra tile and discards first. We three guard the gate: if any of us wins first, the gate wins a hand. Only if you win first does it count for you. First to two wins; drawn hands are reshuffled without counting a win. No gold, no spirit energy. Only this meeting is at stake.”

She looked at Qinglan again. “You’re my preceding player. If a suited tile you discard makes a sequence with two in my hand, I can chow it. Not winds or dragons. Don’t think I take everything.”

◆

The first hand.

Qinglan's thirteen starting tiles were neither good nor bad: the makings of two sequences, three incomplete melds, a pair of East Winds. In the Tuidao School, he would work such a hand patiently. Clear the isolated tiles first. Let the incomplete melds develop into two-sided waits. Then wait on the widest choice of winning tiles, and finally win by self-draw.

Liuxia first discarded Nine of Characters. South and West each drew and discarded before North’s turn. Qinglan drew, then discarded North Wind on his first turn.

Liuxia drew and discarded a Nine of Dots.

On the second turn, Qinglan discarded an isolated Three of Characters. Tuidao practice: the sooner an isolated tile went, the better. Keeping it only took up space.

“Chow!”

Her hand moved faster than his eyes. She snatched up the Three of Characters and exposed the One and Two of Characters from her hand. One, Two, Three of Characters. A sequence slapped onto the table.

Qinglan stared.

After the chow, Liuxia casually discarded a North Wind.

On the third turn, he discarded a Seven of Bamboo.

“Chow!”

Six, Seven, Eight of Bamboo. Another sequence. Liuxia discarded an East Wind.

Qinglan frowned. He wanted to read Liuxia’s hand, but she had discarded only four tiles: Nine of Characters, Nine of Dots, North and East. Those were not enough to identify her remaining seven uniquely. Her two exposed sequences were plain to see, but those were what she had chosen to show.

On the fourth turn, Liuxia drew a tile, slipped it into her hand without a glance and discarded a White Dragon.

“Ready,” the stout disciple said with a grin. “Our senior fellow disciple Liuxia is ready to win.”

Only the fourth turn.

Qinglan stared at the table, thoughts racing. She had chowed One-Two-Three of Characters and Six-Seven-Eight of Bamboo, leaving seven in hand. She had discarded Nine of Characters, Nine of Dots, North, East and White Dragon. Then the rest…

The fifth turn. He drew a Five of Characters, which joined his Four to form a two-sided wait. That left an isolated Two of Characters. Useless.

He discarded it.

“Mahjong!”

Liuxia laid down her hand: one Two of Characters; Five, Six, Seven of Bamboo; Three, Four, Five of Dots. Plus the two sequences already exposed.

“One, Two, Three of Characters. Six, Seven, Eight of Bamboo. Three, Four, Five of Dots. Five, Six, Seven of Bamboo. A pair of Twos of Characters,” the stout disciple announced for her. “Basic winning hand!”

One hand. Five turns. Over.

◆

The tiles were swept into the middle. Qinglan remained seated, still holding a tile he had never had time to discard.

“What were you thinking about?” Liuxia asked as she shuffled.

“What you had left in your hand.”

“Work it out?”

“Not yet.”

“Exactly.” Liuxia built the wall at astonishing speed. “You were still thinking. I'd already won.”

◆

For the second hand, Qinglan changed his approach.

He began holding back the middle tiles Liuxia could most easily chow, choosing honours first, then terminals less likely at that moment to complete a sequence. Terminals could be chowed too—he remembered that, and still chose according to what he had seen. In this hand, Liuxia chowed none of his further discards.

But his own hand slowed too. Simple tiles accumulated. Incomplete melds crowded one another. By the eighth turn, he was still two steps from being ready to win.

Liuxia could not chow his discards, but she was hardly idle. The stout disciple discarded a South Wind. She had a pair. “Pung.” For the rest, she relied on her own draws, turn after turn, quick and sure.

The ninth turn.

“Self-draw.”

Liuxia laid down her tiles. A South Wind triplet, three sequences, a pair of eyes. Basic winning hand. Self-draw.

Qinglan still had three tiles that fitted nowhere.

Liuxia tossed the half-shelled peanut into her mouth, chewed twice and looked at him. “Two hands. You lose.”

◆

He had lost the entry match.

Qinglan rose and saluted Liuxia with clasped hands. His face gave nothing away. But Yuening saw how white his fingers had tightened inside his sleeves.

“We have urgent business with the Hall Master,” she could not help saying. “Ghost Hand is going to act at the qualifiers—”

“Everyone coming to see the Hall Master has something urgent,” Liuxia interrupted. “One says a great storm is coming at sea, another that raiders are coming to the city. Another says he’s calculated who will win the Tianyuan Mahjong Assembly.”

Yuening set the ebony badge Qi Tingjun had left them on the table edge. “We lost the meeting trial and accept that. But a Bureau of Balance warning doesn’t become false because we lost a hand. Please take the original letter inside and give us a receipt.”

Liuxia looked at the pass, then at her. At last she sent the thin disciple inside. Returning, he placed a receipt on the table and said only that the original letter had been delivered. Whether the Hall Master would see them remained unanswered.

“Come back next month for the entry trial, Tuidao School.” Liuxia had sat back down to shell peanuts. “And remember to walk faster.”

◆

The party left Pinghe Hall.

Behind a second-floor window, a middle-aged man in a purple robe had been standing all along, a little purple-clay teapot cradled in his hand. He watched the scholar walking slowly away below.

He was short, a little past fifty, yet his hair was still black, and he wore a short tuft of beard on his chin. His purple robe had a collar of deep-purple sable; Nanchao people laughed that he felt the cold even in the fifth month. His eyes were bright, and always seemed to hold a hint of a smile. His fingers were thick and short, the knuckles callused from a lifetime of building tile walls. Nanchao people said that Jinghe, Hall Master of Pinghe Hall, had played faster than anyone in his youth. Age had slowed him. A cup of tea could now last him an entire afternoon.

Liuxia came upstairs and bowed. “Hall Master. Sent them away. The Tuidao man lost the first hand in five turns, the second in nine. Never even got ready to win.”

Jinghe did not turn.

“He wasn't ready in the second hand,” he said unhurriedly. “But of those simple tiles he held, he didn't give you a single one to chow.”

Liuxia paused.

“In the first hand, he dealt in to you. He hadn't understood Pinghe Hall's play then.” Jinghe sipped his tea. “In the second, he never dealt in at all. In the space of one hand, he'd understood half of it.”

“And the other half?”

“The other half,” Jinghe said, “is how to be quick. No one can think that through for him.”

◆

Towards evening, Qinglan sat alone on the stone steps by the harbour, watching the tide rise little by little.

He had seen people lose at tiles countless times, watching beside his father as a child. But since returning to the table, this was the first time he had lost two hands in succession, unable even to make up the step by which he lagged in time.

He remembered what the man in the woven hat had said beneath the old archway: Accuracy is no use.

In Yunchuan, he read accurately. Read slowly. Finished reading, then played. In Nanchao, the tiles did not wait for him to finish.

Footsteps came from behind. Hugging her ledger, Yuening sat beside him and placed an oilpaper parcel by his hand.

“Pei Zhiwei sent these. Prawn dumplings. They're cold.”

Qinglan did not move.

“Master Zhumo is going through game records at the inn,” Yuening said. “Says he'll find every famous Pinghe Hall match from the last thirty years and explain them to you one by one tonight. Jinshi's practising his horse stance in the yard. Says he's training to be faster. I think he ate too much. Lu Zhaoye went up on the rooftops. Says he's finding out about Pinghe Hall.”

She paused. “Liuxia was right. You were still thinking. She'd already won.”

“I know.”

“What will you do, then?”

Qinglan watched the tide. It was rising quickly. A rock that had been exposed a moment earlier was already under water.

“Learn,” he said. “Learn how to be quick.”

Yuening glanced at him and pushed the parcel into his hands. “Eat first, then. You won't get quicker on an empty stomach.”

At last, Qinglan smiled. He opened the oilpaper and picked up a dumpling. Cold, but the wrapper was still translucent.

◆

That same evening, a drunken old man lay slumped across a table in the farthest corner of Chaosheng Teahouse.

His hair was grey, his old silk robe spotted with grease. Three empty wine pots stood or lay scattered on the table. When a waiter came to clear them, he caught the man's sleeve and shouted thickly:

“My… my dockyard… I didn't lose it! They cheated me out of it! That hand… that Five of Dots… he put it there himself…”

The next table roared with laughter. “Old Hong's drunk again! Three months now. Same story every day!”

Pei Zhiwei happened to pass with a pot of sobering tea. He stopped.

That Five of Dots. He put it there himself.

He remembered what Qi Tingjun had said at the Rain Pavilion. Three months earlier, in Nanchao, someone had moved discards in the confusion and changed their original order.

Pei Zhiwei sat opposite the old man and poured him a bowl of tea.

“Old sir,” he said. “Tell me about that hand. Take your time.”

The old man cupped the bowl and stared at him for a long while. Then he lowered his voice and leaned close, his breath heavy with wine.

“It wasn't the dockyard they wanted.”

“What was it?”

“What lies beneath it.” The old man's eyes shone in the lamplight. “Twenty years ago, my father's ship went down in those waters. There was a tile aboard… My father said it was a tile that shone.”
