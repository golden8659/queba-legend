Chapter 11The Woman in Red

At dawn, Chaosheng Teahouse had barely taken down its door boards when Liuxia was already seated by a second-floor window.

A set of tiles. A pot of tea. A plate of peanuts.

“Late.” She did not look up.

“Dawn, exactly.” Qinglan sat opposite her. “Not a quarter of an hour late.”

He had not slept all night. Old Hong was missing. Jinshi had made Changfeng lie down at the inn, but Qinglan knew Lu Zhaoye had taken the Five of Dots and the footprint record to the branch office, and Diver Li’s dwelling was still being asked about. Meng Chuan’s strangely lost games might connect to another end of it. So he had come anyway.

“Exactly on time is late.” Liuxia swept the tiles down with a clatter. “At Pinghe Hall, we’re at the table before dawn. Sit. Three rules today.”

She raised one finger. “First. Don’t wait for the best. A good enough tile comes along, take it.”

A second finger. “Second. Don’t calculate twice. One tile. One look. Decide. Discard. You Tuidao people turn every tile over in your heads three times. By the time you’ve finished calculating, everyone’s gone home.”

A third. “Third. Don’t look at your own hand.”

“Don’t look at my own hand?”

“Look at other people’s.” Liuxia said, “The instant you draw a tile, you should know where it belongs. Keep your eyes for the table. Who paused. Who drew a different breath. Where someone’s hand reached.”

Qinglan paused, then suddenly smiled.

“What?”

“The third rule,” he said. “That one I know.”

◆

The first two, however, were hard going.

Liuxia split the practice tiles into three piles, taking turns to play Qinglan’s preceding player, following player and opposite seat. It was not a real four-player game and no points were settled. Whenever she exposed a tile from the “preceding player” pile, Qinglan had to call chow or pass in time. From the other two piles he could only pung, kong or win, never chow.

“Preceding player, Three of Bamboo! Chow or not?”

“Chow—”

“Too slow! Take this back first. Again!”

“Preceding player, Five of Characters!”

“Pass.”

“Pass? You’ve got the Four and Six! You won’t even chow the Five to fill that gap? Holding out for a two-sided wait? Until next year?”

After two hours, Qinglan’s forehead was wet with sweat. He had never been so harried at a mahjong table. Before one tile had passed through his thoughts, Liuxia’s next was already on the table.

But after those two hours, his hand reached for a chow a beat faster than when he had sat down.

During a break, Liuxia shelled a peanut. For once, she did not eat it. She turned it between her fingers.

“Do you know why I play so fast?”

“Everyone in Nanchao is fast.”

“My father was a fisherman,” Liuxia said. “When I was seven, he told me you had to cast your nets as the tide went out. A quarter of an hour late, and the fish went with it. That day he cast too late. When the tide came back, he didn’t make it out.”

She tossed the peanut into her mouth and chewed hard.

“I haven’t waited since.”

Qinglan looked at her without speaking.

“What are you staring at?” She glared and shoved the tiles forward. “Keep playing!”

They played another hour. His hand grew another beat quicker. Twice, he was already reaching as her tile touched the table.

Liuxia saw. She offered not a word of praise, only pushed the peanuts a little closer to him.

“Have a couple,” she said. “At noon, my second senior fellow disciple plays downstairs. Remember what you promised.”

◆

At noon, the ground floor of Chaosheng Teahouse was packed.

The central table was surrounded so tightly no one could pass. Meng Chuan, Pinghe Hall’s second senior disciple, sat in the South seat. Twenty-seven or twenty-eight, fine-featured, his hands quick and steady. Drawing, arranging, discarding: all flowed like water. He was the fastest player of his generation at Pinghe Hall. Jinghe had chosen him for the qualifiers the day after tomorrow, and all Nanchao wanted an early look at his play.

All three of his opponents were from Sea Whale Trading House.

Qinglan and Liuxia stood in the crush. Yuening held her ledger beside him. Behind them, Zhumo craned his neck.

Meng Chuan began the first hand well. Chow on the third turn. Pung on the fifth. Ready on the sixth: a two-sided wait for Three or Six of Characters. Neither tile had appeared. A broad, promising wait.

The room held its breath.

On the seventh turn, Meng Chuan drew a tile, glanced at it and paused with his finger on it.

Only a moment. Quick as a blink.

Then he discarded the Four of Characters from his hand.

He had broken up his two-sided wait for Three or Six.

A murmur of dismay rose. Liuxia’s nails dug into Qinglan’s arm. “Again! Every hand. Right at the last step, he plays one wrong tile!”

Qinglan said nothing. He was watching Meng Chuan’s hand, not his tiles.

Meng Chuan’s fingers reached forward, then forced themselves back.

He seemed to have made a choice, then stopped himself.

Qinglan followed his gaze.

Each time, just before his finger stopped, Meng Chuan glanced swiftly in the same direction: the little table beside the stairs.

◆

A woman sat there.

She wore a long scarlet dress, red as flame against a face so pale it was almost translucent. Her brows tilted upwards. A touch of rouge marked the outer corners of her eyes; her lips held something not quite a smile. One hand supported her cheek. The other idly played with her tea bowl’s lid. Ting. Ting. Ting.

Two Sea Whale Trading House stewards sat beside her, deferential in every gesture. Even when pouring her tea, they held the pot in both hands.

Qinglan watched her.

Something moved behind her. He blinked and looked again. Nothing. Yet a moment earlier, he thought he had seen several crimson, white-tipped, furry shapes flicker behind her skirts and vanish.

One. Two. Three… He had not counted them all.

Suddenly, the woman raised her eyes and looked straight at him across the crowded room.

She smiled.

Then she lifted her tea bowl and gently set its lid in place. Ting.

At the mahjong table, Meng Chuan’s finger trembled. He discarded a good tile.

“Mahjong!” A Sea Whale player laid down his hand.

The second hand. The third. Exactly the same.

Each time, Meng Chuan was faster than his opponents. Often, when he was ready to win, they still needed two or three steps. Yet at the very last step, he always played one wrong tile. The room went from held breath to sighs, then muttered curses.

“That’s Pinghe Hall’s fastest?”

“They’re sending him to the qualifiers? Pinghe Hall’s going to disgrace itself.”

The three Sea Whale players seemed unsurprised by their victories. After every win, the stout man in the North seat smiled and saluted towards the stairs, as though paying his respects.

◆

“It’s her,” Qinglan whispered in Liuxia’s ear. “Every time she closes the lid, your fellow disciple plays a wrong tile.”

Liuxia went white. “She’s signalling him? He’s working with Sea Whale?”

“Not necessarily,” Qinglan said. “Look at his mouth.”

He watched Meng Chuan’s face. No expression showed on those fine features. But with each loss, his lips pressed together a little tighter, until they were white.

“Watch the first thing he does after losing.”

Liuxia looked. Meng Chuan pushed away his tiles, head lowered, and glanced quickly towards the stairs. There was no resentment in that glance. It was a plea.

He was begging the woman.

Liuxia’s lips trembled. “His family… All he has is a little sister. Xiaohe. Twelve. I haven’t seen her for a fortnight…”

◆

That afternoon, Liuxia cornered Meng Chuan in Pinghe Hall’s rear courtyard.

Qinglan stayed outside the round gateway. First he heard Liuxia shouting. Then a long silence. Then a man crying, very, very quietly.

After a long time, Liuxia emerged, eyes red.

“Sea Whale’s men took Xiaohe. Half a month ago,” she said. “They told him that if he wins even one hand at the qualifiers, they’ll throw her into the sea. They said the woman in red beside the stairs would tell him how to lose each hand.”

“Does he know where they’re keeping her?” Qinglan asked.

“No.” Liuxia gritted her teeth. “He’s searched for half a month. Found nothing.”

Qinglan was silent a moment.

“I know someone who might.”

Meng Chuan came through the round gateway, supporting himself on its frame. His eyes were swollen and red. He looked at Qinglan, his lips trembling for a long time.

“You… you’re the one from Yunchuan?” His voice was terribly hoarse. “Liuxia says you saw it after watching one hand.”

Qinglan nodded.

Suddenly, Meng Chuan lifted his robe to kneel. Qinglan caught him.

“For half a month…” Meng Chuan gripped his arm until his knuckles whitened. “Every wrong tile feels like a knife cutting into me. But I don’t dare play the right one. If I win, Xiaohe…”

He could not go on.

“The qualifiers the day after tomorrow,” Qinglan said. “We’ll find a way together for you to decide your own discards. First, bring Xiaohe back.”

◆

Towards evening, Qinglan returned alone to Chaosheng Teahouse.

The woman in red still sat beside the stairs. The two Sea Whale stewards were gone. Only a pot of cold tea remained on the table.

Qinglan sat opposite her.

“Scholar,” she said first, her voice lazy, touched with amusement. “You’ve been staring at me all afternoon.”

“You’ve been watching me all afternoon too.”

She laughed until her shoulders shook. “Interesting,” she said. “I’m Feiyan. And you?”

“Qinglan.”

“I know.” Feiyan rested her cheek on her hand and studied him. “The scholar from Yunchuan with the hundred and thirty-seven tiles. Every Ghost… every soul in Nanchao is talking about you.”

A word had slipped out. Or perhaps she had let it.

“Where is Meng Chuan’s sister?” Qinglan asked.

Her smile did not change. “Why would you think I’d tell you?”

“Because you tapped the bowl lid seven times today,” Qinglan said. “Several times, you waited until he’d picked his tile before you tapped. You could have done it sooner.”

Feiyan’s eyes narrowed a little.

“You were stalling.” Qinglan watched her. “You sit on their side, but you don’t want to drive it through to the end for them.”

Her finger stopped playing with the lid.

“Do you know, scholar,” she said slowly, “people who read others too accurately don’t live long?”

“Someone said something much like that to me,” Qinglan replied. “He said accuracy was no use.”

“Who?”

“A man in a woven hat.”

The smile faded abruptly from Feiyan’s face. She lowered her eyes to her reflection in the tea and did not look up.

◆

For a long time, Feiyan said nothing.

Outside, darkness gathered. Lamps were lit. For an instant, in the lamplight on her almost translucent face, Qinglan thought he saw something deeply tired.

“Scholar,” she said at last, more softly, “do you know why a fox has nine tails?”

Qinglan shook his head.

“One tail. One hundred years,” Feiyan said. “I cultivated my spirit for nine hundred years. Now I have only eight.”

She dipped a finger into the cold tea and slowly drew a circle on the table. Inside it, she drew a hand with five fingers spread.

“They have the missing one,” she said. “As long as I sit here for them, they leave it alone.”

She wiped the water away until the table was clean.

“The old dockyard at the eastern end. Third warehouse. Only two guards at night.” She rose, scarlet skirts turning beneath the lamps. “There’s only a child inside. The other one, the old man you’re looking for, isn’t there.”

“Where is he?”

At the stairs, Feiyan looked back and smiled.

“Scholar. I’ve found one person for you. The other—what will you trade for that one?”

She went downstairs. Qinglan hurried to the window. People came and went along the arcaded street below. No trace of scarlet.

Only an elusive fragrance lingered, like plum blossom flowering in snow.

◆

That night, Qinglan took the others to a shack at the far western end of the docks.

It was low and shabby. Wet leather diving suits hung outside, reeking of the sea. After three knocks, a rough voice shouted from within. “Get lost! Not taking work!”

“Brother Li,” Qinglan called through the door. “We’re from Yunchuan. On the road, Li Xingzhou told us to give his name when we reached Nanchao.”

Silence inside.

The door slid open. A lean, wiry man stood there, short, and shaped from an otter: glossy brown fur clung damply to him, and a long scar ran across his chest, where the fur had never grown back. He looked Qinglan up and down, whiskers twitching, brows knotted.

“My brother?” he said. “That fool who’s escorted deliveries for twenty years and reaches for his purse whenever someone’s short of travel money?”

He was a foundling of the Li family. Thirty years ago, after a great flood, Li Xingzhou, then a boy of ten or so, found a half-frozen otter cub on the riverbank and carried it home inside his coat. From then on the Li family had one more son. He took the family name Li, and was called Boyuan.

Behind him, an opened letter lay weighted on the table. Its handwriting was large and crooked.

“He sent word telling me not to touch Sea Whale’s work.” Li Boyuan slapped the letter. “Said they’re Ghost Hand. As if I needed him to tell me. I’ve dived there once. At the bottom, something glowed in the mud. A dim green light. Like an eye watching me.”

He shivered. “Thirty years in the water. I’ve seen everything down there, but never anything that looked back. After I surfaced, I didn’t dare go in again for three days.”

“He said if we gave his name at the post station, someone would change our horses.”

“Bah. Those post-station friends of his only have lame horses.” Li Boyuan cursed, but the knot in his brow loosened. “Did he send a message with you?”

“No,” Qinglan said. “We’ve come to ask for help. To save a child. Tonight.”

◆

Around midnight. The old dockyard at the eastern end. The third warehouse.

Li Boyuan approached through the water. Like a fish, he slipped silently into the waterway beneath the warehouse. Lu Zhaoye crossed the roof. Jinshi and Liuxia waited outside the warehouse doors. Changfeng steadied the sampan at the water’s edge, holding its scull. Yuening crouched beside him, abacus in her arms, counting the guards’ lamps one by one. Two men of Balance were already waiting in the outer lane.

“I can see two for now,” she whispered. “That agrees with what she said. We still have to look elsewhere.”

One guard dozed against a doorpost. The other paced with a lantern.

Jinshi drew a deep breath, pulled on the golden lion head and swaggered out of the shadows, humming the lion-dance rhythm. “Boom—boom—clang—”

The guard with the lantern turned. A snarling lion face with a golden scar loomed into the light. He howled and dropped his lamp. The dozing guard woke, grabbed a club and gave chase. “What is that?”

“A lion!” Jinshi turned and ran faster than anyone. “Nanchao’s lions don’t lose! Yunchuan’s lions know how to run!”

The two guards chased out one after the other. Jinshi circled towards the outer lane and only spun back at the agreed lamp. Two men of Balance closed from either side and pinned the pursuers separately. The retreat before the warehouse door was clear.

Liuxia covered her mouth, shoulders shaking with suppressed laughter. “Is this how you rescue people in Yunchuan?”

“In Yunchuan,” Yuening said, clicking her abacus without a change of expression, “we avoid a fight when we can.”

The warehouse was quiet. After a while, its door opened from within. Li Boyuan emerged dripping, carrying a girl of eleven or twelve. She was skin and bones, her eyes wide. She did not dare make a sound.

Liuxia rushed to embrace her. “Xiaohe!”

Only then did the child burst into tears.

After crying a while, she caught Liuxia’s sleeve between sobs. “Sister Liuxia… this morning, before dawn, they carried an old grandpa into the warehouse… later, they dragged him onto a boat…”

“When?” Changfeng lunged forward.

“A-after… after dark.” Xiaohe shrank into Liuxia’s arms. “One of the men who came wore a woven hat… he made no sound when he walked… he told them that on the night of the great tide, he’d come himself.”

Lu Zhaoye dropped from the roof and shook his head. “Searched the whole place. No old man.”

He and Qinglan exchanged a glance. The fur on Lu Zhaoye’s tail began to stand on end.

Veins rose on Changfeng’s hands as he gripped the scull.

◆

On the way back, Changfeng suddenly stopped at the docks.

He stared out to sea. Far offshore, a large ship lay dark at anchor without a single lamp. She rode low, deep in the water. Thick cables hung from her stern into the sea.

“That’s Sea Whale Trading House’s salvage boat.” The boy’s voice shook. “I know those mooring lines. They dragged someone aboard. Could Grandpa be there?”

Li Boyuan narrowed his eyes and watched for a long time. His face darkened.

“Tomorrow night is this year’s greatest tide,” he said. “As it ebbs, it washes away a layer of seabed mud. In twenty years, that water will have been shallowest on this one night.”

He turned to Qinglan.

“They probably mean to dive that night. Record the boat’s name and those lines first, and have the branch office keep watching. Don’t rely only on our few pairs of eyes.”

That night, the lamps burned until morning in Pinghe Hall’s rear courtyard. Meng Chuan held Xiaohe without a word, patting her back again and again as he had when she was little.

At the inn, Jinshi practised his horse stance in the yard until sweat covered his head.

“The lion contest is tomorrow,” Yuening leaned on the sill, watching him. “You still have time for the sea?”

“A lion contest is one thing, rescuing someone another.” Jinshi clenched his teeth, his legs not shaking at all. “Tomorrow we beat Huo Xiao in daylight, then go to sea together at night!”

Yuening thought and said nothing, moving two beads. Tomorrow’s lion contest by day, the great tide by night, qualifiers the day after. Three things crowded into two days. They could lose none of them.

(End of Chapter Eleven)
