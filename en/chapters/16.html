Chapter 16The Defending Champion

On the third day, the sky cleared.

Overnight, the clouds above the sea had vanished. Sunlight fell on the dockside square, making the platform’s red felt glow. The crowd was larger than those of the first two days combined. Even the roofs of the arcaded buildings were packed.

All Nanchao knew: of Pinghe Hall’s four places, only two remained: the defending champion’s and the one Yuening was defending in another’s place. The challenger had no name on the list. Only the drawing of a woven hat.

At seven, Jinghe mounted the platform with his walking stick.

He did not sit in the armchair. He went to the centre and saluted the crowd.

“Before the challenges begin,” he said, “Pinghe Hall would like to invite someone up.”

A stir ran through the spectators.

A short, sturdy old smith climbed the steps, his thick arms bare, a copper ruler in his hand so thin the light shone through it.

◆

“Master Chai made the original four arena tables himself twenty years ago,” Jinghe said. “Yesterday we sealed two imitations and put clean tables in for play to continue. Today we open the seals and check them again before all Nanchao.”

In the front row, several faces among the silver whales changed.

Only after the branch supervisor and yesterday’s witnesses checked the seals was the first imitation brought forward. Chai Shouheng crouched and put his copper ruler beneath the edge, tapping inch by inch.

Thud. Thud. Thud. Dull.

At the challenger’s side, the sound changed.

Ting—ting—

Clear as an empty bowl. Everyone heard it.

Chai Shouheng felt beneath the edge. Click. The wooden panel slid open. He tilted the whole table onto its side so the crowd could see: a narrow compartment beneath the top. Two tiles still inside.

A Three of Dots. A Nine of Characters.

The crowd erupted.

The imitation at the former third table was unsealed in public as well. The same tap, the same clear ring. The two compartments and the tiles stored in them matched the drawings on the sealing record, one by one.

He stood, tucked the ruler into his belt and addressed the silver whales coldly.

“A good copy of my tables. Pity rosewood doesn’t need nails.”

As he came down the steps, he passed Qinglan at the front of the crowd, and his stride slowed by half a step.

“I used to build the Tuidao School’s tables too.” He did not look back, and kept his voice so low that only Qinglan could hear. “Their tables had straight edges. No one was allowed to rest a hand on them.”

By the time Qinglan turned his head, the old smith had already pushed his way into the crowd.

◆

“Under the rules of Pinghe Hall’s challenges,” Jinghe said, his quiet voice cutting through the uproar, “a hand proven to involve cheating is void. The place is restored.”

Sea Whale’s chief steward sprang up. “Slander! The tables are yours! You made the compartments yourselves—”

“And the incense?”

A woman in a long purple embroidered dress stepped from the crowd. Xu Zhifan held a small porcelain bottle. Pale-faced, Ruan Jianing followed her.

“Bone-numbing herb. Deepwhale Ambergris.” Xu Zhifan raised the bottle. “The incense offered to Miss Liuxia the day before yesterday. Young Master Pei scraped off its ash.”

Ruan Jianing held a ledger high. “In the past three months, Sea Whale Trading House ordered fifty catties of incense from my shop. Here are the accounts.”

The chief steward went white.

The branch supervisor laid out both days’ original records, the stop calls and the witness accounts of the dismantling on the platform, one by one. At the first table, someone close by had seen the challenger take tiles from beneath the table with his own eyes, and the scene had been sealed. At the third, though the tiles had been scattered, the compartment, that hurried push of the tiles and the record of the replacement table being carried in all matched, point by point.

Qinglan stepped from the crowd, took the ebony badge from his robe and held it aloft. A set of scales on its face. “Uphold Balance” on its back.

“Officers of the Nanchao branch of the Bureau of Balance,” he said. “The witnesses and evidence are before you.”

Several Guards of Balance who had been waiting beyond the crowd pushed through and seized the chief steward from either side. The row of silver whales dissolved into chaos.

Dragged away, every trace of his round-faced smile gone, the chief steward twisted towards the platform and screamed, “You think taking me ends this? The Master’s reach is wider than the sea!”

Qinglan watched him dragged away without speaking, then turned and went back to the second-floor window above the arcade.

◆

The provisional judgments on the two tainted seats were revoked, and the seats returned to Pinghe Hall. The seat Yuening had won back on a clean table went to Meng Chuan, just as she herself had said after the match. Pinghe Hall players sat behind all three tables again. The old cases stayed open; the return of the seats did not close them.

The old master returned to the first table. Liuxia, arm in its sling, to the second. Yuening rose from behind the third and gestured Meng Chuan forward. “Brother Meng. Yours again.”

As he sat, he first turned to search the crowd below. Xiaohe was perched on Changfeng’s shoulders, clapping until both palms were red.

Only the final place remained.

The defending champion’s.

Jinghe slowly went behind the fourth table, lifted the purple-clay teapot from the armchair, set it by the table and sat.

The crowd parted of its own accord.

A man approached from its farthest edge.

Black robes. A woven hat. A blade-cut split in the hat, sewn shut with fine hemp thread. His footsteps made no sound. He moved like a shadow sliding over the ground.

Step by step, he mounted the platform and sat opposite Jinghe.

He placed both hands on the table. A black leather glove on the left. The right—

The right was bare. From its back to the wrist, a broad patch of shrivelled old burns showed clearly in the sun.

The square fell utterly silent.

◆

On a roof opposite the arcaded building, a yellow-and-black shape silently settled behind the ridge.

Lu Zhaoye’s chest was still thickly bandaged, his face white as paper. One hand pressed his wound, the other gripped the tiles. He had climbed out of the inn’s back window and made his way to this building’s highest roof.

His golden eyes fixed on the scarred hand below. He did not blink.

◆

“Twelve years,” Jinghe said slowly, watching him.

The man said nothing.

“Twelve years ago, I played nine hands in succession. In the last, a young man sat opposite me. Very pale hands. Very steady,” Jinghe said. “He swapped a tile. So neatly no one in the crowd saw. I saw.”

He pushed his teapot a little farther aside.

“I said nothing. I held a tile and looked at it for a long time. In the end, I laid down my hand. Self-draw. I won, but not cleanly.” His voice was calm. “For twelve years, every sip of tea has reminded me of that tile.”

At last, the man spoke. Low and steady, as if rising from the bottom of a well. “You won that hand.”

“I did,” Jinghe said. “But I didn’t speak. Today, I will.”

He looked up at the woven hat and spoke each word distinctly.

“The ghost at that table twelve years ago was you.”

◆

Everyone held their breath.

The two older Pinghe Hall disciples filling the other seats had gone pale. They scarcely knew where to put their hands.

Beneath the woven hat, he seemed to smile.

“And what does saying it change?” he asked. “You had no proof twelve years ago. You have none today.”

“Who saw what today, and which step they saw—that’s the step we record.”

Qinglan’s voice came from the second-floor window, quiet but clear.

“Many people watching doesn’t mean a thing is established. But if a hand really did move, don’t think clearing the tiles quickly will erase it.”

◆

The four rolled for seats: two accompanying players East and West, Jinghe South, challenger North. Under the arena agreement East began with an extra tile and discarded first, the other three holding thirteen. The two companions each played for themselves and helped neither side. The supervisor first counted one hundred and thirty-six tiles and compared all four Four of Dots. Only one had a fine crack in its upper right corner; the other three were intact. The crack left the face unharmed. All four agreed to use it, and Zhumo drew its original appearance.

Shuffle. Build the wall.

The man’s hands were fast. Faster than Hu Shan’s. Faster than the grey-robed man’s. Faster than any Qinglan had ever seen. Thirteen tiles flowed like water through them and settled in a neat row.

Jinghe’s hands were slow. He arranged the tiles one by one, as though counting his own years.

Up in the arcade, Yuening returned to the window, abacus on the sill. Below, Jinshi and Huo Xiao stood to either side of the arena, eyes fixed on the crowd like nails. Zhumo moved his writing things down beside the supervisor, recording only public tiles and irregularities he could see clearly, never filling a concealed hand from a word called down from a roof.

“Senior brother,” Qinglan said at the window, “watch for messages below. Report what you see to the guards first. Don’t seize the wrong child flying a kite.”

“Who?”

Qinglan’s gaze swept the crowd, inch by inch.

It reached a roof opposite and stopped.

A boy stood there flying a kite, letting out and drawing in the string. A black swallow circled directly above the platform. Its tail swung east. Swung west.

“The eastern rooftop, the kite-flyer,” Qinglan said. “Several times he first leaned to look at South, then let out or reeled in line, then North discarded. Block his view of South first. Record the line separately.”

Without argument, Jinshi reported the direction to the guards. Huo Xiao took men round towards the stairs, and only then did Jinshi run for the building.

◆

The first turn. The second.

The man in the woven hat discarded a North Wind and a Nine of Dots. Jinghe discarded an East Wind and a One of Characters.

On the second turn, as the man in the woven hat arranged his tiles, a Four of Dots suddenly tipped onto its side at the table’s edge, its face and the fine crack in its upper right corner exposed together. The supervisor, Jinghe and Zhumo before the platform: three pairs of eyes fell on it at once. The supervisor took up his brush and wrote in the original record: Second turn: North exposed sideways. With a flick of two fingers the man drew the tile back into his hand and played on. He did not discard it into the river. That tip did not look like a slip.

Above the rooftop, the black swallow suddenly tilted and fell.

Jinshi first pulled the cloth curtain across the sightline. Only then did the guards ask the boy to lower the kite and question him separately.

The crowd cheered.

The man’s hand paused. Only a moment. Then he played on.

That outside sightline is cut for now, Qinglan thought. The rest depends on the table.

◆

The fifth turn.

The man discarded a Five of Dots.

Qinglan narrowed his eyes. He had placed it half an inch farther right than the other tiles.

He remembered the planted discard Qi Tingjun had demonstrated at the Rain Pavilion. Old Hong’s account of his match. There was a Five of Dots lying plain as day in the opposite player’s discard pool.

Yuening noted where the Five of Dots had landed in the river. Qinglan did not take that alone as proof it was a false tile. There were four Five of Dots; discarding one into the river did not mean he could not win on another.

“That cracked Four of Dots is in the original record,” Yuening said. “On turn four he punged Green Dragon, and what he discarded was Eight of Bamboo. No one has ever seen him discard that Four.”

Qinglan nodded. The Five in the river might be nothing more than bait.

◆

Jinghe happened to hold a Five of Dots.

The old man studied the Five in the discard pool, his fingers resting still on the backs of his own tiles. Below, everyone waited in anxious silence.

At the window, Qinglan did not shout. In a sworn match of honour, spectators could not advise the players on their tiles. He only watched Jinghe, repeating silently: Don’t play it.

Jinghe slowly reached into his own concealed hand and selected Five of Dots, without touching the discard river.

A breath drew in across the square.

The old man turned the tile once between his fingers and set it back.

He discarded a Three of Bamboo.

For the first time, the man’s hand stopped in midair.

◆

“Twelve years ago,” Jinghe said slowly, his eyes on his own tiles, “I held a tile and looked at it for a long time. In the end, I laid down my hand.”

“Today, I’ve held a tile and looked at it for a long time.”

He raised his eyes. “This one, I keep.”

The man said nothing.

He drew a tile and glanced at it. His right hand, the hand covered in burns, suddenly swept along the wall—

“Stop!”

Qinglan’s voice cracked like thunder from the window.

Everyone looked at the man’s right hand.

It had already withdrawn. But an instant earlier, it had swept past the wall, almost too fast to see. So fast that even Jinghe, sitting opposite, had seen only a blur.

Qinglan had seen.

To everyone else, that sweep was only a shadow. In his eyes it slowed, moment by moment: the fingers loosened first, then the heel of the palm drew in, and the stack at the edge of the wall shifted, ever so slightly. Before, he too would have seen only the shadow.

He had not calculated twice. Liuxia’s lesson. One look. Decide.

“When his right hand passed the wall,” Qinglan said steadily, “he put a tile from his hand into it. And took another out.”

◆

The crowd erupted.

The man sat motionless. “Proof?”

“Let the scorekeeper check the tiles.”

Yuening came down from the building and crossed the crowd. Only after the match supervisor confirmed she was a scorekeeper registered in Yunchuan, and Jinghe nodded, was she invited onto the platform to do the count. She stood beside the table and looked at him. “Under the Code of the Mahjong Way, when cheating is suspected, a scorekeeper may check the tiles on the spot. Please show your hand.”

After a moment’s silence, the man slowly laid down his eleven tiles.

Yuening examined the tiles one by one. The Green Dragon pung, three exposed tiles; he had just drawn, eleven concealed. Fourteen altogether, not one too many. Yet the cracked Four of Dots, which the original record showed openly glimpsed from the side on the second turn, was not among the eleven, not in the river and not in the open sets. Where had that tile gone? She had the witnesses guard all four players’ tiles and let no one disturb them. Only then did she follow the direction Qinglan had pointed when he called the stop, and look at that stretch of the wall.

“Record the normal drawing end in one place, and the stack his hand passed in another,” Yuening said. “After a pung, the drawing order skips. We can’t pluck a turn number out of the air and treat it as how many tiles have left the wall.”

She reached to the place his hand had just passed and turned over a tile.

A Four of Dots.

Yuening did not simply call out “Four of Dots.” She turned the tile’s upper right corner towards the witnesses. The fine crack was still there, matching both the original appearance Zhumo had drawn before play and the record of the second-turn exposure.

“Of the four identical faces, only this physical tile bears this crack,” she said. “Count the other three too. Same face alone doesn’t mean the same tile.”

From beside the platform, Zhumo raised the original record. “Second turn: North exposed Four of Dots sideways, fine upper-right crack; supervisor and South both saw. No public action taking it from his hand appears after that.”

The full set was counted again: still one hundred and thirty-six. Four Four of Dots, the other three with intact corners. The supervisor confirmed the hand movement he had seen with his own eyes. Jinghe signed only for the side exposure and the river he had seen himself; Qinglan separately recorded the one-for-one exchange he had witnessed. The two accounts agreed from start to finish.

Yuening closed the ledger. “The tile the original record places in his hand went back into the undrawn wall without any discard, pung or kong. Tile-switching established.” The supervisor announced before everyone, “The challenger loses this hand, and Pinghe Hall holds the arena. The cheating and the evidence go separately to the Bureau of Balance and the Arbitration Office for further investigation and judgment. The existing tile-breaking sentence and lifetime ban from sworn matches stand as before.”

◆

The man sat motionless.

All Nanchao watched him.

He suddenly laughed. A very low laugh.

“Good,” he said. “Then we’ll play clean.”

He pushed his hand into the middle, sweeping all the tiles together. “Shuffle again. This hand, I won’t swap.”

Jinghe watched him and slowly nodded.

“The arena is already held,” Jinghe said. “This next hand is a private contest between us. It changes no result, moves no spirit energy and excuses no punishment.”

The supervisor sealed the old tiles and the original record and brought out a new, inspected set. Branch officers guarded the platform exit while they waited for the escort to come up, and North was not allowed to leave the table. Jinghe insisted on using these few moments to play this one hand against him again. None of the four at the table was given a single word of immunity.

“Pinghe Hall’s rule,” the old man said. “Quick means not keeping people waiting.”

◆

That hand, Jinghe played quickly.

Too fast for Zhumo to record it all.

The Hall Master, past fifty, sent his fingers flying. At each draw he took and discarded; after a chow, he discarded first, all in one motion. It was as though the champion who sat nine games twelve years ago had returned behind this table.

The man in the woven hat was fast too. But in this hand he drew and discarded only in normal order. His right hand no longer reached towards the undrawn wall or beneath the table.

He watched the old man opposite.

Twelve years earlier, this man had held a tile and studied it for a long time before laying down his hand. He had known then: this man had seen, but had not dared speak. Someone who did not dare speak could never truly defeat him.

But today, the man had spoken.

And after speaking, his hands had grown faster. As though everything that had weighed on his fingers for twelve years had suddenly fallen away.

The man in the woven hat drew a tile. For the first time, he could not decide at a glance. He held that tile back in his hand and discarded a previously discarded tile instead: he, too, had read what Jinghe was waiting on. But Jinghe’s wait was two-sided. He could hold back one side, not the other.

The seventh turn.

“Self-draw.”

Jinghe laid down his hand. All Sequences. Four sequences and a pair of eyes. Perfectly clean. Not one tile too many. Not one too few.

The cheering sent the seabirds wheeling up from the docks.

◆

The man in the woven hat stood.

He looked at Jinghe. His burned hand pressed once against the table edge before he saluted him with clasped hands.

Then he turned towards the second-floor window.

“Qing boy,” he said. “You’ve learned to be quick.”

“But speed is no use either.”

Qinglan stood at the window, watching him.

“I know,” he said. “Speed only lets me see your hand.”

“And what is enough?”

“All Nanchao,” Qinglan said, “has seen it too.”

The man’s wrist turned. Three bone tiles flew from between his fingers. One embedded itself in a platform post. One in a Guard of Balance’s scabbard. One in a flagpole.

The crowd flinched. When they looked again, the woven hat was gone.

Only a black leather glove remained on the platform.

The crowd below crushed together. At its farthest edge, a flick of red clothing swung towards where the shadow had gone, and then it too was gone.

On the roof opposite, Lu Zhaoye sprang up and lunged after a black shadow flickering through the crowd. But as he reached the second rooftop, the wound in his chest tore open. He lurched, rolled off the eaves and fell into Jinshi’s arms below.

“Trying to get yourself killed?” Jinshi roared.

Lu Zhaoye struggled twice to rise. Could not. Teeth clenched, he stared after the vanished shadow, eyes reddening.

Qinglan retrieved the glove from the platform, came over and placed it in his hand.

“The left,” he said. “Yours is the right. A pair.”

Lu Zhaoye gripped it until his knuckles whitened. “A pair of gloves. Next time, I want the hands.”

◆

That evening, Jinghe held a banquet at Pinghe Hall.

After checking both cases’ original records and each person’s testimony, the Arbitration Office officials engraved two mahjong jades in public and set them before Qinglan. “In the table-switching case, you reported the lead and helped preserve evidence. In the tile-switching case, you stopped play in person and the physical evidence matched. One jade for each case. The cases, we keep pursuing.”

Yuening moved an abacus bead softly. “Four now.”

Arm still in its sling, Liuxia raised a cup of tea to Qinglan. “Tuidao. You were slow to learn. But you learned.”

Meng Chuan held Xiaohe. Huo Xiao carried his blue-green lion. Jinshi, red-faced from drinking, was arguing with Huo Xiao over whose lion head weighed more. Changfeng helped Old Hong sit in a corner. The old man gazed at the sea beyond the window without a word, a smile on his face.

Qinglan took his tea over and sat beside him.

“Old sir. The tile beneath that water—”

“Let it sleep.” Old Hong watched the sea. “I’ve guarded that water for twenty years. I’ll keep guarding it. If you need the tile one day, come back. Changfeng and I will take you out to bring it up.”

Changfeng nodded vigorously. “I can dive now! Uncle Li taught me!”

Amid the bustle, someone from the post station brought an urgent letter.

Qinglan recognised the writing on its cover. Tiandu. Bureau of Balance. Qi Tingjun.

It was not a reply. Counting the days, Ke Jingduo’s horse could not yet have reached Tiandu. Qi Tingjun had written first, of his own accord.

Only a few lines:

A great fire last night on the street behind the Scorekeepers’ Institute.

The house that burned was the one Shen Suyun stayed in ten years ago.

After the fire was put out, only one thing was found intact in the ruins: an ebony abacus frame, with the name “Yun” carved on its lowest crossbar.

Come to Tiandu at once.

The tea bowl fell from Yuening’s hand and struck the floor with a sharp crack.

She looked down at the small ebony abacus on her belt. On the first bead of its lowest rod was a tiny carved “Yun”.

How could there be two?

Qinglan came to her side, crouched and picked up the broken bowl, piece by piece.

Yuening did not move. Did not cry. She only untied the abacus from her belt and held it in her hands. Her fingers passed over its beads one by one, down to the lowest rod, stopping at the bead engraved with “Yun”.

“The day my mother left,” she said softly, “she took only one thing.”

“What?”

“Her own abacus.” Yuening looked up, her eyes frighteningly bright. “She left this one for me. Her own, she took with her.”

At some point, all the noise in the room had fallen away.

Beyond the window, Nanchao’s sea rose and fell. At the far end of the Great River lay Tiandu.

◆

That night, Qinglan did not sleep.

He walked alone to the far eastern end of the docks. The tide had gone out, and the timber piles stood out of the water one after another, hung with dripping seaweed. Three days ago, it was here that he had placed the White Dragon in someone else’s hand. Over these three nights he had reached inside his robe again and again, and each time found nothing there.

A thread of fragrance suddenly drifted on the wind, like plum blossom opening in snow.

On top of the third pile sat a patch of red. Feiyan had not taken off her shoes, and she was not smiling. Her eight tails hung down, their tips steeped in the black water.

She jumped down from the pile, took his hand, pressed something into his palm and closed his fingers over it, one by one.

A bone tile. Its corners were worn smooth and round, and it was still warm from her body.

Qinglan opened his mouth.

“You gave me something first,” Feiyan said. “This time, I give first.”

She let go without waiting for him to speak.

Qinglan closed his hand tight around the tile.

Overhead, the Big Dipper hung between two masts. The tile in his palm turned cool for a moment, then little by little grew warm again. He turned it over to its back. The seven pinprick points lit up one after another. A green line rose from the tile’s edge, held together and slowly gathered towards its centre. Beneath the little Ghost Hand mark, three slender Bamboo marks emerged.

Three of Bamboo.

These past few days he had known it himself: the breath had already taken up its third current. But with the tile out of his hands, there had been nothing to reflect it.

He looked up. There was no one on the pile any more. Out on the black water beyond the harbour, a small boat with no lamp lit was rowing away. The thread of plum-blossom scent on the wind had faded too.

Qinglan tucked the White Dragon back inside his robe. Through the layer of cloth, it was warm.

(End of Volume II: Storms over Nanchao)
