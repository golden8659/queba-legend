Chapter 8Leaving the City

The boat for Nanchao would leave early in three days’ time.

Zhumo made a trip to the bookshop. When he returned, ten slim, trimmed volumes had joined the things in his basket. The shop had commissioned copies of old, publicly available game records, diagrams checked as well as copied, for delivery and inspection at its Nanchao branch. The deposit covered paper and ink; the balance would come after delivery. He marked the gaps in the originals in the margins, without filling them in to make a handsome hand.

For those three days, the Rain Pavilion was busier than on the day of the sworn match of honour.

Early on the first morning, Jinshi did not take his apprentices onto the high posts. He had the two youngest practise turns on the low ones and stood beside them to catch them.

“Still haven’t recovered?” Qinglan asked.

“The tiredness in my legs is gone.” Jinshi wrapped the stone tile. “But the two stakes I paid didn’t come back after a night’s sleep. I can’t call on the same burst of strength I could before.”

He told the apprentices to stand back and tightened the rope beside the high posts once more.

Qinglan rested a hand on the White Dragon. Of the six stakes he had received, two were his senior brother’s. He wanted to say something, but Jinshi was catching a child coming off a post. Qinglan stepped forward to support the other side.

That night, he still gathered his breath as his master had taught him. The faint energy among the plants outside came very slowly; the six stakes received into the White Dragon the day before had to be joined to his own energy strand by strand. The moment he rushed, his chest tightened. He stopped, waited for his breathing to settle, then began again.

While moving the posts the next day, he used that breathing to steady his stance. A weight that once forced him to change hands halfway now stayed in his grip until he set it down. As he lowered the post, he still had time to notice a child’s foot at the other end and warn him away from the wooden base.

At dusk, looking through the old game record, Qinglan tried following two possible lines of play at once. He used to reach the fourth move and have to look back at the earlier discards. This time he went further, but stopped at a blank. No concealed hand had been recorded there.

He wrote down two possibilities, without choosing one to pass off as fact.

The day after that, when he tried stepping onto a low post as Jinshi did, his footing wavered and he had to catch the pillar.

Jinshi steadied him.

“Your strength lasts longer. Your feet still need practice.”

Qinglan let go of the pillar and began again from the lowest post.

Qi Tingjun came first. Still in his xiezhi robes, Balance blade at his waist, he entered and laid an ebony badge on the table. A set of scales was engraved on its front. On the back were the words “Uphold Balance”.

“Orders from Tiandu. I must return. The Arbitration Office sent people to inspect the seals, and the case has been handed to the Bureau of Balance,” he said. “Mochuan’s statement, both Red Dragons and Gan Henian’s Two of Dots must all go back to the Bureau of Balance.”

Yuening looked at the two wooden boxes with their sealing strips. “Both Red Dragons—we’re taking both?”

“Both. The box stays sealed, and we record where each one came from,” Qi Tingjun said. “The new one is hard proof that Hengchang hired a cheat. Compare the way the hand is carved on its back with the old tiles from the match at the northern apothecary, and that match can be re-examined; the academy’s old case can be closed as well. Whatever Hengchang swallowed, it will have to give back.”

He pressed a hand on the lid of the box holding the old one. “The old one was locked in the vault of the Tiandu Mahjong Arbitration Office ten years ago. If it could get out of that vault and turn up at the door of the Rain Pavilion, then whoever can reach into that vault is working with Ghost Hand. This tile is the key to reopening Master Qing’s case.”

“So it has to be sealed,” Yuening said.

“One seal from the scorekeeper, one from the witness, and one more when it reaches the Bureau of Balance. Which day, through whose hands, from where—every step recorded,” Qi Tingjun said. “What evidence fears most isn’t being lost. It’s being quietly swapped.”

“And this?” Yuening picked up the badge and turned it over.

“There is a branch of the Bureau of Balance in Nanchao,” Qi Tingjun said. “Show them this, and they will listen to you.” He paused. “Not accept everything. But at least hear you out.”

Qinglan put the badge away safely. “You trust us now, Captain?”

“I trust your reading of the tiles.” Qi Tingjun looked at him. “And I trust that this time you didn’t take the terms they wrote as some law of nature. Mochuan brought the original letter to report it; we protected the medicine shop first. The men left outside the abandoned dye works failed to stop the two who escaped. That too must go in the record as it happened.”

Zhumo had been standing by the stairs, hugging his bamboo basket, ears drooping. As Qi Tingjun passed, the panda spoke suddenly.

“Ten years ago, you stood behind Supervisor Gan. I sat beside the table recording the hand,” he said. “We both saw. Neither of us spoke.”

Qi Tingjun stopped.

“You kept silent because you were only a copyist,” he said. “I kept silent because I was only seventeen, and my master was still there.”

“And afterwards?”

“Afterwards, my master was gone.” Qi Tingjun looked at Zhumo. “Only then did I begin investigating for myself. Whatever I uncover, I record. What I haven’t established, I can’t pretend I’ve already made clear.”

Zhumo lowered his head. After a long time, he bowed solemnly. Qi Tingjun returned the bow. Ten years later, the two had finally spoken of that day for the first time.

Qi Tingjun turned and walked to the door, then paused once more without looking back.

“See you in Tiandu.”

◆

Pei Zhiwei came second.

On his back was a bundle bigger than Zhumo’s bamboo basket. In his hand was the three-tier red-lacquer food carrier. He set it on the table. “I’ve given the manager my notice.”

“Your notice?” Jinshi’s eyes widened. “Who’s going to make our prawn dumplings now?”

“Which is why I’m coming with you.” Smiling, Pei Zhiwei opened the carrier. The top tier held bamboo-shoot buns fresh from the steamer. “No morning tea in the world compares with Nanchao’s. I’ve made pastries and dumplings for ten years and never tasted a Nanchao prawn dumpling. Besides—”

He took a little dog-eared booklet from his sleeve and opened it for Qinglan. Its pages were densely filled with the names of sea-goods shops in Nanchao. Several had been circled in red.

“Bone-numbing herb. Chill Cicada Powder. Both come from Nanchao’s sea-goods shops,” Pei Zhiwei said. “Who sells medicinal herbs, who buys them, which ships come and go—ten years hearing kitchen gossip teaches you a thing or two.”

He paused and lowered his voice. “One more thing. The man in the woven hat came to the Rain Pavilion three times before the match. Every time, he sat at the back of the second floor, in the corner by the window. He ordered only a pot of the cheapest coarse tea. Two copper coins. Never more, never less. He would hear one instalment of the story, then leave.”

Qinglan frowned. “He came for the storytelling?”

“For the storytelling,” Pei Zhiwei said. “I looked at him once when I brought his tea. He listened as though he were keeping accounts.”

Zhumo was already reaching for his second bamboo-shoot bun. “Count me in,” he said indistinctly.

Jinshi reached for one too. “Count me in too!”

Yuening looked from them to Qinglan and slapped down her abacus. “Six people, then. Ten days by river, meals and lodging aboard included. One gold coin each for passage. Six people, six coins.”

Everyone at the table fell silent.

Qinglan took out the Dipper gold coin and placed it on the table. Yuening counted their loose change again: a little over twenty copper coins.

“Five gold coins short,” she said.

Qinglan said nothing. His gaze rested on the seven stars worn bright on the coin.

◆

Tao Lingtao came third, with Xingyu and a group of young embroiderers, carrying a heavy brocade pouch. The moment she entered, she set it before Qinglan.

“I was worried you might be short of passage money,” Tao Lingtao said. “Everyone in the workshop talked it over. We’d like to lend you enough to get on your way.”

After hearing the fare, Tao Lingtao untied the pouch and counted out ten gold coins.

Qinglan looked up. “We’re only five coins short for passage. We don’t need this much.”

“And once you reach Nanchao, won’t you need to eat and find rooms?” Tao Lingtao pushed the money closer. “Ten coins, on loan. All of you come back safe.”

This time Qinglan did not push it away. He bowed to her. “We’ll remember this debt.”

Yuening entered a loan of ten gold coins in the ledger, then used six of them to pay the fares in full. She put the remaining four in a separate pouch for travel expenses, and Qinglan took back his Dipper gold coin. Before departure, she changed one tael of silver from the travel funds into copper coins for small purchases ashore.

Only after watching her finish the entry did Tao Lingtao say, “When you repay it, tell the girls what happened along the way.”

Xingyu stepped out from behind Tao Lingtao and saw Qinglan folding the two green-bamboo handkerchiefs together and tucking them into his robe. The new one lay on top. Its bamboo was straight now, though a loose thread still hung at the edge.

She reached out, then drew back her hand.

“When you come back, I’ll finish the edge.”

Qinglan settled his robe and nodded to her.

◆

On the evening before they sailed, Du Yunwen also came from the academy. His hand scarcely shook now. He carried a roll of paper tied with red cord.

“Master Wen asked me to bring this. Master Zhumo made one copy of the academy match. Master Wen copied it again for the academy.” He placed the game record on the table. “Master Wen says that from now on, the first mathematics lesson for academy students will be the Three of Characters in the sleeve’s shadow.”

Luo Jinke followed him. She had taken off the white cloth that had bound her wrist for days, and flexed her five fingers for Qinglan. “Better. Next sworn match of honour, I play for myself.”

Xiao Cheng’an sat on his storytelling stage, stroking his beard and smiling as people came and went. He said nothing.

Only after dark, when everyone had left, did he call Qinglan to the stage.

“This old man is starting a new story,” he said. “I have the title already.”

“What story?”

“It begins with a penniless scholar passing through Yunchuan.” Xiao Cheng’an closed his folding fan. “The title is Legend of the Mahjong King.”

Qinglan stared, then laughed despite himself. “Master Xiao, I am hardly a Mahjong King.”

“Not yet,” the old man said unhurriedly. “A storyteller concerns himself with the beginning. The ending is yours to play.” He ran a hand over his storyteller’s block. “This old man once told half a story about the Tuidao School. When it reached your father’s senior fellow disciple, there was nothing more to tell.”

◆

On the night before departure, unable to sleep, Qinglan climbed onto the Rain Pavilion’s roof.

Someone was already there. Yuening sat hugging her knees, the small ebony abacus beside her feet, staring at the moon.

“Even scorekeepers can’t sleep?”

“Scorekeepers are people too.” Yuening shifted aside, making a little room for him on the tiles. “I was wondering whether my mother felt like this before she left for Tiandu. Awake the night before. Gone early the next morning.”

Qinglan sat beside her.

“She left this abacus behind.” Yuening picked it up, rubbing the bead engraved with Yun. “I used to think she was afraid I’d have nothing for company. Now I think she was afraid she might not come back. Someone had to carry on the calculation.”

“You will reach the answer.”

“And you know that too?”

“I read it.”

Yuening glared at him but did not argue. After a while, she asked suddenly, “That oath of yours. Have you broken it?”

Qinglan thought for a long time.

“I did,” he said. “But breaking it doesn’t mean I can say I never swore it.”

“Why?”

“He taught me to count tiles so that no one could conjure an extra tile before my eyes.” Qinglan looked towards the distant Three-Coloured River. “Sitting down that day was my own choice. If I play a hand wrong or judge something wrong from now on, I have to answer for it. I can’t keep saying my father would forgive me.”

Yuening said no more. She held the abacus against her chest and leaned against the roof ridge, watching the moon sink slowly westward.

A small river lantern drifted along the Three-Coloured River.

Then a second. A third… Eleven in all, one behind another, drifting down from upstream, a string of lights on the dark water.

A figure crouched on the stone steps at the riverbank. Behind him, a tail lay motionless.

Yuening drew a small breath. “The cat.”

Qinglan said nothing. They sat on the roof and watched the eleven lanterns drift past the Rain Pavilion, past the stone arch bridge and on downstream, until one by one they were too far away to see.

On the day the boat sailed, the landing was crowded before dawn.

The Three-Coloured River flowed through the morning mist. Indigo, crimson, pale yellow, long strands drifting downstream. At the river’s end lay the Great River, and at the Great River’s end, the sea. Nanchao.

The lion-dance apprentices stood in two rows, each in brand-new clothes. At their head, the senior apprentice held the golden lion head in both hands.

The golden scar still marked its forehead. The left eye socket was still empty.

Jinshi went to him and took the lion head. He cradled it, stroking it for a long time.

“Master,” the senior apprentice said, eyes red. “You needn’t worry about the troupe.”

Jinshi nodded. He untied the yellow silk sash he had worn for more than ten years and fastened it round the senior apprentice’s waist.

“From today, you are the lion-dance master.”

◆

Someone pushed through the crowd. The old sugar seller.

Breathless, he took a small object wrapped in red cloth from inside his clothes and offered it to Jinshi with trembling hands.

“That night, the whole street was full of broken sweets,” he said. “I was picking them up, and I found this. I thought, it’s the eye from Master Jin’s lion. I must keep it for him.”

The red cloth opened to reveal a brass-bell eye. Yellow brass, still dented by the blow of the club.

Jinshi cupped it in his hands. His lips trembled for a long time, but he could not speak.

“The old one,” Yuening said softly beside him.

Jinshi fitted the eye back into the lion head’s left socket and tightened it. Then he turned and held the head before Qinglan. Taking the cinnabar-dipped brush from his apprentice, he offered it in both hands.

“Xiaolan,” he said. “You dot it.”

◆

Qinglan took the brush.

He remembered the night of the lion dance. Jinshi standing below after dotting both of the lion’s eyes, looking up and seeing him. The cinnabar dripping from the brush and striking the flagstones with a soft pat.

First the eye, so the lion could see.

Qinglan raised the brush and placed a scarlet dot on the brass bell of the left eye. The dented eye caught the morning light.

Then the nose, so the lion could smell.

Last, the forehead. The brush touched the very centre of the golden scar.

“To give the lion spirit,” Qinglan said softly.

He set down the brush and looked at Jinshi. “Brother. My father told you your heart burned too hot to sit still at the mahjong table. To go and learn the lion dance, where they could use that fire.”

Jinshi’s eyes were red through and through.

“That ‘pass’ at the abandoned dye works,” Qinglan said, “was you sitting at a mahjong table and holding that fire in. But there will be plenty of places on the road ahead that need it.”

Jinshi opened his mouth. No words came. He pulled the lion head over his own and turned to face his apprentices.

From beneath it came a deep, muffled roar, thick with tears.

“Lion-dance troupe—raise the lions!”

◆

The gongs and drums began.

Neither the drums of the lion-awakening ceremony nor the drums of battle. These were farewell drums, measured and unhurried, as though saying: go on, go on, travel far, then come home.

The red lion leapt and tumbled on the landing. At the bow stood the golden lion. Jinshi took off its head and held it in his arms, his back to the shore.

The boat had left the shore.

Tao Lingtao and Xingyu waved handkerchiefs from the bank. Before departure, Tao Lingtao had drawn Qinglan aside to say one thing. “Young master, the workshop door will always be open to you. When the road tires you, come home for a bowl of coarse tea.”

The little embroiderers ran along the riverbank beside the boat until they could run no farther. Luo Jinke stood on the highest stone step, raising her healed right hand. Du Yunwen stood beside her, steadying his spectacles. The old sugar seller held a golden sugar lion high in the air.

On the road beyond the landing, a black horse stood quietly. Its rider wore deep blue-green xiezhi robes. He watched the boat from afar until its sail rounded the bend in the river, then turned his horse north.

Tiandu lay to the north.

Jinshi had not looked back. Holding the lion head, he faced downstream, his shoulders shaking.

Lu Zhaoye crouched on the mast and watched for a long time. For once, he made no teasing remark. He took a clean handkerchief from his clothes and tossed it down. It landed squarely on Jinshi’s head.

“Wipe your face,” he said. “Catfolk don’t like watching people cry.”

Jinshi caught the cloth and scrubbed his face. His voice was muffled. “The lanterns on the river last night. Yours, weren’t they?”

No answer came from the mast. Only after a long time did a very quiet voice drift down.

“Eleven. Not one sank.”

After another pause, he said, “Ten lamps for my family. And one more, for me.”

◆

The boat left the Three-Coloured River and entered the Great River.

Towards evening, Pei Zhiwei lit a small stove at the stern and cooked a pot of rice porridge with sliced fish. Zhumo sat on the deck with a bowl in his hands, closing his eyes in pleasure after each mouthful. “Brother Pei, this fish porridge is fresher than anything the palace cooks in Tiandu could make.”

“You’ve eaten palace food?”

“No,” the panda admitted honestly. “But I imagine this is what it must taste like.”

Pei Zhiwei laughed until he could hardly stand. Zhumo finished his porridge, set the bowl down and wrote a few words on a fresh scroll.

Volume I: Dawn over Yunchuan.

Below, line by line, he recorded three matches. The Rain Pavilion sworn match: the waterway returned to the Tao family. The Shouzhuo Academy rematch: the rear hill returned to the academy. The abandoned dye works west of the city: the White Dragon neither staked nor surrendered; Qinglan won by self-draw. After each entry, in careful script, stood the date and a name: Qinglan.

After a moment’s thought, he added a small final line. “West resists pung. Credit where due.”

The boat rolled. With a cry, Jinshi lunged for the rail and was violently sick.

“Lion-dance master!” Lu Zhaoye gloated from the mast. “Steady as anything on plum-blossom posts. Can’t manage a boat?”

“Posts… posts don’t move!” Jinshi clung to the rail, green-faced, and waved a feeble hand.

Pei Zhiwei brought him a bowl of ginger broth with a smile. “Ten days by water to Nanchao. Time to get used to it, troupe master.”

Yuening leaned against the rail and worked her abacus. After a while, she said suddenly, “Two.”

“Two what?”

“Mahjong Jade tokens.” She did not look up. “Two so far. Nine to enter the Tianyuan Mahjong Assembly. Seven more to go. If we win in Nanchao—”

“Three,” Qinglan said.

Yuening glanced at him. “Better at counting than me now, are you?”

Qinglan did not answer. One by one, he took the things from inside his robe and laid them on the deck, and Yuening handed over the rubbing from the hidden fold of her ledger: his father’s White Dragon, the ink rubbing from its back, the Seven of Bamboo, the Dipper gold coin they had kept, and the two bamboo-embroidered handkerchiefs.

A mahjong set had one hundred and thirty-six tiles. He had gathered only five or six things on this journey.

Yet every one had been kept safe for him by someone, for a long time.

The river wind lifted a corner of the rubbing. Under the moonlight, the seven little white points lay quietly in their ladle shape, pointing north.

Qinglan put everything away, one piece at a time, handed the rubbing back to Yuening, and raised his eyes to the southern sky.

◆

That same evening. Yunchuan. The Rain Pavilion.

The second-floor storytelling room was packed. Even the windowsills were full.

Xiao Cheng’an. A white beard, a folding fan, a storyteller’s block. He struck the block, and silence fell.

“Today, we begin a new story.” The old man’s voice was not loud, but it carried far. “Legend of the Mahjong King.”

“In the city of Yunchuan stood the Rain Pavilion. And into the Rain Pavilion came a penniless scholar. His purse held only a few dozen copper coins, and he had sworn never to touch a tile again…”

The old man opened his fan. His voice suddenly deepened. “But listen well, dear audience. There is a kind of oath in this world that waits to be broken.”

He struck the block.

“That day, early in the afternoon, Hengchang Trading House brought a player for the East seat. Ten long, slender fingers. He shuffled as though playing music. Built the wall as though embroidering silk—”

He told of Hu Shan stacking the wall. Of the needle that pricked Luo Jinke. Of the scholar standing at the very edge of the crowd with his eyes closed. A child below the stage stamped his feet in impatience. “Why won’t he sit down and play?” The whole room laughed.

The listeners were spellbound. Children sat open-mouthed; adults forgot their tea. Xiao Cheng’an told of the afternoon match, of Proprietor Tao kneeling before everyone, of the scholar walking step by step towards the table. Of the ninth turn. A thumb passing over the face of a tile—

“The young scorekeeper counted the tiles, one by one. One, two, three… One hundred and thirty-four. One hundred and thirty-five. One hundred and thirty-six—”

He struck the block.

“One hundred and thirty-seven!”

The room cheered.

Amid the cheering, at the very back of the room, in the corner by the window, sat a man.

He wore a woven hat with a blade-cut split sewn shut in fine hemp thread. From beginning to end, he had not moved. Quietly, he listened to the entire instalment.

When the performance ended, the crowd poured out. By the time a waiter went to clear that corner, the man was gone.

On the table lay a single copper coin.

Payment for the tea.

A pot of coarse tea cost two copper coins. He had left only one.

Xiao Cheng’an came over, leaning on his cane. He studied the coin for a long time, then picked it up and slipped it into his sleeve.

“The other one,” the old man said softly, “he means to pay next time.”

(End of Volume I: Dawn over Yunchuan)
