Chapter 5A Game at the Academy

The Songtao Academy had only seven page boys.

By the time Qinglan counted the eighth, the wall had been built, the wild tile turned up, and in the first game the academy had already lost four points.

That eighth page boy was huddled in the northeast corner of the lecture hall, squeezed under a pillar with the other seven, in the same blue cotton clothes, holding the same plain folding fan. The others fanned with no rhythm at all, fanning when hot, stopping when drowsy; only his fan stopped by count—two pauses, and the string of prayer beads on the chest of Qian Youqing in the West seat paused twice with it; three pauses, and the beads paused three times.

Whenever the fan stopped, his eyes were fixed on Qinglan's back.

Qinglan rested his left hand at the edge of the wall and did not turn around. The White Dragon over his heart, through the red cloth wrapping, was cold and rough, like a bone soaked in well water.

It started at noon.

The sun had just slipped past the roof ridges when Qinglan's party left by the North Gate. Yuening walked at the very front in a clean goose-yellow skirt-and-jacket trimmed with green, two small white flowers pinned in her twin buns, the small ebony abacus on her left palm and a brush in her right hand, a half-length of thread wound around its shaft, as though even while walking she might need to note something down at any moment. She walked fast but with small steps, her waist swaying lightly with each one, the hem of her skirt opening and closing above her ankles. Qinglan followed behind and did not know where to put his eyes, so he fixed them on the loose end of thread swinging from the brush. Zhumo carried his half-man-high bamboo basket on his back, the sleeves of his green bamboo-patterned robe short enough to show his furry arms. Jinshi brought up the rear with the lion head on his shoulder, his red headband streaming back in the wind. The crack on the lion head's brow had just been mended with gold lacquer by the old master of the lion-dance troupe, leaving a golden scar; the left eye was still empty, so it looked like a cat that had been beaten and still wasn't convinced.

Qi Tingjun had not come with them. He had gone ahead to the academy, saying he wanted to “look at the doors first.”

Just outside the North Gate was the wharf. A salt boat was unloading, the gangplank creaking under the weight, porters lined up in a row, their work chant shaking the sky. One of them was young, narrower in the shoulders than the rest, but with regular, upright features. A sack of salt pressed down on him until half his face was flattened against the burlap, and without a sound he shuffled toward the bank.

Yuening's step slowed by half a beat.

“That's old Zheng's son,” she said under her breath. “I've seen his name in the household register at the branch office. Last year he came in once to report a work injury. He pressed his own fingerprint, and his handwriting is better than his father's.”

Qinglan stopped and took another look.

The young man set down his sack, straightened up, wiped the sweat from his face with his sleeve, and his gaze swept their way and moved indifferently on. He did not know Qinglan, nor would he know that his father was at this moment locked in the Bureau of Balance's prison; still less would he know that under the bed in that house in the south of the city, a letter of recommendation with his name on it had lain pressed for ten years.

“Captain Qi's men went to the south of the city this morning to fetch that camphor chest,” Yuening said. “It was opened in front of the ward head. I saw it at noon in the handover register at the branch office. Under the evidence courier's column, Captain Qi had written a line in his own hand.”

“Was the letter of recommendation inside?”

“It was.” She tapped the abacus frame with the shaft of her brush. “Wrapped in three layers of oilpaper. The paper hasn't even yellowed. He hid it carefully.”

Jinshi had heard from behind and pushed up beside them. “Then what are we waiting for? Take it to the lad! The future his father begged for him has been going moldy under a bed, and here he is hauling salt—”

“It can't be taken to him,” said Yuening. “Old Zheng said it's his confession. A confession is evidence. Once evidence goes into a box at the Bureau of Balance, no one may take it out on their own and give it away.”

“But it's his!”

“It's his, and it's also his father's confession.” Yuening did not look back. “When the case is closed, the Bureau of Balance will return it to the original owner. The original owner is old Zheng, not his son. Whether old Zheng gives it to him is old Zheng's business.”

Jinshi opened his mouth, held it in for a long while, and finally squeezed out: “You people keep your accounts far too clean.”

“Accounts have to be clean,” said Yuening. “Once an account gets dirty, someone uses it like a copper coin.”

After that, none of them said anything. Qinglan looked once more toward the far end of the gangplank. The young man had shouldered another sack, his back bent like a fully drawn bow.

He remembered what Zheng Shouzhuo had said that morning, leaning against the wall of the storeroom: I still have a son hauling sacks at the wharf.

To the very end the old man had not spoken that name. Perhaps it was this person he had been protecting.

The academy in the north of the city had two characters on its gate plaque: Songtao.

White walls and black tiles, three courtyards deep, two old ginkgo trees at the gate, their leaves still green. Behind the courtyard wall was a bamboo hill, spreading from its foot all the way up to its waist; when the wind passed, the whole hill rustled.

That bamboo hill was the academy's back hill. Last month, in a sworn match of honour, the academy had lost, and the back hill had gone to Hengchang. Now a row of new wooden stakes stood at its foot, small yellow Hengchang flags stuck in their tops, axes and hemp rope piled beneath the flags, waiting only for a document to come down so the felling could begin.

Under the ginkgo tree a scholar was crouching, staring blankly at the row of stakes.

He was twenty-seven or twenty-eight, with naturally curly hair gathered long at the back of his head and tied high with a blue-beaded cord, a few curling locks hanging by his ears. Round-rimmed spectacles sat on his nose; he wore a washed-out grey-green scholar's robe, and from the brown silk cord at his waist hung a ring of green jade and a dark green tassel.

“Assistant tutor Du,” Yuening called.

Du Yunwen scrambled to his feet and tried to cup his hands, but his right hand would not close however he tried. On its back was a patch of wrinkled new scar, still pinkish, creeping from the web of the thumb up to the wrist, and his fingertips were still twitching faintly. Somewhat embarrassed, he drew the right hand into his sleeve and made half a bow with his left alone. “Miss Yuening. Is this Young Master Qing? What happened at the Rain Pavilion the day before yesterday is all over the academy.”

“That game last month,” said Qinglan. “How was your hand hurt?”

“Halfway through the second game, a page boy came to top up the tea, tripped, and a whole pot of boiling water went over the back of my hand.” Du Yunwen smiled bitterly. “The strange thing is, it scalded, all right, and after a scald it ought to hurt, but it didn't. It was numb, numb from the fingers to the elbow. I couldn't even hold a tile. In the last two games I couldn't even build the wall straight. Doctor Lan came to look at it later and said the scald was the least of it; it was as if something else had been poured into this hand, and it had stuck all the heavier in the scalded flesh. It took half a month to come back, and to this day the fingers still twitch.”

Qinglan and Yuening exchanged a look.

Numb. Numb from the fingers to the elbow.

Qinglan's right arm was numb at this moment too, and had been since yesterday, and had not yet come back. The difference was that he knew how he had gone numb.

“And that page boy?” Yuening asked.

“Couldn't be found. The outsiders who came to watch that day brought a dozen or more page boys, it was all a jumble, and no one could say whose he was.” Du Yunwen looked at the row of stakes. “The academy's bamboo was planted under three successive men who served as head of the academy. Cut it down, and it will never come back.”

Jinshi hoisted the lion head onto his shoulder and thumped his chest. “Brother Du, you just play and don't worry! Anyone who dares make trouble at the door, I'll sit on him and squash him flat.”

Du Yunwen was taken aback, then couldn't help laughing.

The lecture hall was packed.

Where the lecture desk usually stood, a rosewood mahjong table had been set, a copper plate set beside each of its four legs, polished bright enough to show a reflection. The students were crammed in tight; there were people sitting even on the windowsills.

The head of the academy, Yanqing, sat in the place of honour. He was about forty, his hair coiled in a topknot crossed by a pin inlaid with green jade; he wore a dark green robe with wide sleeves, the cuffs edged with gold-thread scrollwork, the lining ivory white. His eyes were very steady, the short beard under his chin neatly trimmed. Behind him stood Wen Yanli: a long grey-white robe, a broad indigo sash at the waist, long hair half grey and half black, coiled into a bun held with a blue-tasseled pin, the rest falling down his back; half-rimmed copper-wire spectacles on his nose, a tuft of goatee on his chin, a blue-covered arithmetic classic tucked under one arm and an ebony ferule in the other hand, its tip tapping his palm.

Qi Tingjun did not sit in the place of honour. He had pulled up an old bench and sat in the corner by the window, the hem of his xiezhi robes pressed on the bench, the Balance blade across his knees, while at the door stood two guards, each a Guard of Balance. He had come early and, as soon as he arrived, had made a round of the whole lecture hall front and back—the back door, the window lattices, the passage to the kitchen—even knocking on the pillar where the page boys stood.

Xiao Cheng'an sat leaning on his cane in the witness seat: a small black satin cap, a short white beard, a brown gold-patterned overcoat over a long black gown, an old folding fan resting on his knee. Beside his hand was a small wooden box, its lid sealed with a sealing strip of the Arbitration Office.

Yuening spread out the original account kept by the academy and the scroll of the game record copied by the academy's chronicler side by side, and read aloud with her fingertip on the lines:

“In the third round, the South seat discarded a Two of Dots, the West seat punged, three tiles laid out; in the seventh round, the East seat discarded another Two of Dots; in the eleventh round, the North seat discarded one more Two of Dots. Three plus one, plus one more: five.” She looked up. “A set has four of each tile. At that table last month, there was one Two of Dots too many.”

A buzz went through the lecture hall.

“Wins and losses played with a set that has an extra tile do not count.” Xiao Cheng'an tapped his folding fan lightly on the edge of the table. “That is an old rule of the Arbitration Office, not something I made up today. Reviewing under the original agreement: the original record does not tally, the old ruling is suspended, and as of today Hengchang may not fell the bamboo. If both parties are willing, there will be a rematch here and now, and the rematch will decide the back hill; if not, the old case will be investigated as it stands and judged once it is clear.”

Someone in the back row stood up.

Qian Youqing. The same brown gold-embroidered robe, so fat he stretched it taut and round, the big string of dark prayer beads hanging on his chest, each bead the size of a hen's egg, the head bead bigger still. The day before yesterday at the Rain Pavilion, it was while rolling these beads that he had shouted “Search him!”

“Willing.” He rolled the prayer beads fast. “Drag it out, and the bamboo will just grow old. This time I'll sit myself.”

“And the academy?” Xiao Cheng'an looked at Yanqing.

Yanqing did not answer at once. He first turned to look at Wen Yanli and Du Yunwen. Wen Yanli's ferule stopped. Du Yunwen drew his right hand from his sleeve and pressed it on his knee, held it down; the fingertips were still twitching.

“Each side fields two seats,” Yuening said. “For the academy, it ought to be Mr. Wen and Assistant tutor Du.”

“I can't sit.” Wen Yanli's voice was dry. “In that game last month, I was the academy's guarantor of the agreement; I pressed the guarantee seal on the agreement. A guarantor may not turn around and take a seat in the rematch. It's printed on the back of the agreement.”

The lecture hall fell silent for a moment.

“The academy asks Young Master Qing to take a seat,” Yanqing said at last.

The flesh under Qian Youqing's eyelids twitched. He was about to speak, but Yuening had already pushed another sheet of paper across. “Substitute player, or a party in his own right—that has to be written down clearly first.”

“What's the difference?” Jinshi asked Zhumo in a whisper.

“A substitute player sits for someone else; the win or loss belongs to the original party, and so does the Mahjong Jade,” said Zhumo. “A party sits for himself; the win or loss is his own, and if he wins, the Mahjong Jade goes to him.”

“On the academy's side, no substitute player is needed.” From his sleeve Yanqing took out the academy's register, turned to the last page, wrote a line with his brush, and stamped it with the private seal of the head of the academy. “From today, Qinglan is a visiting student of the Songtao Academy. The academy's affairs, the academy's people sit for.”

“A visiting student?” Qian Youqing sneered. “The head of the academy is quick with his brush.”

“The academy has never asked where a visiting student comes from, only whether he is willing to study.” Yanqing turned the register around to show Xiao Cheng'an. “The previous head of the academy took in a night-soil carrier, and the one before him took in a street singer. If Elder Xiao finds it improper, please say so.”

Xiao Cheng'an glanced at it and nodded. “Proper. The register is dated today, and so is the agreement. The order is clear.”

Qian Youqing looked at Qinglan, then shot a quick glance toward the northeast corner of the lecture hall, then sat back down and wound his prayer beads once. “Accepted. Hengchang's other seat, our bookkeeper will take.”

A thin man of about forty with an abacus squeezed out of the back row, bowed to both sides, and sat down at the table without a word. He was no one of name or reputation, not to be found on the rankings of the mahjong guild, only the man who kept the day ledger at Hengchang's counter.

The seats were drawn and fixed: East seat, Hengchang's bookkeeper; South seat, Du Yunwen; West seat, Qian Youqing; North seat, Qinglan.

Yuening spread out the agreement and read it clause by clause:

“The Tuidao School, four games, the dealer does not rotate. In each game the East seat takes one extra tile and discards first; the others hold thirteen and wait to draw. The third tile from the tail of the wall is turned to fix the wild tile; the wild tile may act as a wild tile; the turned tile itself stays in the wall and counts only once. Pung and kong allowed, no chow; Seven Pairs may win. An ordinary win is four points, Seven Pairs sixteen points. On a deal in, the discarder pays; on a self-draw, each of the other three seats pays; payments between partners on the same side cancel out.”

She paused, then read the next clause:

“It is permitted to declare ready. A player who has declared ready receives an extra four points on winning; once ready is declared, the hand is locked, and the player may only discard whatever he draws. A player who draws his own winning tile and does not win, but discards it, has passed on the win and may not win again in that game.”

When Qinglan heard this clause, his eyelid flickered.

That morning, in the storytelling room on the second floor of the Rain Pavilion, Qi Tingjun had tested him with nine discards, and this was exactly what he had tested: a player who has declared ready will not pass on a win and then go back to draw the same tile. He had just used this rule once, and by afternoon it had been written into the agreement.

“This clause was added at Hengchang's request,” said Yuening, without any rise or fall in her voice. “The academy did not object.”

Qian Youqing rolled his prayer beads with a click. “For someone who plays slowly, declaring ready and taking a gamble is a way to stay alive too.”

“The net points over four games decide the back hill.” Yuening read the last line. “A tie goes to the owner before the original ruling, namely the academy.”

Qian Youqing's hand stopped. “That's in the agreement?”

“It was in last month's original agreement, Article Nine.” Yuening turned the original agreement to that page. “A tie goes to the original owner. Your firm's proprietor pressed his fingerprint on it.”

Qian Youqing said nothing more.

“Today we decide property. No gold coin is staked, and no spirit breath is staked.” Yuening closed the agreement and ran her thumb along the fold; her nails were trimmed round and showed a faint pink. “Under the table, at the four corners, is a copper boundary. Whoever moves a single thread of spirit breath, the copper boundary will light by itself, and it will be judged to betray the Way. No passing of tile information from outside the table. If you see anything unusual, report it to the match supervisor first; do not touch anyone else's hand yourself.”

Xiao Cheng'an laid his folding fan on the edge of the table. “Both parties press the agreement, I set the seal, and we begin.”

Qinglan sat down and, through two layers of cloth, pressed a hand over his heart.

The White Dragon in the red cloth wrapping was warm, its back unmarked, not a single line. The root of that strand in his body was still there, but not a single thread was left on it; it had been drained dry yesterday and had not grown back yet. At this moment he was just like any spectator in the lecture hall, unable to move anything at all.

Just as well, he thought. The four copper plates under the table could sleep soundly today.

He rested his right arm on the edge of the table. The arm was still numb; lifted halfway, it sank again, and the wide sleeve of his old blue robe hung down and covered half the corner of the table. He built the wall with his left hand.

In the first game, the wild tile turned up was the Five of Dots.

By the sixth round, Qinglan knew this game could not be saved. Not because his tiles were bad, but because he was slow: he had to draw with one hand and sort with the same hand, and watch the discards of the other three seats besides. Only when he pulled his mind away from his tiles did he realize that two things in the lecture hall matched.

One was the fan in the northeast corner.

The other was Qian Youqing's prayer beads.

The fan paused twice, the prayer beads paused twice; the fan closed and opened, and Qian Youqing reached out to draw a tile from the wall. His hand knew what to do before his eyes did.

In the ninth round, Du Yunwen drew a tile. His right hand could not hold it, so he switched to the left, and held the tile pinched between his middle and index fingers, hesitating a long while. The Six of Characters in his hand was surplus; discarding it meant throwing a fresh tile, and keeping it would make nothing either. He gritted his teeth and discarded it.

“Win.”

Qian Youqing pushed his tiles over. The Six of Characters filling the middle, an ordinary win, four points.

Du Yunwen's left hand froze on the table, and it was a long while before he drew it back.

“The academy, minus four points,” Yuening wrote in the ledger, her voice flat.

Qinglan did not look at Qian Youqing. He looked at the northeast corner.

Seeing Qian Youqing win, the page boy had stopped his fan and was clutching it in his palm. Qinglan took a second look at him: he was half a head taller than the other page boys, his face pressed under a small cloth cap so it could not be seen clearly, but the hand gripping the fan showed—coarse on the back, big-knuckled, not a scholar's hand, but a hand that had carried trays and hauled crates.

Stranger still was his right hand.

He did not fan with his right hand. His right hand stayed clenched in a fist, drawn up inside his cuff.

The White Dragon over his heart went cold for a while, and the cold was not frost but a roughness, as if something two zhang away were licking at it.

When it came time to shuffle the tiles, Qinglan turned sideways and asked a small page boy standing behind him to pour tea: “How many page boys does your academy have?”

The small page boy, ten or so, holding a copper kettle, thought about it. “Seven.”

“And that one?” Qinglan lifted his chin toward the northeast corner.

The small page boy rose on tiptoe and looked, looked for a long while, and the color drained from his face all at once. “I don't know him… there are only seven of us.”

Qinglan shook his head at him, telling him not to make a sound.

He turned and gave Zhumo a look, tilting his head toward the back door. Zhumo tugged Jinshi's sleeve and said something in his ear. Jinshi hoisted the lion head onto his shoulder and went out by the side door, looking for all the world as though he were off to find somewhere in the back courtyard to set down the lion head. Just outside the back door of the lecture hall was the back courtyard; the back courtyard connected to the kitchen, and behind the kitchen was a low wall, and beyond the wall was the back hill. If anyone wanted to slip quietly out of this lecture hall, that was the only way.

He did not speak up to report to the match supervisor. Speak up, and the page boy would put away his fan, Qian Youqing would brush it off with “the child was hot and fanning himself,” and nothing would stick. He only turned his head to glance at Qi Tingjun, then flicked his eyes toward the pillar in the northeast corner. Qi Tingjun sat by the window, eyes half shut as if dozing, but his eyelids lowered a fraction, which counted as an answer. From where he sat, he could see the pillar in the northeast corner of the lecture hall, and the back door as well.

In the second game, the wild tile was the One of Bamboo.

Qinglan sat North, his back to the north wall. The pillar in the northeast corner was behind him to his left, two zhang away. From where that page boy stood, he could see over Qinglan's left shoulder to the row of tiles standing in front of him—the greater part on the left clearly, while one corner of the right end was hidden by his own drooping right sleeve.

His right arm was numb, resting on the edge of the table, and the sleeve simply hung there. It could not be helped, and everyone could see it.

In the seventh round, his tiles were these: Three of Characters, Four of Characters; Six of Bamboo, Seven of Bamboo, Eight of Bamboo; Two of Dots, Three of Dots, Four of Dots; three Seven of Dots; a pair of East Wind. Thirteen tiles.

Waiting on the Two of Characters or the Five of Characters; the One of Bamboo was the wild tile, and drawing it would also win.

With his left hand he moved the Three of Characters half an inch to the right, under his sleeve. The cuff pressed over the tops of the tiles, and the top stroke of the Three of Characters was covered in the sleeve's shadow; seen from two zhang behind him to the left, only the two lower strokes remained.

A “two.”

He moved the Four of Characters over as well, set it up snug beside it, and then did not move.

The fan stopped. Once, again, and once more, with a close-and-open in between.

Qian Youqing's prayer beads paused with it. He looked down at his own tiles. He held two Three of Characters—this Qinglan had deduced from the way he played in the third round: in that round he would rather break a pair of Nine of Dots than touch his Characters, so among his Characters there had to be a pair he could not bear to give up; later, when Four of Characters went into the river one after another, he never even glanced at them, so his pair could only be the Three of Characters.

The fan told him: North's hand had a Two of Characters and a Four of Characters, waiting on the Three of Characters in the middle.

There were four Three of Characters in all. Qian Youqing held two himself, one was already in the river, and where the last one was, no one knew. Waiting in the middle on the very last tile of its kind—North's hand was as good as dead.

Then the Five of Characters was safe. North had no use for a Five of Characters.

In the ninth round, Qian Youqing drew a tile, thought a moment, and pushed out the Five of Characters.

“Win.”

Qinglan laid his tiles down. Three of Characters, Four of Characters, Five of Characters; Six, Seven, Eight of Bamboo; Two, Three, Four of Dots; three Seven of Dots; a pair of East Wind. Fourteen tiles, four sets and a pair.

For an instant the lecture hall was silent, and then someone cried out.

Qian Youqing's face went first red, then white. He stared at that Three of Characters, at the stroke along its top. The stroke was right there, black and straight; a moment ago the sleeve had covered it, and now the sleeve had moved away.

“An ordinary win, four points,” Yuening wrote. “The West seat dealt in. The academy, plus four. Over the first two games, a tie.”

Qian Youqing did not look at the northeast corner again. He rolled his prayer beads too fast; the newly replaced cord was a size thinner than the bead holes, and the beads slipped out of place, clicking and clacking.

In the third game, the wild tile was the North Wind.

When it came time to shuffle the tiles, the fan in the northeast corner did not stop again, fanning on and on, very fast, as if the man himself could no longer stand the heat. Qian Youqing's prayer beads did not pause once. He no longer trusted the fan, Qinglan could see that; what he trusted was something else—he believed North's last hand had been put on deliberately for the fan to see, and so whatever North displayed this game ought to be read in reverse.

So Qinglan let him look.

This game he did not lay his right sleeve on the table. He drew the numb arm down onto his knee, and with his left hand stood his tiles up neat and square, so that whoever looked from whatever angle could see them perfectly clearly.

He held two Four of Bamboo.

In the fifth round, the bookkeeper in the East seat discarded a Four of Bamboo, and it went into the river.

By the tenth round, Qian Youqing's eyes were darting back and forth between North's tiles and the river: there were four Four of Bamboo in all; North had two standing, one was in the river, and the remaining one was in his own hand. North was openly displaying two Four of Bamboo for all to see—this was baiting him to discard a Four of Bamboo; in other words, North did not want a Four of Bamboo; North wanted something else.

He kept his eyes on North the whole time.

So he never looked at Du Yunwen in the South seat.

Du Yunwen had not said a word all game. His right hand rested under the table; he discarded with his left, and very slowly, as slowly as if waiting for someone to hurry him. His tiles were these: One, Two, Three of Characters; three Five of Dots; Seven, Eight, Nine of Dots; a pair of West Wind; a Two of Bamboo and a Three of Bamboo. Thirteen tiles, waiting on the One of Bamboo or the Four of Bamboo; the North Wind was the wild tile, and drawing a North Wind would also win. Four of Bamboo: one in the river, two with North, and the remaining one—

In the eleventh round, Qian Youqing discarded the Four of Bamboo.

“Win.” Du Yunwen's voice was so soft he could not hear it clearly himself, so he said it again. “Win.”

With his left hand he pushed his tiles over. Two, Three, Four of Bamboo; One, Two, Three of Characters; three Five of Dots; Seven, Eight, Nine of Dots; a pair of West Wind.

“An ordinary win, four points,” Yuening wrote. “The West seat dealt in. The academy, plus four. Over three games, the academy is net plus four points.”

The lecture hall exploded. Students crowded on the windowsills slapped their thighs and shouted. Wen Yanli knocked his ferule hard against his palm with a sharp crack that startled even himself.

Du Yunwen lowered his head and looked at his right hand that would not straighten. The fingers curled and loosened, but in the end did not close.

Qinglan reached out his left hand across the corner of the table and touched the back of Du Yunwen's left hand.

“It wasn't me,” he said. “You worked it out yourself. You were ready, you didn't throw a single tile carelessly, and you waited all the way to the eleventh round for it to come.”

In the fourth game, the wild tile turned up was the Green Dragon.

The turned tile stayed in the wall; the remaining three Green Dragons could each be used as any tile at all.

Qian Youqing's calculations, Qinglan had worked out for him perfectly clearly.

After three games, the academy was net plus four points. If this game ended in a drawn hand, the back hill went to the academy. If Qian Youqing won on a discard, four points, it would only bring things level, and a tie went to the original owner; the back hill would still be the academy's. If he won by self-draw, the two academy seats would each pay four points, and the bookkeeper's share, being on the same side, would cancel out: Hengchang net plus eight points, minus four, plus four—only then would the back hill go to Hengchang.

But to declare ready changed everything. To declare ready added four points to a win. Win on a discard, eight points, minus four, Hengchang net plus four—a win; self-draw, the two academy seats each pay eight points, Hengchang net plus sixteen, minus four, plus twelve—an even bigger win.

Declare ready, and as long as he could win, no matter whose discard he won on, Hengchang won.

That was why he had wanted that clause added.

In the fourth round, Qian Youqing drew a tile and discarded it without even looking: a Nine of Characters. “Declare ready.”

Yuening wrote: “West seat, fourth round: declare ready, discarding the Nine of Characters.”

From this moment, the thirteen tiles in his hand were locked tight; whatever he drew, he had to discard, and not one tile could be changed.

Qinglan looked down at his own tiles.

His starting hand of thirteen: a pair of Two of Characters, a pair of Three of Bamboo, a pair of Seven of Bamboo, one Five of Characters, one Six of Dots, one Nine of Dots, one East Wind, one South Wind, one North Wind, one Green Dragon.

In the first round he drew a Five of Characters, making a pair, and discarded the East Wind. In the second round he drew a Six of Dots, making a pair, and discarded the North Wind. In the third round he drew a One of Bamboo and discarded it. In the fourth round he drew an Eight of Characters and discarded that too.

Now his hand was: Two of Characters, Five of Characters, Three of Bamboo, Seven of Bamboo, Six of Dots—five pairs, ten tiles; plus one Nine of Dots, one South Wind, one Green Dragon. Thirteen tiles.

Seven Pairs, one pair short.

The Green Dragon was the wild tile. If a Nine of Dots came, the wild tile would match the South Wind; if a South Wind came, the wild tile would match the Nine of Dots; and if he drew another Green Dragon, two wild tiles, one matching the Nine of Dots and one the South Wind—Seven Pairs as well.

Nine of Dots: Du Yunwen had discarded one in the second round, he held one himself, and two were still out there. South Wind: one in the river, one his own, and also two out there.

He did not know what Qian Youqing was waiting on. He knew only that Qian Youqing had declared ready, so his hand was locked tight. A locked hand that drew a tile it could not use had no choice but to discard it.

As long as what Qian Youqing drew was a Nine of Dots, or a South Wind—

The fifth round. The East seat and the South seat had both drawn and both discarded, and no one had won.

It came to the West seat, and Qian Youqing reached out to the wall to draw a tile.

Just then there was a sound at the back door of the lecture hall.

Not someone pushing it open. Something outside the door had come to rest, lightly, against the doorframe.

The White Dragon over Qinglan's heart went suddenly cold.

Not the roughness of before, but cold—cold as if someone had pressed a lump of ice against his heart through his clothes, with frost still forming beneath the ice. His left hand stopped at the edge of the wall, the fingertips gone numb.

He did not turn around. He looked from the corner of his eye.

The back door stood open a crack, and in the crack stood a man. His woven hat was pulled very low, his face unseen. He raised one hand and, with two fingers, lifted the brim of the woven hat a little, lifting it a sliver, then let it fall.

It was the left hand. A black leather glove, a little hand embroidered in gold thread on its back. The other hand was drawn into his sleeve and never came out.

Some two hundred people sat in the lecture hall, and hardly any looked toward the back door. The students were all craning their necks at the mahjong table, at the fat hand in the West seat reaching to draw a tile.

But Qian Youqing saw him.

His hand stopped in midair, the tile he had just drawn pinched in his fingers, not yet turned over. On his face there came first a look of relief, like a drowning man who sees someone on the bank; then that look drained away without a trace, and something else took its place.

The man he saw had not come to save him.

The four copper plates under the table stayed dark. Not a thread of light.

Qinglan stared at the four copper plates, and his mind raced through it: the man outside the door was present, the White Dragon had gone cold, yet the copper plates had not lit. That meant he had done nothing, had not moved a single thread of breath, only stood there and let the one who was meant to see him see him.

He had come to watch. To watch Qian Youqing, to watch this game, and to watch him.

By the window, Qi Tingjun opened his eyes. He did not rise; his right hand came to rest on the hilt of his blade, and he tilted his head toward a Guard of Balance at the door. The guard walked toward the back door, not quickly, as though going to shut a door the wind had blown open.

The man in the crack of the door was gone.

The cold of the White Dragon receded after him, slowly, like a tide.

Qian Youqing turned the tile over and looked at it.

The Nine of Dots.

He had declared ready. His hand was locked. The Nine of Dots was not his winning tile; he could only discard it.

He held the Nine of Dots for a long time, so long that the bookkeeper softly prompted, “Proprietor,” so long that Yuening raised her eyes to look at him. His fingers were trembling. It was not that he did not know the Nine of Dots was dangerous—there was only one in the river, and everything North had discarded this game was Wind tiles and fresh tiles, clean as a blank sheet of paper. But he had declared ready, locked his hand, and could change nothing.

It was the clause he himself had asked to add.

The Nine of Dots dropped into the river.

“Win,” said Qinglan.

He laid his tiles down: a pair of Two of Characters, a pair of Five of Characters, a pair of Three of Bamboo, a pair of Seven of Bamboo, a pair of Six of Dots, a pair of Nine of Dots, and the South Wind with the Green Dragon for a pair. Seven Pairs, fourteen tiles.

At first there was no sound at all in the lecture hall; then the whole place exploded, and dust sifted down from the roof.

“Seven Pairs, sixteen points.” Yuening's brush tip paused on the paper a moment before it came down. “The West seat dealt in.”

She wrote another line, and when she had finished, raised her head and read it item by item:

“First game, the academy minus four; second game, plus four; third game, plus four; fourth game, plus sixteen. Over four games, the academy is net plus twenty. In accordance with the agreement, the bamboo grove on the back hill goes to the academy.”

Qian Youqing sat without moving. The hand with which he had chosen to declare ready still stood in front of him, thirteen tiles, not one laid down. Qinglan glanced at it: One, Two, Three of Characters; Six, Seven, Eight of Characters; Four, Five, Six of Dots; a pair of East Wind; a Seven of Bamboo and an Eight of Bamboo. Waiting on the Six of Bamboo or the Nine of Bamboo.

Qinglan had held a pair of Seven of Bamboo. In this game, not one Six of Bamboo or Nine of Bamboo had shown itself. Had Qian Youqing drawn a Six of Bamboo or a Nine of Bamboo this game, or drawn a Green Dragon, he would have won.

All he had lacked was that one tile.

Yanqing, head of the academy, stepped forward and pressed his seal. Xiao Cheng'an set down the witness seal. Qian Youqing stared at the last stroke on the agreement, his lips quivered twice, and without raising any objection he pressed his fingerprint in Hengchang's column, half of it smudged askew.

Wen Yanli's ferule fell to the floor. He bent to pick it up, and when he straightened, the space behind his spectacles was wet; he wiped it with his sleeve, pretending it was dust.

Xiao Cheng'an, before everyone, broke the sealing strip on the Arbitration Office's small wooden box.

The box was lined with a yellow silk sash, and on the silk lay a piece of Mahjong Jade, emerald green, a little longer than a thumb, auspicious clouds carved in relief on its face. Xiao Cheng'an picked it up and, with a small carving knife, cut today's date into the back: the sixteenth of the fifth month.

“North seat, for the Songtao Academy, Qinglan,” he said. “Having won a formal sworn match of honour, he is awarded one piece of Mahjong Jade. This one is your own.”

Qinglan reached out his left hand and took it. The jade was cool, a little powder still in the fresh cuts.

Two pieces now.

“Mahjong Jade records only how many matches you have won,” Xiao Cheng'an added, “not how much skill you have. Today's match you won with one hand and a pair of eyes, so the jade ought to be solid and true.”

Someone in the lecture hall laughed out loud—laughing because he had seen that drooping right arm.

Qinglan laughed too. But while he laughed, his eyes went to the northeast corner.

Beneath that pillar there stood seven page boys still.

The eighth was gone.

From the back courtyard came a dull thud, like a sack of rice hitting the ground.

Qinglan was the first on his feet. He rose too fast, and his numb right arm struck the corner of the table so hard his vision went black, but he paid it no mind and ran around the mahjong table toward the back door. Zhumo followed with the bamboo basket on his back, Yuening followed Zhumo with the ledger in her arms, and Qi Tingjun had somehow already reached the door, faster than any of them.

The back courtyard was not large: a well, a jujube tree, a thin wisp of smoke still rising from the kitchen chimney. At the foot of the low wall, Jinshi was half kneeling on the ground, his knee pinning a man down.

The man wore a page boy's blue cotton clothes, his small cloth cap rolled off to one side, revealing a face of about twenty, with a black mole on one cheekbone.

Qinglan knew this face.

The day before yesterday at the Rain Pavilion—Hengchang's brown-clad attendant who had carried the red-lacquered tea tray and served tea to Luo Jinke. From the bottom of that tea tray, a needle had later been turned up.

“Over the wall,” Jinshi panted. “I was crouching at the kitchen door and saw him slip out the back door of the lecture hall—he even ran one of his shoes off. I jumped on him, and he…”

He did not finish. They had all seen it.

The attendant was shaking. Not the shaking of fear, but of cold; his teeth chattered, his lips were blue. It was the fifth month, the sun not yet down, and a layer of white had formed on his eyebrows.

His right hand was clenched tight in a fist. Between the fingers of the fist, frost was seeping out.

“Open your hand,” said Qi Tingjun.

The attendant shook his head like a rattle drum.

Jinshi went to pry his fingers open; he pried one open, and another curled back. At the third, Jinshi himself sucked in a cold breath and let go. “It's ice! Like touching a lump of ice!”

The fist opened all the same. Not pried open—the hand opened by itself, the five fingers stiffly splayed, unable to close again.

On the palm a hand was drawn.

Drawn in ink, the size of the palm, five fingers spread, the ink a greyish color with fine white mixed in, like powder ground from bone. The hand was drawn with great practice, every knuckle there in a single stroke; but the drawing stopped at the heel of the hand, and below the heel it was clean, with nothing at all.

Frost crept outward from the fingertips of that ink hand, over the attendant's wrist, up toward his forearm. Wherever it passed, the skin turned first white, then bluish.

Qinglan took a step forward.

The White Dragon over his heart sank heavily, so cold he could barely breathe. Almost at the same moment, the attendant screamed—the frost seemed to have scented something, shot upward, and in an instant leapt past his elbow.

“Back!” Zhumo seized the back of Qinglan's robe and hauled him back. “It's smelled you! It's smelled that piece you're carrying!”

Qinglan was dragged two stumbling steps backward. He watched the frost; it was still creeping, only a little slower.

“What is this thing?” Yuening asked, her voice tight, though her brush never stopped; she had already written in the ledger: “Back courtyard, attendant, right palm.”

“A reversed thing.” Zhumo's voice was hoarse. “Heavenly Tiles give breath outward; this thing only draws inward, and what it draws in never comes out. It was meant to draw on Heavenly Tiles, and since it can't get near Heavenly Tiles, it draws on people first. People have no breath in them, so it draws their warmth. He stood in the lecture hall all afternoon with this drawn on him, two zhang from you, and that White Dragon of yours was rough all afternoon—it was reaching for your tile. When it couldn't reach, it filled itself with him.”

“Then wipe it off!” said Jinshi.

“Bone-powder ink doesn't wipe off,” said Zhumo. “It takes plant-ash water, soaked through, before it'll dissolve. I'll go boil some in the kitchen. It'll take the time it takes to drink a cup of tea.”

“A cup of tea?” Jinshi looked at the attendant's arm; the frost had already crept to the upper arm. “In a cup of tea, he'll be cold all the way through!”

Qinglan pressed his left hand over his heart.

He thought of moving the White Dragon away—the White Dragon was close, so the thing drew hard; but moving it away would only slow it. He wanted to do something else. He felt the tile; its back was bare, unmarked. He searched inside himself and found the root of that strand; the root was there, but not a single thread was on it.

He could move nothing.

For the first time he knew so clearly what “zero” meant: a man standing outside a burning house, gripping an empty bucket.

Jinshi looked at Qinglan, then at the attendant, gritted his teeth, set the lion head on the ground, and drew the stone tile from the yellow silk sash at his waist.

It was a dull, dusty thing, looking like an old bone tile broken off a brick, its face worn smooth so that no one could tell which tile it had once been. This morning in the storeroom he had used it in front of Qinglan for the first time, and even now a round red mark remained on his palm.

“Jinshi—” said Qinglan.

“Don't reason with me.” Jinshi pressed the stone tile into that frosted palm and held it down with his own hand. “The old troupe master used to say: a lion dance takes two people, a head and a tail; however tired the one at the tail gets, while the lion hasn't touched the ground, he's not allowed to let go first.”

He drew a breath and sent it out.

On the worn face of the tile two circles surfaced—a Two of Dots. Beneath the stone tile, two rings of faint golden light rippled outward, one inside the other, circling past the heel of the attendant's hand and hooping the ink hand inside them. The frost paused.

But only for a moment. The ink hand seemed enraged; its ink darkened, and the frost bulged out from under the golden rings, pushing open a fine crack.

Jinshi drew another breath and sent it out again.

The two rings of golden light flared and pressed down tight once more, shutting the ink hand in completely. The frost stopped creeping and drew back, back to the wrist, back to the palm.

Jinshi's face had gone white. The hand pressing the stone tile was trembling, the round mark on his palm glowing red, and beneath the red a shiny blister had swelled up.

“Two breaths,” Zhumo said beside him. “That's enough. Send any more and you'll go cold yourself.”

“Panda,” said Jinshi without turning, “the ash water.”

Zhumo was already running for the kitchen.

In the kitchen an old woman who tended the fire was cowering behind the stove in fright. Zhumo did not speak to her; he opened the firebox, scooped out a ladle of ash, tipped it into the pot, added water, added firewood. Qinglan followed him in and worked the bellows for him—with his left hand. Yuening stood in the doorway, the ledger tucked in the crook of her arm, her eyes roving over the kitchen.

“What's that?” she said suddenly.

In the pile of firewood beside the stove a big bundle of something dark brown was stuffed, wrinkled, tied with straw rope; from the rope hung a small wooden tag branded with a few characters: Nanchao Sea Goods.

Kelp.

Qinglan's hand on the bellows stopped.

“It was sent this spring,” the old woman behind the stove said, trembling. “Hengchang's people sent it, said it was a gift of respect to the academy, to add a dish for the teachers. They sent two big bundles; one's been eaten, and this one's left. It's dried—it keeps.”

“That game last month,” Yuening asked, “who came to the kitchen to boil the tea water?”

The old woman thought. “There were a lot of people that day, and Hengchang lent us an attendant to help in the kitchen. It was… it was that one outside. He said tea water has to be steeped with kelp to taste sweet, and he cut half a sheet of kelp himself and threw it in the kettle…”

Yuening crouched, drew a sheet from the bundle of kelp, and shook it open against the light from the door. Caught in the folds were a few bits of broken leaf, smaller than a fingernail, greyish brown, with serrated edges.

She turned and saw Qi Tingjun standing in the kitchen doorway.

Qi Tingjun took a flat little wooden box from inside his robe. Its sealing strip was intact—it was the one the evidence courier had resealed in public that morning. Before setting out, he had asked for it back from a Guard of Balance, had not returned it to the Bureau of Balance's storehouse, and had carried it on him all the way to the academy.

“When Doctor Lan left, he mentioned the Nanchao sea-goods shop,” he said. “I thought, if it could be of use at the academy, there was no sense letting it sleep in the storehouse.”

In front of Yuening he tore open the sealing strip, took out the packet of medicine, peeled back the oilpaper, peeled back the kelp, picked out a fragment of leaf with tweezers, and laid it beside the one in Yuening's hand.

The same greyish brown, the same serrations.

“The same bundle of goods,” said Yuening. “Assistant tutor Du's hand wasn't ruined by the scald. The boiling water was a blind. What mattered was the kelp steeping in the kettle.”

“The medicine came from the same bundle too,” said Qi Tingjun. “The poison was taken from this bundle, and so was the antidote. Whoever sent the poison had the antidote all prepared as well, and tucked it in Zheng Shouzhuo's sleeve.”

He wrapped the two fragments separately, added a line on the small box's resealing strip, pressed his fingerprint, and handed it to Yuening. Yuening pressed the second.

The ash water in the pot came to the boil.

When the ash water was splashed on the attendant's hand, the ink hand twisted like a living thing.

Zhumo let no one else do it. He wiped with a piece of coarse cloth dipped in ash water himself; with each layer he wiped, a layer of ink dissolved, and the dissolved ink was black. Where it fell on the ground, the earth formed a thin layer of frost, and the frost was melted again by the sun. When he wiped down to the heel of the hand and the last stroke of ink came off, the attendant let out a long breath and slumped on the ground, a little color returning to his face.

Only then did Jinshi lift the stone tile away. The two rings of golden light scattered; he sat down hard on the ground and tucked that hand inside his jacket, and kept it there a good while before taking it out. The blister had burst, and his fingertips were numb.

“It's nothing,” he said, and seeing Qinglan's face, added, “Really, it's nothing. Better than holding up the lion all day.”

When the attendant came to, the first thing he saw was Qi Tingjun's xiezhi robes, and he shrank into the corner of the wall.

“The tea tray you carried that day, the needle in its bottom—who gave it to you?” Qi Tingjun asked.

The attendant said nothing.

“The night before last you asked for leave to go home and see your mother,” Qi Tingjun went on. “My men went to your house. Your mother is lying in bed, with a prescription on the bedside. The medicines on it can't all be filled at the three pharmacies in the south of the city; it needs grass roots from the south. You went home to deliver medicine.”

The attendant's tears came pouring down all at once.

“The prescription is my mother's,” he wept. “She's been sick two years. The doctor said she needs grass roots from the south. We can't afford them, and we can't get them anyway… He said he had them, he'd give them to me every month, as long as I did a few jobs for him. Carrying tea, spilling water, standing under the pillar fanning today…”

“Who is he?”

The attendant's lips trembled a long while.

“The one in the woven hat,” he said. “I never saw his face. When he drew this hand on me, all I saw was his glove. He said, with this drawn on me, wherever I am, he'll know; and if I talk out of turn, this hand will suck my warmth dry.”

He looked down at his palm, clean now, and wept again. “Just now… he was at the back door. He looked at me once, and this hand started to go cold. I tried to run, got as far as the foot of the wall, and couldn't run any further.”

Qi Tingjun was silent a moment.

“Your mother,” he said, “my men will fetch her to the east of the city tonight and have Doctor Lan see her. The medicine, the Bureau of Balance will fill for you. You come back with me and tell everything from these three months, from the beginning, clearly.”

The attendant stared at him blankly, then suddenly threw himself down on the ground and kowtowed to him.

A Guard of Balance on each side, they hauled him away.

“If you see anything unusual, report it to the match supervisor first.” Qi Tingjun looked back at Qinglan. “You reported it—with your eyes. I saw the fan in the first game. Call a halt on the spot, and he'd brush it all away with 'the page boy was hot.' I waited for him to walk out the back door on his own—ink on his hand, a confession in his mouth. Only that counts as solid proof.”

In the lecture hall, Qian Youqing still sat in the West seat, the prayer beads clutched in his hand. Qi Tingjun presented the attendant's confession and the bowl of plant-ash water together before the witness seat.

Xiao Cheng'an read it through and tapped the line “No passing of tile information from outside the table” on the agreement with the head of his cane. “Hengchang's side passed tile information from outside the table; there were fan signals, there is a personal confession; it is judged to betray the Way. The academy has already won this match; one line is added to the ruling: Hengchang betrayed the Way and is judged the loser.” He set down the witness seal. “The man goes to the Bureau of Balance.”

“Qian Youqing,” said Qi Tingjun, “come back to the Bureau of Balance with me.”

Qian Youqing opened his mouth but no words came. A Guard of Balance on his left and another on his right lifted him up, and the string of prayer beads knocked once against the threshold with a click.

Qinglan went back to the back courtyard, stood by the well, and looked down at his left hand.

He was thinking about that ink hand. Five fingers, spread, bone powder mixed in the ink, drawn with great practice. But below the heel of the hand, it was empty.

He took the White Dragon from inside his robe and, shielding it with his body, turned it over and looked. The little hand carved in the lower right corner of the tile's back also had five fingers spread; below the heel of the hand were several tiny dots, too small to make out, as if pricked by a needle point.

Those dots were not on the ink hand. Nor were they on the hand on the back of the plain-backed Red Dragon.

He wrapped the White Dragon back up, tucked it inside his robe, and said nothing.

The sun had slid a good way toward the west, and the academy's ginkgo trees stretched their shadows across the steps.

Qi Tingjun sat on the stone steps outside the lecture hall, the Balance blade upright before his knees, the red cord on its scabbard trailing to the ground. Qinglan sat down beside him, one person's width apart.

Qi Tingjun took an oilpaper packet from inside his robe and opened it. Inside was a letter, its envelope yellowed, its corners worn fuzzy.

“Zheng Shouzhuo's letter of recommendation,” he said. “I opened it and read it. It was written by an old clerk of the Scorekeepers' Institute, recommending the Zheng boy to enter the Scorekeepers' Institute to study scorekeeping, saying the child reckons quickly and his heart is upright. The old clerk's grandson had studied tiles under Zheng Shouzhuo for ten years, and he remembered the kindness.”

He turned the envelope over and pointed to a corner of the flap. There was a faint red stamp there, the receiving stamp of the gatehouse of the Scorekeepers' Institute in Tiandu, with a date inside it.

“The letter was written in Tiandu and registered right there at the gatehouse of the Scorekeepers' Institute,” said Qi Tingjun. “The date of registration is nine days before that game at the Wangjiang Teahouse.”

Qinglan looked at the date.

“That time Zheng Shouzhuo went to Tiandu, someone invited him to make up a table,” said Qi Tingjun. “He lost his land deed and this letter, and they gave them back to him on the spot, keeping only a copper coin. Within nine days, that game at the Wangjiang Teahouse was played.”

“The same man?”

“I don't know.” Qi Tingjun wrapped the letter up. “I only know that in the same month, in the same city, someone was looking for a face the whole city trusted.”

Neither of them spoke.

In the lecture hall, the students were still noisily taking apart the mahjong table, and someone was singing the academy song, off-key.

“Ten years ago, at that game at the Wangjiang Teahouse,” Qi Tingjun began, his voice still flat, “the match supervisor was my master. Gan Henian.”

Qinglan turned his head to look at him.

“I was seventeen, standing behind him, holding the body-search register,” said Qi Tingjun. “That game ruled your father a Ghost Hand, and my master signed the ruling. The night he signed it, he did not sleep. The next day he told me that the few lines he had written under the North seat's column after the game broke up were a sealed endorsement, folded and stamped across the seam, handed to the chronicler to be attached after the cut strip, awaiting re-examination someday. What he wrote, he did not tell me. He only said, 'This game isn't over.'”

“And then?”

“Then that sealed endorsement disappeared,” said Qi Tingjun. “The first entry at the head of the attached file was gone entirely, and the sealed endorsement joined to it was gone with it. He asked, and everyone said they didn't know. This morning, the panda gentleman told me that on that night at the inn, someone tore away that half of the original seal right in front of him. He never opened it, and doesn't know what was written inside.”

He paused.

“My master never saw it again either,” he said. “He wanted to reinvestigate that game and submitted two petitions; both were suppressed. Three months later, someone sent word to him at the Bureau of Balance: that half of the sealed endorsement still existed, its original seal unbroken, and if he wanted it, he could go out of the city to collect it.”

“He went.”

“He went. Alone, on horseback, out of Tiandu's North Gate, at night,” said Qi Tingjun. “The next morning someone found him at the foot of the North River embankment north of the city. The horse was grazing on the embankment, its saddle in perfect order. He had fallen onto the stones at the foot of the embankment, and his neck was broken.”

A ginkgo leaf fell and landed on the scabbard.

“The Bureau ruled it a fall from his horse,” said Qi Tingjun. “I went to collect his body. His right hand was clenched in a fist, clenched so tight it took me a long time to pry it open. In his palm was a Two of Dots.”

Qinglan's heart jumped.

The Two of Dots. The extra tile in the original record had been a Two of Dots.

“What kind of Two of Dots?”

“Bone face, bamboo back, looking just like the Two of Dots from any set,” said Qi Tingjun. “I handed it in, and it was sealed in the Bureau of Balance's evidence vault. I was seventeen that year. I didn't dare look.”

“Didn't dare look at what?”

“The back of the tile.” Qi Tingjun looked down at his own hands. “In ten years, I have never once turned it over to look at its back.”

He stood and hung the Balance blade back on his belt.

“After midnight,” he said, “you tell me whether that hand is the left or the right. When I'm back in Tiandu, I'll go and turn that Two of Dots over.”

Qinglan wanted to say that the man at the back door had lifted his woven hat with his left hand.

He opened his mouth, then shut it again. Seeing it once did not count. At midnight tonight, he would look once more.

Yuening was waiting for him at the door of the lecture hall.

In her hand was a length of blue silk cord, untied from her own brush; its end was a little frayed.

“The jade?”

Qinglan handed her the new piece of Mahjong Jade.

Yuening did not take it. She pointed at his waist. “Where did you hang the first one?”

The first was still tied to his belt with an old hemp cord knotted in a dead knot, the ends of the knot frayed. Yuening took one look, and her brows knit.

“You call this hanging?” she said. “That's how you tether a donkey.”

She crouched down at his waist, untied the old hemp cord, and threaded the two pieces of Mahjong Jade side by side onto the length of blue silk cord. Her fingers were very quick, as quick as working an abacus; the blue silk cord went through the holes in the jade and back again.

Qinglan stood and did not dare move.

She was crouched too close. Close enough that if he lowered his head he could see the crown of her head, the fine parting between her twin buns, the small white flower on the right bun with one petal creased by a fold. She smelled of ink, freshly ground ink, mixed with a little of the ebony of the abacus beads, and something else he couldn't name, very faint, like paper dried in the sun.

She was bent tying the knot, so close that he could see the small stretch of collarbone inside her crossed collar, delicate, rising and falling shallowly with her breath; below it, a line of shadow pressed under the green trim. His eyes bumped into it there and quickly sprang away. A stray strand of hair that had not been combed up hung down from her temple and brushed the back of his hand.

He hid that hand a little behind his back.

As she tied the knot, her knuckles pressed against his side through the cloth—press, a loop, press again. The spot had never been ticklish, but now it felt as if ants were crawling there, and every bit of strength in his body was strung tight on that one small patch.

“Stop holding your breath,” said Yuening, without looking up. “You're holding it so hard the cord under my hands is going taut with you.”

Only then did Qinglan realize he had been holding his breath all along, and hurriedly let it out—too fast, and nearly coughed.

Yuening tied a knot. It was the knot the scorekeepers used to seal boxes, loop pressed over loop, its face flat, like a small seal. When she had finished, she pressed the knot with her fingernail.

“This knot,” she said, standing up and brushing the dust from her skirt, “no one in Yunchuan can untie but me. If you lose it, it's a lost item of public property; if someone steals it from you, it's stolen goods.”

“Then is it mine, or the public's?”

“It's yours.” Yuening slid her brush back into her bun and turned to go. “But if it's lost, it's still something entered in my accounts.”

She took two steps, did not look back, and said one more thing: “Tonight you go to the archway; I'm not going. When you come back, I want to see these two still on this cord.”

Qinglan lowered his head and looked at the two pieces of Mahjong Jade side by side at his waist, emerald green, auspicious clouds, one carved with the fourteenth of the fifth month, one with the sixteenth of the fifth month.

When he looked up, Yuening had already reached the ginkgo tree. The setting sun leaked through the gaps in the leaves and fell on her hair bun. She reached up to tuck that stray strand back behind her ear, her fingertip sliding down along the curve of her ear and pausing a moment on the lobe, and her steps were a little quicker than when she had come.

When the party left the academy, more than half of Hengchang's row of wooden stakes at the foot of the back hill had already been pulled up by the students, the small yellow flags strewn all over the ground. Du Yunwen stood at the foot of the hill, steadying himself against a bamboo with his left hand, gazing up at the bamboo tips until the sun above them had moved on a little way.

Jinshi carried the lion head on his shoulder, that hand tucked inside his jacket, and did not take it out the whole way. Zhumo walked beside him, turning his head now and then to look at him, asking nothing.

When they passed the North Gate, the salt boat at the wharf had been unloaded. The porters were squatting on the bank eating, and that young man squatted at the very end, holding a coarse porcelain bowl of unpolished rice with a single chopstickful of pickled greens. He ate very fast, as if afraid someone would snatch it.

Qinglan walked past him.

He did not stop. He could give him nothing now: that letter of recommendation had to go back into the Bureau of Balance's box, that old man was locked in the Bureau of Balance's prison, and that face the whole city trusted had been borrowed and used by someone for ten years.

He only committed this face to memory.

The sun sank behind the hills west of the city, and the edge of the sky burned red. Yuening, walking in front with the ledger in her arms, suddenly stopped and looked back at the sky.

“It's the hour of the Rooster,” she said. “Less than three double-hours till midnight.”

Qinglan pressed his left hand over his heart.

The White Dragon was warm, its back unmarked. The root of that strand in his body was still there, and not a single thread was on it.

But at his waist there was one more piece of jade, and in his sleeve a copper coin with two file marks, a line of small characters carved on its back: Midnight, the old archway, collect one game.

(End of Chapter Five)
