Chapter 1One Hundred and Thirty-Seven

At the third watch of the night, someone was building a wall of tiles in the Rain Pavilion's deserted main hall.

Click. Click. Click-click.

Qinglan opened his eyes in the woodshed straw, thinking he had misheard. Rain had fallen for half the night; water streamed from the eaves. Yet through it came that unmistakable sound: bone against bone, one stack set upon another, swift and steady. An old hand.

Before nightfall, he had paid a waiter two copper coins for a dry corner of the woodshed. The waiter took the money, then doubled back before leaving, hunched his shoulders and whispered, “Sir, if you hear someone building a wall in the hall at night, whatever you do, don't go and look. That old table beneath the Mahjong God's shrine, behind the counter—it's haunted.”

At the time, he had thought the waiter was merely frightening a stranger. And a stranger he was now: he had lived in Yunchuan for a few years as a child, but more than a decade later, no one here knew him.

Now the red cloth wrapping against his heart moved, very slightly.

Neither cold nor heat. It felt as though someone had reached through his robe and tugged it with two fingers. Inside lay a White Dragon his father had left him, never once away from his body in ten years. It had never done this before.

Qinglan pushed aside the straw and felt his way barefoot to the rear window. It faced the side entrance of the Rain Pavilion's ground floor, where the door had been left a finger's width ajar. He brought his eye to the gap.

The hall was dark. An oilcloth covered the table of justice in the middle; four copper plates beside its legs caught a faint gleam, like four closed eyes. The light came from the east wall. Behind the long counter stood the Mahjong God's shrine, its ever-burning lamp no larger than a bean.

Beneath the shrine, against the wall, stood an old square wooden table with peeling paint. Usually it held only tea things.

Now the tea things had been pushed aside. Four walls of tiles stood on the table.

Four hands were building them.

Only hands. Grey-white, half transparent, as though seen through steam from a cooking basket. Above the wrists they faded to nothing, leaving only scraps of cuff suspended in the air. Yet their details were frighteningly clear: the eastern hand had thick knuckles; the southern one was small and thin, indigo lodged beneath its nails like a dyer's; the western one wore a copper ring; the northern one paused after every stack, as though counting.

The tiles were real: the old, retired set of bamboo-backed bone tiles kept beneath the shrine, their backs polished bright by use. The hands were insubstantial, yet they pushed the tiles along—building the walls, breaking them, dealing the starting hands—without a single mistake.

Qinglan's mouth went dry. He remembered something his father had told him when he was very small. One winter night, his father had pointed to an old mahjong table with a broken leg in an inn and said:

“Xiaolan, things that have spent long enough at a mahjong table remember people. Not the people themselves, but the one moment they cannot let go. Death alone makes no ghost; a lingering thought leaves a shadow. Most days it lies dead. But let a Heavenly Tile pass nearby, and it wakes and plays the hand exactly as it was.”

“Then we…”

“We carry a tile.” His father pressed his chest. “So we can see them. Other people can only hear.”

A bed creaked upstairs. The waiter on night duty rolled over, muttered, “At it again,” and pulled his quilt over his head.

He could hear. He could not see.

On the old table, a hand had already reached its middle stages. The thick-knuckled hand at East drew a tile, pulled its right wrist inward as though adjusting a cuff, then brought it back close along the standing tiles, swift as a swallow skimming water.

Had it been a living hand, Qinglan might not have seen clearly. But that sleeve was transparent. A tile lay hidden inside it, face outward, showing a spot of red. As the hand drew back, the red slipped into the standing tiles, and one of the tiles already there slipped into the sleeve.

A swap from the sleeve.

Just then a fifth hand reached from the corner of the table. Slender fingers held a folding fan. The ribs came across and pressed the eastern hand down, holding it utterly still.

The eastern hand froze. Its five fingers loosened.

Click.

A tile fell onto the table.

Then all four hands, and the one holding the fan, stopped together. Like a row of lamps extinguished by a single breath, they faded from the fingertips, thinned to smoke and dispersed.

Only the rain remained in the hall.

Only then did Qinglan feel the chill against his heart. He drew out the red wrapping and unfolded it in the sliver of lamplight. The White Dragon's bone-white face lay towards him. On the back, in the lower right corner, was a tiny engraved hand, its five fingers spread; below it, the almost invisible blue-green line had grown a thread thinner since evening.

His father had said that line came from the tile’s bearer, nurtured inside the tile; every bit spent was a bit less, and it could only be nursed back little by little. That was what the old table had borrowed when it woke.

He pushed open the side door and went inside.

The walls on the old square table had collapsed into a heap, as though someone had swept them down carelessly. Only one tile remained at the tail of what had been the eastern wall, its face up.

A Red Dragon.

Qinglan touched it. Cold against his fingertip. A real tile: old bamboo backing, its red paint faded to the colour of rust. The shadow was unreal, but the tiles it had moved remained on the table.

He looked up. The Mahjong God's shrine was directly overhead. Its clay figure had been fashioned after the likeness handed down from the Hall of the Mahjong God: tall and imposing, a long beard to the chest, a gold crown, gold-thread clouds across a purple robe. Seven stars on its breast formed the ladle of the Dipper. Its right hand held a tile whose face was white, without a single mark.

Every image of the God of Mahjong in every teahouse and mahjong house in the land looked like this. People said no painter was permitted to set a brush to the tile in the god's hand.

The incense had long since burned out, its ashes cold. Yet at the very moment he looked up, a thin thread of smoke rose from the cold ash, climbed straight into the air, then inclined towards his heart.

And straightened again.

The lamp flame flickered. In the wavering light, the clay figure's gilded eyes were lowered, as though watching him.

Holding his breath, Qinglan returned the Red Dragon to the tile box and closed the lid. From inside his robe he took his last copper coin and laid it on top. His father had taught him this too: a copper coin on a tile box kept it quiet at night. Copper consumed scattered breath; shadows could not stand in its presence. One coin could drive shadows from an old table. To govern a table of justice, copper had to form a ring, its boundary activated by the Arbitration Office. That was why four copper plates were set beneath the table in the middle of the hall.

No one seated at a table of justice could use spiritual power. Nor could the table itself stir. Most likely that was why the Rain Pavilion had moved this old table aside and replaced it with a new one fitted with copper.

He retreated to the woodshed, lay in the straw and listened to the rain with open eyes for the rest of the night. The old table made no further sound.

He fell asleep only as dawn approached.

“One hundred and thirty-seven tiles.”

The hoarse voice in the woodshed was very low. “The extra one goes into my hand in the fourth hand.”

Qinglan opened his eyes behind the stacked firewood.

The rain had stopped sometime, though the roof still dripped. A drop fell into the straw beside his ear. He had meant to leave at first light and catch an early boat from the quay, but had overslept.

A line of afternoon light entered through the door. Three people stood in it.

“The tea tray?” The second voice was high and thin, with a suggestion of oily sandalwood prayer beads.

“Brought up in the third hand. There’ll be a needle hidden beneath the rim. Once the girl from the dye works takes it, her hand will stiffen in about the time it takes to drink a cup of tea.”

“The substitute?”

“Old Zheng's sitting downstairs. One stroke of that goatee, and who in this city wouldn't trust him? He takes the seat and plays out a drawn hand. If the Tao side is behind after the first three hands, one draw makes the waterway ours.”

The thin voice chuckled. “And the wild tile?”

“Third from the tail of the wall. I'll build it. Turn it over, and it'll be a Red Dragon.”

The third man had not spoken. He stood in the darkest part of the doorway. Qinglan could see only the edge of a woven hat and a hand hanging at his side. A black leather glove, with an open hand embroidered on its back in gold thread, all five fingers spread as though reaching to seize something.

At last the man spoke. His voice was quiet, but each sentence gave off cold like water rising from the bottom of a well:

“The Master doesn't want a single waterway. He wants every sworn match of honour in Yunchuan played to one man's will from this day forward.”

At that moment, a chill struck Qinglan's heart.

The White Dragon against his chest had felt a gentle tug from the old table the night before. Now it was as though someone had cast it into a snow pit. Cold pierced the red cloth, his blue robe, and burrowed into his bones. He looked down. A fine white frost was forming on his lapel and creeping outward.

As it spread across the cloth, the frost made the faintest sound. Thin and brittle, like the first crack across a winter river.

“What's that?” asked the thin voice.

The woven hat turned.

Qinglan clenched his teeth. His father had taught him a method he had practised only secretly at night these past ten years: send a breath down from his heart, into that cold, to stop it drawing from outside. He had never used it before other people. He did not know what would happen.

He sent the breath down.

The White Dragon grew quiet. The frost stopped spreading.

But the breath had its price. Warmth drained from his right hand, first the fingertips, then his entire thumb, as though someone had plunged it into newly melted snow and held it there. He tried to move it. His thumb remained rigid, white tinged with blue, like a piece of someone else's finger.

Footsteps approached the woodpile.

One stick was pulled away, then another. Daylight entered through the gap. Half a long, thin face appeared in it, yellow teeth bared. It glanced behind the wood and withdrew. A pair of black boots approached and stopped less than two feet from Qinglan's face, furnace ash from the rear alley clinging to their toes.

The boots remained there for some time.

“You behind the woodpile,” said the man in the woven hat. “A boat leaves the city at the hour of the Monkey. I've paid your fare.”

A copper coin fell, bounced on the straw and stopped before Qinglan's nose.

“Step inside the Rain Pavilion,” the unhurried voice went on, “and you'll be the second to suffer a public tile-breaking sentence.”

The boots turned and left. The door opened and closed, and three sets of footsteps dispersed into the alley.

Qinglan lay motionless in the straw until every sound was gone.

The second.

He knew who the first had been.

He crawled out from behind the woodpile, gripping the copper coin, his right thumb still numb. The coin's edge had been filed, and a thin mark cut into it, like half a finger.

He returned to the rear window. Beyond the gap in the side door, voices clamoured. More than two hundred spectators packed tightly around the table of justice. A girl in a pale yellow dress sat in the scorekeeper's place, hanging a small wooden sign on a screen:

The Tao family dye works, west of the city, versus Hengchang Trading House: rights to the waterway

First hand: Tao wins one point

Second hand: drawn

The third hand was about to begin.

By the side door, a brown-clad Hengchang servant carried a tea tray into the crowd.

Qinglan took half a step towards the quay.

He had sworn never to touch the tiles again. Ten years ago, at Wangjiang Teahouse, a hall full of people had watched his father's tiles smashed onto the stone paving. He had been fourteen, kneeling to gather the fragments until his hands were covered in blood. That night his father had placed the White Dragon in his palm and said only, “Xiaolan, the tiles are clean. It's people who are dirty.”

Then his father had left, and never returned.

The tea tray was ten paces from the table.

Qinglan thrust the coin into his sleeve, flung open the side door and charged in.

“Don't take that tray!”

Everyone turned. The brown-clad servant turned too, his foot slipped, and a whole pot of scalding tea flew towards Qinglan. Qinglan raised his arm to shield himself. Tea drenched half his body; the collision threw him backwards, his head struck a pillar, and his sight went black.

By the time he had hauled himself upright against the pillar, the tray had reached the table.

The curly-haired girl in the South seat reached to take it.

“Ah—” She shook her hand. “A splinter.”

Too late.

Laughter swept the crowd. “Where did this mad scholar come from?” “Drunk in broad daylight, I'd say.” In the West seat, a plump steward fingering prayer beads narrowed his eyes at Qinglan's soaked old blue robe and laughed loudest. “Tea is served upstairs, good sir.”

The very same thin voice from the woodshed.

The tall, thin man at East did not laugh. His gaze settled on Qinglan's hanging right hand, on the thumb white tinged with blue. His mouth slowly widened, revealing yellow teeth.

Qinglan recognised those teeth. The man recognised him too: the rat behind the woodpile.

For the third hand, Qinglan could only stand among the crowd and watch.

The curly-haired girl was Luo Jinke, a dyer at the Tao family works. At first she managed. By the middle of the hand, the fingers of her right hand would no longer bend, and she had to support it with her left to draw a tile. She bit her lip without a sound.

At North sat Tao Lingtao, the proprietor, a woman of about thirty with twin buns, rounded shoulders in a pink cross-collared top, and a long dark-brown skirt beneath which she sat with the settled poise of a woman who ran her own household. Usually a smile waited on her face, her eyes curving with an easy charm. Now her lips were white.

On the eleventh turn, the man at East drew a tile, fitted it into his hand and pushed over the thirteen tiles before him.

“Self-draw.”

One, Two, Two, Three, Three, Four, Four, Five, Five, Five, Six, Seven, Eight and Nine of Bamboo. Fourteen tiles, all Bamboo, none claimed by pung.

“Full Flush. Concealed hand.” The scorekeeper moved an abacus bead, her voice clear. “Under this match's rules, only the higher of those two values counts; they do not combine. Two points. On a self-draw, both Tao seats pay two. After three hands, Tao is three points down.”

“Mahjong Adept Hu, from Nanchao,” someone murmured beside Qinglan. “Hu Shan. They say he's never lost a sworn match.”

Luo Jinke rose to shuffle for the fourth hand. Her fingers had scarcely touched the tiles when they spasmed violently. Bone tiles clattered across the floor. A pinprick of red in the web of her thumb had swollen purple-black.

The plump steward at West, Qian Youqing, slapped the table and stood. “A player unable to play forfeits the match!”

“Injury or illness permits one substitute player, provided the other side raises no objection.” Without even looking up, the scorekeeper pressed the agreement flat on the table. “You signed this, Steward Qian.”

“No objection! None at all!” Qian Youqing spread his arms. “Ladies and gentlemen, who will take this seat for the Tao side?”

More than twenty girls from the Tao family embroidery workshop crowded the stairs. The youngest tore free of her sister's hand and rushed to the table. “Me! I know how! I watch Mistress Tao every day!”

Tao Lingtao caught her in her arms, crouched and smoothed her disordered hair.

“You're only twelve,” she said. “No one under eighteen sits at the table. The Way of Mahjong matters more than a waterway.”

The little girl burst into tears and buried her face in her sister's arms.

The crowd quietened. A small, thin old man slowly stood. His back was slightly stooped; his greying hair was tied in a little topknot with a faded blue strip of cloth. A sparse goatee hung from his chin. His indigo cotton robe had been washed grey, its cuffs patched in matching cloth. An old bamboo-backed bone tile turned between the fingers of his right hand, polished glossy by who knew how many years. Zheng Shouzhuo, of the mahjong house south of the city, was known throughout Yunchuan for his honesty. Families liked to send their children to him for their first lessons in the tiles. His name appeared on no Arbitration Office grade list; he had never aspired to one. But the whole city trusted him.

“Proprietor Tao.” Zheng Shouzhuo clasped his hands. “Such poor skill as I possess is at your service. Let me take this seat. If I lose, the loss is mine.”

The hall breathed out in relief. Tao Lingtao's eyes reddened, and she was about to return his bow.

“Wait.”

The crowd parted. The drenched scholar approached the table, a swelling still visible at his temple.

“Master Zheng,” said Qinglan. “Might I ask how the Tao side is to win this hand?”

Zheng Shouzhuo stroked his beard and smiled kindly. “Young man, who can say how one wins at a mahjong table? Play steadily and win a hand. That's all.”

“Win a hand, and Tao still loses.”

The hall fell quiet again.

Qinglan turned towards the scorekeeper. “Tao is three points down. A win on a discard takes payment only from the player who deals in. Unless it's a Full Flush or Seven Pairs, that's at most one point. Tao would still be two down. A self-draw after calling a pung takes one point from each Hengchang seat. Tao would still be one down.” He paused. “Only a concealed self-draw, taking two from each seat, brings Tao one point ahead. A Full Flush or Seven Pairs by self-draw does the same.”

The scorekeeper looked up at him for a moment. “He's right.”

“This hand,” Qinglan said, looking at Zheng Shouzhuo, “allows no pung, no win on a discard. There's nothing steady about it.”

Zheng Shouzhuo's smile did not change. “Naturally I know it requires a self-draw. It was only a casual remark. Why pick at my words, young fellow?”

Qinglan did not argue further. He went to Tao Lingtao and lowered his voice so that only she could hear:

“In the woodshed just now, someone said there was a needle hidden under the tea tray for the third hand. Someone also said, ‘Old Zheng takes the seat and plays out a drawn hand.’” He looked at her. “I have no proof. All I ask is that you choose someone who dares to lose, not someone who wants to.”

Tao Lingtao looked down at Luo Jinke's swollen wrist.

She too had seen the pot of tea spill over the scholar.

She straightened and bowed deeply to Zheng Shouzhuo. “The Tao family will remember your kindness, Master Zheng. For this hand, I ask this young gentleman to play.”

Zheng Shouzhuo's sleeve swept across his body. Shaking his head with a smile, he withdrew into the crowd, behind the brown-clad Hengchang servants.

“The scholar?” Qian Youqing looked Qinglan up and down, then laughed aloud. “No objection. Sit!”

“Wait.” Tao Lingtao did not let him sit. Turning towards the stairs, she called, “Go to the back and bring that camphorwood chest.”

Two of the older workshop girls ran off. Soon they returned, carrying the chest between them to the table.

The lid opened, releasing the scent of camphorwood. A ceremonial robe lay folded inside, green as pine needles and white as a silkworm's cocoon, with gold-thread bamboo leaves all along the collar. A red cord hung with tassels, and a blue-green jade pendant adorned the waist.

It was a fine garment commissioned for the son of a wealthy gentleman named Wan on the east side of the city. The workshop had spent a month making it and meant to send it over after today's match.

“When you take a family's seat, you wear that family's clothes.” Tao Lingtao shook out the robe, draped it over his soaking old blue one and smoothed the collar herself. “Sit down covered in tea stains, and they'll already think less of us.”

The robe was dry, easing the wet chill over half his body. Qinglan tied the red cord and, through the two layers of cloth, settled the red wrapping against his chest.

“Young Master Wan's robe.” Qian Youqing clicked his tongue. “Lent to some scholar. How will you account for that afterwards, Proprietor Tao?”

“The Tao family lent it,” said Tao Lingtao. “If it's soiled, we pay. If the match is lost, we bear it.”

On the stairs, the workshop girls gripped the rail. They had spent seven nights embroidering the bamboo leaves on that robe.

Hu Shan rested his hand on the table edge and stared at Qinglan. “The rat in the woodshed dares to sit at the table?”

“Even a rat,” said Qinglan, taking the South seat, “can count tiles.”

The scorekeeper's name was Yuening. Her pale yellow dress had green edging along its crossed collar, and a small flower adorned each of her twin buns. Standing beside the table, her slender white neck rose from her collar like a newly peeled bamboo shoot. She did not have anyone collect the scattered tiles for play.

“Once tiles fall to the floor, the set is withdrawn,” she told both sides. “They rolled everywhere. Anyone could see their faces; no one can say whether a foot or hand slipped one away or put one back. Clause seven of the agreement: if tiles leave the table, a fresh sealed set replaces them, to be counted before both sides.”

Qian Youqing opened his mouth, but said nothing. Both sides had signed that clause to prevent the other tampering with the tiles. He could hardly contest it.

A waiter swept the scattered bone tiles into a bamboo winnowing tray and took them away to be sealed. Yuening fetched a spare tile box from the counter. Its opening bore a sealing strip marked by the Rain Pavilion and both the Tao and Hengchang parties, sealed before play began. She tore it open before everyone, poured a fresh set onto the table and counted each tile.

“One hundred and thirty-six.” Then, as required, she repeated for the newcomer the rules announced at the opening. “Every tile bears the Rain Pavilion's brand on its back. A four-corner copper boundary lies beneath this table. If anyone uses spiritual breath, it will light of itself. That is a betrayal of the Way.”

Qinglan lowered his eyes. Four polished copper plates were indeed set beside the table legs, dark as four closed eyes.

The White Dragon still lay against his heart. Its chill had subsided, but he knew that if he sent another breath into it, all four eyes would open at once.

He glanced once more towards the east wall. Behind the counter, the old square wooden table held its tea things in perfect order, as though nothing had happened. Before the shrine, his copper coin still lay untouched on the tile box. Copper consumed stray breath, from people and old tables alike. Within the copper ring around this table of justice, neither could stir.

The incense lit at the start of play had burned to its base after three hands. An old custom governed sworn matches: when a substitute joined, the scorekeeper lit a fresh stick and announced whose place the newcomer would take. Yuening went to the shrine and rose on tiptoe to reach the incense holder. Her sleeve slipped partway down her forearm, revealing a small wrist bone. Her feet strained beneath her skirt as though she might lose her balance at any moment. She lit a fresh stick and announced softly, “Fourth hand. Tao side, South seat. Substitute player.” Everyone was watching the scholar. No one paid attention to the incense.

The smoke rose straight, then halfway up inclined towards South.

“Draught from the side door,” a waiter muttered, and went to close it.

The smoke straightened. Qinglan lowered his eyes and did not look that way again.

Yuening's gaze settled on his right hand. “What happened to your hand?”

“Frostbite.”

“In the fifth month?”

Qinglan did not answer.

Yuening did not ask again. Beside her stood a cup of hot tea for her own use. Before counting the tiles, she nudged it half an inch towards him, then bent her head to her work, as though the cup had moved by itself. In the steam Qinglan caught another scent, like paper dried in sunlight, or gardenias at the foot of an academy wall. It drifted from her cuff. He returned his gaze to the wall of tiles.

Hu Shan shuffled and built. His fingers were long and thin; they flowed like water as he laid the eastern wall, then paused briefly at its tail.

Yuening turned over the third tile from the tail.

A Red Dragon.

“Red Dragons act as wild tiles. Begin.”

A low stir passed through the crowd. By the stairs, someone whispered, “Why is it always the end he builds…”

Qinglan's starting hand held thirteen tiles: One, Two and Three of Characters; Five and Six of Bamboo; Three and Four of Dots; Seven, Eight and Nine of Dots; East Wind, North Wind and Red Dragon.

Hu Shan discarded an East Wind first.

Qinglan's turn to draw. Normally he swept his thumb across a tile as he took it; one touch of the carving told him what it was. Now his thumb felt nothing, as though thick ice lay between it and the tile.

He had to turn it up, look, then turn it back.

Someone behind him snorted. “Can't even feel a tile.”

On the first turn he drew a Nine of Bamboo and discarded North Wind. Qian Youqing followed with an East Wind.

Second turn: South Wind drawn, South Wind discarded.

On the third turn came Seven of Bamboo, completing the Five-Six-Seven sequence. He sent Nine of Bamboo into the discard pool.

His hand was waiting.

One-Two-Three of Characters, Five-Six-Seven of Bamboo, Seven-Eight-Nine of Dots: three complete sets. That left Three and Four of Dots, an East Wind and a Red Dragon. A Two or Five of Dots would complete the Dots sequence, with East Wind and the wild Red Dragon forming the pair of eyes. Another East Wind would make the pair, freeing the Red Dragon to complete the Three-Four sequence. Another Red Dragon would also do.

Two East Winds were already visible in the discard pool, one from Hu Shan and one from Qian Youqing. With one in his own hand, at most one remained in the wall.

Seven or eight spectators pressed behind him, craning to see his tiles. He turned up every tile he drew, and his standing tiles were low; those behind could see everything. Someone understood and whispered to a neighbour. The whisper passed from person to person into the crowd.

On the fourth turn, Hu Shan discarded a Two of Dots.

Qinglan did not move. Someone behind him made a startled sound and was elbowed by a neighbour.

On the fifth turn, Qinglan drew and discarded. When play reached West, Qian Youqing rolled his prayer beads, leisurely selected a tile and slapped it into the discard pool.

Five of Dots.

The workshop girls on the stairs drew breath together. Someone could not help shouting, “Win!”

Qinglan's hand rested on the table edge, unmoving.

“Scholar,” Qian Youqing said, smiling at him, “don't you want this one?”

He had done it on purpose. He could see every face behind Qinglan: someone had exclaimed when the Two of Dots fell; now others were leaning in. He need not see the tiles to guess what the scholar was waiting for. He had already worked it out: a win on his discard would earn Tao one point from him alone, leaving them defeated. This tile was a test of whether the scholar could truly count.

“Pass,” said Qinglan.

The crowd erupted. “Mad! Won't even take a win handed to him!” “Proprietor Tao, who have you hired?”

Tao Lingtao sat at North, the hand holding her tiles trembling slightly, but said nothing. Yuening made a note in the ledger, glanced at Qinglan, then lowered her head again.

Seventh turn. Hu Shan drew.

He lifted the tile, drew his right wrist inward as though adjusting a cuff, then slid it back close along his standing tiles. Swift as a swallow skimming water. Of the two hundred pairs of eyes in the hall, not one saw clearly.

Qinglan had not seen the tile clearly either.

But he knew the movement. The wrist drawn inward, then returned along the standing tiles: at the third watch last night, the thick-knuckled hand at East on the old table had moved exactly so. The spot of red in the sleeve entered the standing tiles; one already standing went into the sleeve. A living man's sleeve was not transparent. Qinglan could not see what lay inside, but he had seen that ghost hand.

In that instant, the White Dragon against his heart turned cold enough to sear like a branding iron.

Not coolness. Pain. Cold burned through that small patch of flesh on his chest as though someone were boring an ice pick into his heart. Frost began to spread again, creeping from within his robe and over the gold-thread bamboo leaves at his collar.

Instinctively his breath sank, ready to send another breath into the tile and suppress the cold.

The four copper plates beside the table legs suddenly gave off a trace of warmth, like four closed eyes whose lids had stirred.

He stopped himself short.

The copper cooled again.

He could not use it. Not a single breath. The least movement of spiritual breath at this table would make the boundary blaze before the whole hall. Everyone would see Qing Yuanshan's son betray the Way at the table of justice.

He could only endure.

Cold bored into his bones in waves. He clenched his teeth, his knuckles white on the table edge. Frost coated the gold-thread leaves and gave off a thread of white vapour in the stifling air.

Yuening's brush stopped in midair. She stared at the vapour, then at his face.

But amid the pain, Qinglan understood something.

The White Dragon grew this cold only when something came near. In the woodshed, the man in the woven hat had stood less than two feet away. Now nothing at the table had changed except Hu Shan's hand, just returned from his sleeve.

The tile had entered play.

He began to count.

Four Red Dragons in a set. One lay face up at the wall's tail as the wild-tile indicator, visible to all. One was in his own hand. The other two—whose hands held them, or where in the wall they lay—he did not know.

Hu Shan knew. Hu Shan had built the wall. Hu Shan knew which tile was wild. And now he had swapped another in from his sleeve.

Could Qinglan halt the match? On what grounds? A cold patch on his chest? A ghostly hand last night that no one else could see? He could not say it. If he did, his would be the second set of tiles smashed.

Only one way remained: finish the hand and let the tile reveal itself.

Hu Shan discarded a Six of Characters and tapped the table edge.

Eighth turn.

Hu Shan's turn. He drew a tile, held it a while, frowned, then relaxed and discarded a Nine of Characters.

Not complete yet. But he was waiting; Qinglan could tell. After discarding, Hu Shan tapped the table edge lightly three times, as though counting the tiles that could still complete his hand. The Red Dragon he had slipped in could stand for anything. He had far more winning draws than Qinglan.

Qinglan drew a One of Bamboo and discarded it.

Qian Youqing discarded. Tao Lingtao discarded.

Ninth turn. Hu Shan drew first.

The whole hall held its breath. Hu Shan brought the tile before his eyes, looked, and his mouth stirred without quite making a smile. He cast it into the discard pool.

Eight of Characters.

South's turn.

Qinglan reached to the wall and drew a tile.

His thumb pressed its face. He could feel nothing.

An old man behind him sighed softly. “Has to turn up every tile to see it. Hardly looks like a mahjong player at all…”

Qinglan turned the tile over.

Five of Dots.

He laid down his thirteen tiles and placed the Five beside the Three and Four of Dots.

“Self-draw.”

One-Two-Three of Characters, Five-Six-Seven of Bamboo, Three-Four-Five of Dots, Seven-Eight-Nine of Dots; one East Wind and the Red Dragon standing for another, making the pair of eyes. Fourteen tiles, none claimed by pung.

The stairs fell silent for a moment. Then the youngest workshop girl was the first to scream.

Yuening's abacus clacked. “Concealed self-draw, two points. Each Hengchang seat pays two; Tao gains four. Three down after the first three hands. Across all four—Tao wins by one point.”

The workshop girls burst into tears. Tao Lingtao covered her mouth.

Qinglan did not look at them. He raised both hands and set them on the table edge:

“No one touch any of the four hands.”

Hu Shan's right hand was already moving.

He did not gather his tiles. He drew a single tile from the right end of his row, pinched its edge and slid it towards his chest along the tabletop. His cuff fell, covering half of it.

“Master Witness,” said Qinglan. “His right hand.”

An old folding fan came across and pressed Hu Shan's wrist down.

The storyteller Xiao Cheng'an, serving as witness today, had sat at the corner throughout. A small black satin cap covered his head; his white beard was trimmed short, and a brown, gold-patterned outer jacket lay over his long black robe. The fan's ribs held the hand utterly still. “Put it down.”

Qinglan's heart jolted. The hand with the fan at the old table last night had come across just so, held its quarry just as firmly. He could not be sure it was the same fan; the old table might have remembered some hand played decades ago.

Hu Shan stiffened for a moment. His five fingers loosened.

Click.

A Red Dragon fell onto the table.

Yuening first counted Hu Shan's remaining standing tiles.

“Twelve.” She looked up. “With this one, thirteen. After discarding on the ninth turn, he should hold thirteen. The number is correct.”

Hu Shan's throat worked, then relaxed, as though he had finally let out a breath.

“I'll count something else,” said Qinglan. “The Red Dragons.”

He pointed. “One at the wall's tail, the wild-tile indicator. One in my hand, used just now to win. One in Hu Shan's hand, plus the one he has just put down. Two.” He turned to North. “Proprietor Tao, do you have one?”

Tao Lingtao hesitated, then laid out her thirteen tiles.

Among them lay a Red Dragon.

Five.

Of the more than two hundred people in the hall, not one made a sound.

Hu Shan suddenly laughed, loud and hard. “The extra one is his!” He stabbed a finger at Qinglan. “Do you know who this scholar is? He's carrying a tile!”

Before the words were out, he lunged across the table and tore open Qinglan's robe. The red cord of the green-and-white garment snapped, and its jade pendant struck the table edge with a clink.

The red wrapping fell onto the table and opened.

A White Dragon rolled out, its bone-white face still coated in unmelted frost.

It was as though a stone had been thrown into the hall.

“A White Dragon, and he carries it like treasure?” someone at the back jeered. “It's the god's tile that's blank. Think you're the God of Mahjong?”

Others began to laugh, but Qian Youqing's voice cut them off before the laughter could rise.

“He brought a tile to the table!” His voice was piercing. “You all saw it! Tao's substitute had a tile inside his robe! Tao forfeits!”

Xiao Cheng'an reached out, lifted the White Dragon and turned it over.

A tiny hand was engraved at the lower right of its back, all five fingers spread. Beneath it ran an almost invisible blue-green line, like a single bamboo stroke.

The old storyteller's face changed.

“The Tuidao School…” he murmured. “The Qing family.”

Someone in the crowd understood.

“Qing? Qing Yuanshan, whose tiles were broken in public at Wangjiang Teahouse ten years ago?”

“His son!”

“A cheat's spawn!”

Voices surged like a tide. Someone threw fruit peel onto the table. Others pushed forward, jostling the workshop girls off balance. Tao Lingtao rose with a hand on the table edge, deathly pale, looking from the White Dragon to Qinglan.

Qinglan sat with his robe open. The frostbitten flesh over his heart was exposed, red and white in patches, in the exact shape of a tile.

He did not close his robe.

“Scorekeeper,” he said. “Please turn the tiles over.”

Yuening looked at him.

“All five Red Dragons,” said Qinglan. “Please turn them over.”

Yuening reached out and turned the five Red Dragons on the table over, one by one.

The first bore the Rain Pavilion's brand. The second, a brand. The third, a brand. The fourth, a brand.

The fifth was the one Hu Shan had just put down. Its back was smooth, without a brand. Only a little hand had been engraved at the lower right, all five fingers spread.

Exactly like the hand embroidered on the black glove.

“This set,” said Yuening, quietly but clearly enough to still the hall, “was poured from a sealed box and counted by me before both parties at the start of the fourth hand. One hundred and thirty-six tiles, every one branded. This tile has no brand. It does not belong to the set.”

Without pausing, she pushed the Red Dragon aside and pointed to Hu Shan's standing tiles:

“Remove this one, and Hu Shan has only twelve. One short. One tile from this set is missing from his hand.”

Understanding swept the hall. The count had been correct because someone had exchanged one tile for another.

“His right sleeve,” said Qinglan.

Xiao Cheng'an did not reach for him. A witness observed and judged; he did not search a person. Such were the Arbitration Office's rules. He withdrew his fan and laid it on his knee. “Mahjong Adept Hu, please turn out your right sleeve yourself.”

“On what grounds?” Hu Shan jerked backwards, his right arm pressed tight to his ribs. “Some stripling scholar says a word, and I have to turn out my sleeve in front of everyone? I refuse!”

He jerked too hard. His chair tipped back, and he hastily reached for the table edge with his right hand. His cuff swung open.

Click. A tile slipped from his sleeve and fell onto the table, the Rain Pavilion's brand on its back.

West Wind.

That inward-and-outward movement on the seventh turn had sent the Red Dragon from his sleeve into the standing tiles, and this West Wind from the tiles into his sleeve.

“What about the scholar's White Dragon?” Qian Youqing cried.

“I was about to count those.” Yuening picked out the White Dragons from the four walls, the four hands and the discard pool, and lined them on the table.

Four. Every one branded.

“All four White Dragons from this set are accounted for,” she said. “The one Young Master Qing carried has no brand. It is not among these one hundred and thirty-six.”

In a corner, a panda carrying a bamboo basket raised his brush. He had been crouched beside the counter recording the game, unnoticed. Now he held his paper scroll high. “I am Zhumo, the chronicler of today's match. Throughout the fourth hand, the gentleman at South never once reached inside his robe. The four-corner copper boundary”—he paused—“never lit once during the entire hand.”

The boundary had not lit. With his chest frozen like that, he had not used a single breath.

Xiao Cheng'an laid the White Dragon back on its red cloth and pushed it towards Qinglan. He stood and struck the table with his folded fan:

“Hu Shan at East swapped a tile from his sleeve, bringing a hundred and thirty-seventh tile into play. A betrayal of the Way! In the fourth hand, Tao wins by concealed self-draw. Across all four hands, Tao wins by one point. The western waterway—”

He did not finish.

Hu Shan thrust both hands beneath the table and overturned it.

Bone tiles struck the stone floor like hail. Tea bowls, abacus and fruit dishes scattered everywhere. Two waiters leapt to stop him; Hu Shan shouldered them aside and charged for the front door. Qian Youqing cowered beneath a table, clutching his head and shrieking, “Nothing to do with me! Nothing to do with me!” The hall dissolved into chaos. The workshop girls screamed and fled up the stairs.

“Protect the tiles!” Yuening threw herself to the floor, spreading both arms over the scattered pieces. Her pale yellow skirt pooled across the stone. She crouched so low that the nape of her neck showed beneath her twin buns, its fine soft hairs pale gold in the lamplight.

Qinglan snatched up the White Dragon, wrapped it in the red cloth and pressed it back against his heart.

Just then, the White Dragon chilled again.

This time the cold reached outward, like a line tied to his heart at one end, the other being pulled towards the rear door.

He turned.

Through a gap in the crowd, the hem of an indigo robe washed grey slipped through the back door. A blue strip binding a topknot bobbed.

Zheng Shouzhuo.

Qinglan looked down beside Yuening. All four branded Red Dragons were there. The extra tile was gone.

He vaulted the overturned table and gave chase.

Rain had begun again in the rear alley.

Zheng Shouzhuo could not run fast, but no longer needed to. A man stood at the far end: woven hat, black robe, his left hand extended in a black leather glove, holding an oilpaper packet. His right hand hung motionless at his side. The old man took the packet, thrust it into his sleeve and turned into another alley without looking back.

The man in the woven hat did not leave. He turned to face the pursuing scholar, as though waiting for him.

“The boat at the hour of the Monkey,” he said, “has already sailed.”

“That tile,” Qinglan panted. “Give it back.”

With his left hand, the man drew a Red Dragon from his sleeve, held it between two fingers and waved it in the rain, then drew it back into the sleeve; when the hand came out again, a different tile was between his fingers, and he flicked his wrist.

The tile cut through the rain and struck squarely into Qinglan's palm with a crack that numbed his whole arm.

“Take this one back to account for yourself,” said the man in the woven hat. “It's worth more than the other.”

Qinglan looked down.

A Red Dragon. Its bone was yellowed, its red paint dark with age, as though it had lain buried somewhere for years. At the foot of the symbol's final vertical stroke, a chip the size of a rice grain exposed white.

“Your father counted just the same way,” came the voice through the rain. “And what good did counting correctly do him?”

Qinglan looked up. The alley mouth was empty. The man stepped onto the tiles atop the wall and vanished into the rain, leaving only a few broken pieces falling into the puddles.

Qinglan gripped the old Red Dragon. His other hand still pressed the White Dragon against his heart.

The two tiles touched through the thin layer of red cloth.

Cold flooded his entire arm at once.

Raindrops landing on the back of his hand froze, one by one, into fine frost.

The sound of rain vanished. The alley vanished.

He saw a square wooden table. A man in a dark-blue long robe sat beside it, reaching towards a tile box. Qinglan knew that hand; as a child he had touched the calluses on its knuckles countless times.

A blue-grey cuff pressed in from one side and blocked it. The sleeve carried a Red Dragon, which struck the edge of the box with a click.

At the foot of its final vertical stroke was a white chip the size of a rice grain.

Qinglan tried to see the owner of the cuff, but it was as though his eyes were set in his father's chest, able to look out only from there. He saw the blue-grey cuff pause.

Then its owner slowly turned his head.

He looked neither at the tile box nor at his father's face. He lowered his eyes to his father's robe, to the place within it against his heart.

Across ten years, he was looking straight at Qinglan.

The man smiled.

Rain brought Qinglan back to himself.

He knelt in the rear alley's standing water. Something warm flowed from his nose and dripped onto his hand. Blood. His right arm was numb from fingertips to elbow; he could barely hold the old Red Dragon.

With shaking hands, he unfolded the red cloth.

The faint blue-green line on the White Dragon's back had almost vanished.

Hurried footsteps sounded at the alley mouth. Yuening ran towards him beneath an oilpaper umbrella. The moment she reached him, she tilted it wholly his way, sheltering his head and his numb right arm. Water poured from its rim down her own back; she seemed not to notice. Then she saw what he held and stopped dead.

“That Red Dragon…” Her voice tightened. “The year I entered the profession, I saw a rubbing in the evidence illustrations the Arbitration Office sent to scorekeepers everywhere for identification. The tile turned up in Qing Yuanshan's wall at Wangjiang Teahouse ten years ago. The chip beneath the final vertical stroke. The size of a rice grain.”

She crouched, staring at the white fleck. Suddenly they were very close beneath the umbrella. Qinglan caught the scent of soap pod at her cuff, mingled with ink from the ledger. Her breath clouded in the cold rain, and he turned his head a little aside.

“It should be sealed in the Arbitration Office in Tiandu. Sealed ten years ago, and never opened since.”

Qinglan did not answer. He had noticed a rolled slip of paper tied to the old Red Dragon's back with fine thread. With his still-working left hand, he pulled it free and unfolded it in the rain.

Only one line. Rain had blurred the ink, but it could still be read:

One hundred and thirty-seven. The hundred and thirty-seventh tile was never in the wall. It was inside your father's robe.

Qinglan's finger stopped on that line.

The White Dragon inside his own robe bore no brand.

Yuening saw the words too. She slowly lifted her head, rain running down her fringe. Droplets hung on her lashes and broke against her cheekbones when she blinked. Her lips were pale with cold, pressed tightly together. An umbrella rib had caught her collar, pulling it awry so that it slipped down, baring part of a rain-wet shoulder, white as newly laid tile faces.

Qinglan glanced once, then returned his eyes to the writing. He raised his still-working left hand and pushed the umbrella handle an inch back towards her.

She pushed it back. The umbrella never left his head.

There was only so much room beneath it. Her arm touched his; through the wet, cold sleeves came a little living warmth. Through the rain he could hear her breathing, short and quick from running the length of the alley. He did not know her, did not even know her surname. Yet that warmth travelled up his arm all the way to his ears.

“Young Master Qing,” she asked softly, “in that match ten years ago, what was your father carrying inside his robe?”

(End of Chapter One)
