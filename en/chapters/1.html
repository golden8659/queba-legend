Chapter 1The Unfinished Hand at the Teahouse

The ninth turn.

More than two hundred people crowded the Rain Pavilion, yet it was quiet enough to hear water dripping from the eaves.

In the South seat at the square wooden table sat a scholar in a faded blue-green robe. Thirteen tiles stood in a neat row before him. At the end of the wall, the third tile from last lay face up: a Red Dragon. The wild tile for this hand.

The tall, gaunt man in the North seat rested ten long, slender fingers on the edge of the table. His fingertips tapped, softly, one after another.

One more turn, and they would begin drawing from the stretch of wall in front of him.

Hidden in that wall was the Full Flush he had spent the entire hand cultivating.

“One turn left,” someone whispered in the crowd. “If the scholar doesn't draw it now…”

No one finished the thought.

Twenty-odd young embroiderers huddled on the stairs. The youngest, ten or so, buried her face against an older girl's chest, afraid to look.

The scholar reached out and drew a tile from the wall.

He did not look at it.

His thumb passed lightly over its face.

Half a day earlier, he had been no more than a penniless scholar passing through Yunchuan, with a few dozen copper coins left in his purse.

He had sworn never to touch a tile again.

◆

In Yunchuan, the rain always stopped before dawn.

Qinglan stood on a stone arch bridge. Beneath it, the river ran in three colours: indigo, crimson, pale yellow, long strands of each, as though someone had cast three lengths of silk into the water. They were the colours of the Tao family dye works west of the city. They were the colours of Yunchuan.

He took a piece of red cloth from inside his robe and unfolded it, layer by layer. Inside lay a White Dragon.

Its bone-white face was clean. Nothing marked it.

Ten years earlier, his father had received a public tile-breaking sentence. One hundred and thirty-six bone tiles had been smashed at his feet, one by one. Only this one had rolled behind a pillar and survived.

Qinglan wrapped the White Dragon, tucked it back inside his robe, tightened the straps of his old book chest and walked down from the bridge.

◆

The Rain Pavilion stood at the junction of Crossroads Street in the city centre, a three-storey timber building with upswept eaves. Beside the entrance hung a black-lacquered wooden sign with gold lettering: Mahjong Arbitration.

Qinglan took a seat in a corner of the storytelling room on the second floor, counted out two copper coins and ordered a pot of the cheapest coarse tea. His purse grew a little flatter.

The storyteller, Xiao Cheng'an, had a white beard, a folding fan and a storyteller's block. His morning performance always began with How the Mahjong God Set the World in Order.

“And so Divine Lord Jinque, holding one hundred and thirty-six jade tiles, cast them into the sky—”

He struck the block.

“When the jade tiles fell to earth, they became the three suits—Characters, Bamboo and Dots—the four Winds of East, South, West and North, and the three Dragons: Red, Green and White. The Divine Lord said to the lords of the realm, ‘When armies clash, it is the common people who suffer. Henceforth, if you have a dispute, sit down and settle it with a game. Let the loser accept defeat. Let the winner keep faith.’”

The children below the stage called out with him: “Let the loser accept defeat! Let the winner keep faith!”

Without pausing for breath, Xiao Cheng'an told of the Divine Lord's ascent and the Heavenly Tiles being enshrined in the hall; of the Tianyuan Mahjong Assembly, held once every twelve years, whose victor could ascend to the Hall of the Mahjong God and ask Heaven one question. His audience listened, entranced. At last he snapped his fan shut and lowered his voice.

“Remember this, everyone. The Way of Mahjong has a thousand rules, but the first is this: tamper with the tiles, and you betray the Way! Your tiles will be broken in public, and you will never again take part in a sworn match of honour!”

In the corner, Qinglan's hand trembled around his tea bowl. Tea splashed onto his robe.

Through his robe, he pressed a hand against the White Dragon tucked inside.

Ten years ago, the whole world had said his father cheated in that hand. His father alone had denied it, to his dying day. But the verdict imposing the tile-breaking sentence had been signed and sealed. No mahjong table in the world would allow that hand to be laid out again.

Only one place could. The Hall of the Mahjong God. There, before the whole world, he could ask Heaven one question.

The hall's doors would next open in the coming year.

But he had sworn never to touch a tile again as long as he lived.

He lowered his head and rubbed at the tea stains on his blue-green robe with his sleeve. He kept rubbing for a long time.

“That is all for this morning.” Xiao Cheng'an saluted the audience with clasped hands. “Come early tomorrow, everyone.”

◆

The storytelling was over, but the stairs were blocked.

A group of workmen in short brown tunics crowded upstairs around a portly steward with a string of oily, polished prayer beads about his neck. The moment he entered, he slapped a red notice onto the storyteller's stage.

Sworn Match of Honour: Hengchang Trading House versus Tao Lingtao of the Tao family dye works west of the city

Dispute: ownership of the waterway serving the western dye works

Time: early this afternoon. Format: four hands under the Tuidao School rules; reveal a tile to determine the wild tile.

“Master Xiao,” the steward said, all smiles, “we shall trouble you to witness this afternoon's match.”

Xiao Cheng'an looked down at the red notice without answering at once. One by one, the people about to leave stopped where they stood.

A woman with her hair in two buns stepped out of the crowd. She was about thirty, with gentle features and a very pale face.

Tao Lingtao, proprietor of the Tao family dye works west of the city. The dye works had come down through her family; the embroidery workshop in its rear courtyard was her own creation. Over the years she had taken in orphan girls whom nobody on the streets wanted, one by one, and taught them to handle a needle. They embroidered silk dyed at the works, then sold what they made.

“Steward of Hengchang,” she said, her voice trembling, “that waterway has kept the Tao family dye works alive for three generations. If you want to build a wharf, there is land to the east, to the south. Why must it be there?”

“Proprietor Tao.” The steward rolled his prayer beads at leisure. “A sworn match of honour follows the Way of Mahjong. We shall settle the matter at the table. Wouldn't you agree, Master Xiao?”

He paused and lowered his voice, just enough for everyone in the room to hear.

“I understand you have twenty-odd embroiderers in that workshop out back. Quite a few orphan girls you picked up off the streets? The dye works keep that workshop running, don't they? Lose the waterway, and the dye vats stop. Then the workshop closes too—and those girls go straight back onto the streets.”

Tao Lingtao's lips trembled. No words came.

Behind her, a curly-haired young woman strode forward, her cuffs still stained with indigo.

“The apothecary in the north of the city lost to you. So did the academy's rear hill. Every single time, you win! Who does Hengchang have playing for them—a god, or a ghost?”

Silence fell.

The steward's smile stiffened for an instant. Then he stepped aside to reveal the man behind him.

Tall and gaunt, with a pallid face, sunken eyes and ten exceptionally long, slender fingers. He took a bone tile from his sleeve, flipped it, spun it, flicked it. The tile seemed alive, tumbling and dancing over his five fingers before landing upright on the storyteller's stage with a click.

A Red Dragon.

“Hu San,” said the steward. “He will be playing this afternoon.”

Hu San looked at Tao Lingtao and grinned.

Xiao Cheng'an studied Hu San for a long time, then glanced at Tao Lingtao's pale face. At last he closed his folding fan.

“If a sworn match of honour is brought to the Rain Pavilion, this old man has no cause to refuse. Early this afternoon. Downstairs. I will bear witness.”

In the corner, Qinglan stared at the upright Red Dragon. His brow slowly tightened.

◆

At noon, Qinglan spent three copper coins on a bowl of plain noodles at a stall in the alley behind the Rain Pavilion.

The stall stood beside the woodshed. He had just picked up his chopsticks when voices seeped through a crack in the door.

“…Everything ready?” The rasp belonged to Hu San.

“Ready.” The other voice was very low. “It will be on the rim of the tea tray for the third hand. Once that dye girl's fingers stiffen, she won't be able to build a straight wall.”

“And the stacks at the end of the wall?”

“As usual. You shuffle. You build. The last seven stacks are yours.”

A silence.

“The Master says,” the low voice resumed, “win this match, and Yunchuan's waterway belongs to us. After that, every sworn match of honour in Yunchuan will be ours.”

The woodshed door creaked open. A man in a woven hat pulled its brim low and brushed past the noodle stall.

Qinglan saw only his hand.

A black leather glove. On its back, an open hand embroidered in gold thread, its five fingers reaching to seize something.

The man in the woven hat vanished at the end of the alley.

Qinglan sat motionless, chopsticks in hand. The noodles in his bowl had clumped together.

He had sworn never to touch a tile again. He asked himself: should he tell the Mahjong Arbitration Office? He had no proof. Accuse someone of cheating and be wrong, and he would face a tile-breaking sentence and a three-year ban himself. Besides, he was the son of a man whose name had gone uncleared for ten years. Who would believe him?

He ate his noodles slowly. Not a single strand remained.

◆

Early afternoon. The ground floor of the Rain Pavilion.

The square wooden table stood in the middle of the room. People packed every side; even the windowsills and stairs were full.

In the East seat, Tao Lingtao. In the South seat, the dyer Luo Jinke. In the West seat, Hengchang's steward. In the North seat, Hu San.

Beside the table stood a girl in a pale-yellow jacket, a small ebony abacus in her left hand, a brush in her right. Yuening, the scorekeeper for this match. The youngest scorekeeper in Yunchuan, with a memory said to be almost frightening: by the end of a hand, she could remember who had discarded every tile, and on which turn.

“Tuidao School rules. Four hands to decide the match,” Yuening announced. “At the start of each hand, the third tile from the end of the wall is revealed. Whatever it shows will act as a wild tile for that hand. Pung and kong are allowed. Chow is not. Begin.”

Qinglan stood at the very edge of the crowd.

Just one look, he told himself.

◆

In the first hand, Hu San dealt in to Tao Lingtao. The Tao side gained four points. The whole room breathed easier.

Qinglan's frown deepened. Hu San need not have discarded that tile. He had done it deliberately, like a cat letting a mouse run a little farther.

In the second hand, Hu San called pung three times and seemed about to win All Pungs. In the last few turns, however, he suddenly refused to win, broke his hand apart and let the hand end in a draw.

Everyone said his luck was bad. Only a few experienced players exchanged a look. It was not that he could not win. He did not want to.

Yuening recorded the hand. Her brush paused.

She had been a scorekeeper for five years and watched close to a thousand players. She had never seen anyone like Hu San. He shuffled as though playing music, built the wall as though embroidering silk. One sweep of those ten fingers through the tiles, and the whole set obeyed him.

Obeyed him too well.

Quietly, she counted the stretch of wall in front of him: seventeen stacks, neither more nor less. But in the last few stacks, the gaps between tiles were a fraction narrower than elsewhere, as though someone had nudged each one with a fingernail.

Yuening looked up and met Hu San's eyes.

He smiled at her, showing yellow teeth.

A chill ran down her back. She lowered her head and made a tiny note in the margin of her ledger, in shorthand only she could read: “North wall. End tight.”

At the edge of the crowd, Qinglan saw her bend over her writing. He saw Hu San's smile too.

She has noticed, Qinglan thought. But she has no proof either.

Before the third hand, a waiter brought a tray to refill their tea. Luo Jinke reached for it, then snatched her hand back with a hiss.

“What is it?” Tao Lingtao asked.

“Nothing.” Luo Jinke shook her hand. “A splinter on the edge of the tray.”

Outside the crowd, Qinglan dug his nails into his palm.

It will be on the rim of the tea tray for the third hand.

The third hand was brutal.

Halfway through, Tao Lingtao was ready to win: a two-sided wait for the Three of Characters or Six of Characters, with a wild tile still in her hand. The knowledgeable onlookers saw it. Whispers spread, growing louder: the Tao side had a chance!

One turn, two, three… No Three of Characters. No Six of Characters.

Only seven stacks remained in the wall.

The very stretch in front of Hu San, built just a little more neatly than the rest.

The last seven stacks are yours.

The end of the wall. The final tile. Hu San reached out with his long fingers, drew it and, without even looking, laid his hand down.

“Self-draw.”

A Full Flush in Bamboo. A concealed hand. Last Tile Draw.

The room went dead silent.

Yuening's abacus clicked for a moment. Her voice came out strained. “North wins by self-draw. Sixteen points from each of the other three players. The payment between the two Hengchang players cancels within their side. Each of the two Tao players loses sixteen. Thirty-two points lost by the Tao side. Cumulative loss across three hands: twenty-eight points.”

Twenty-eight points. One hand left.

◆

Before the fourth hand, Luo Jinke reached out to shuffle the tiles.

The instant her fingers touched them, her whole hand jerked. Tiles clattered to the floor. She clutched her wrist. Where the “splinter” had pricked her, a hard, purple-red swelling had risen. Her fingers were stiffening one by one. She could not even make a fist.

The steward rose unhurriedly. “If a player cannot take the table, the rules require a forfeit.”

“The Way of Mahjong has another rule.” Yuening did not look up. “If a player is injured or ill, their side may call a substitute player, once only, provided the opposing side has no objection.”

“We have none.” The steward spread his arms and turned to the room. “Well, everyone? Who will play this last hand for the Tao family?”

Nobody moved.

The Tao side was twenty-eight points down. Not even a god could save them in one hand. Besides, anyone who took the seat would make an enemy of Hengchang.

Tao Lingtao searched the faces, one after another. One after another, they turned away.

She turned towards the assembled crowd and slowly sank to her knees.

“Please…”

Her forehead touched the flagstones. Her voice broke. “Those girls in the workshop have no families. The workshop is their home… Please. Play one hand for them…”

The steward burst out laughing, prayer beads swinging against his chest. “Proprietor Tao! Kneeling is no part of the Way of Mahjong!”

Everyone in the room lowered their head.

◆

At the edge of the crowd, Qinglan closed his eyes.

Ten years ago.

Someone had knelt on the floor that day too.

He had been fourteen, kneeling on the flagstones of Wangjiang Teahouse, picking up the bone tiles shattered at his father's feet, piece by piece, until his hands ran with blood. A roomful of people watched. Not one bent down to help.

That night, his father pressed the only unbroken tile, the White Dragon, into his hand. “Xiaolan. The tiles are clean. It is people who are dirty.”

He opened his eyes.

◆

“I will.”

The voice was quiet. Everyone heard it.

The crowd parted of its own accord. Qinglan set down his book chest, walked through to the square wooden table and bent to help Tao Lingtao to her feet.

“My name is Qinglan. I am passing through Yunchuan. If you are willing to trust me, Proprietor Tao, I will play the last hand for Miss Luo.”

The steward looked him up and down: a faded blue-green robe, patched cuffs, a scholar's lean face. He laughed aloud. “Very well, scholar. Sit.”

Hu San did not laugh. He watched Qinglan's hands: clean, uncalloused, nails trimmed very short.

“How many years have you played?”

“Never.” Qinglan took the South seat. “I have only watched.”

Laughter filled the room.

Only Yuening's brush hung motionless. Something about the way this scholar sat, where he placed his hands, the angle at which he studied the wall, made her heart give a sudden jump.

◆

The fourth hand. East Wind.

When the wall was built, Yuening reached out and turned over the third tile from the end.

A Red Dragon.

“The Red Dragon will act as a wild tile.”

That stretch of wall stood directly in front of Hu San.

Qinglan's thirteen-tile starting hand was: One of Characters, Two of Characters, Four of Characters; Five of Bamboo, Seven of Bamboo; Three of Dots, Three of Dots, Six of Dots; East Wind, South Wind, White Dragon; Nine of Bamboo; Red Dragon.

Scattered. Only a pair of Three of Dots tiles, and a Red Dragon acting as a wild tile.

An old hand in the crowd shook his head. “Not even a god could make twenty-eight points out of that.”

Qinglan ignored him. His gaze lingered at the end of the wall before Hu San. The revealed Red Dragon lay there perfectly straight, its red marking painfully bright.

Yuening's pupils narrowed a little.

Qinglan calculated silently. Nine more draws before they reached the stretch of wall Hu San had stacked. He had to win before then, and it had to be a self-draw. Winning on Tao Lingtao's discard would only transfer points within their own side; their total would not change. Winning on either opponent's discard would earn four points for a small hand. Not enough for twenty-eight. Only a self-draw, collecting sixteen from each of the two Hengchang players—thirty-two points—could turn the match around.

A self-draw. Within nine draws.

On his first turn, he drew a North Wind. One already lay in Hu San's discard pool, but he kept it and discarded his East Wind.

Second turn: Three of Characters. One, Two and Three of Characters formed a sequence. He discarded the Four of Characters.

Third turn: Six of Bamboo. Five, Six and Seven of Bamboo formed a sequence. He discarded the South Wind.

The steward discarded a North Wind.

On his fourth turn, Qinglan drew another North Wind, completing a pair. He discarded the White Dragon.

Fifth turn: Seven of Dots. He discarded the Nine of Bamboo.

He was ready to win.

Yuening's fingertips skimmed her abacus as she worked it out for him. Three of Dots, Five of Dots or Eight of Dots would all win. Another Red Dragon would win too. All four North Winds had appeared; there was no point waiting for another.

Two Three of Dots tiles remained, four Five of Dots and four Eight of Dots. Three Red Dragons remained, but one lay revealed at the end of the wall, unavailable to draw. That left two he could draw.

Twelve tiles. Four turns in which to find one.

◆

On the sixth turn, Tao Lingtao discarded an Eight of Dots.

Someone in the crowd shrieked, “Eight of Dots! Scholar! That's one of your winning tiles!”

Every neck craned.

Qinglan did not reveal his hand.

He reached out and drew.

“He's mad!” someone cried, stamping a foot. “Throwing away a win that's right in his hands!”

“He can't calculate tile possibilities at all!”

Tao Lingtao covered her mouth. The steward stared, then rocked with laughter.

Only Yuening entered the turn in her ledger. Her eyes were brighter than before.

Seventh turn: One of Bamboo. Discarded.

Eighth turn: Nine of Characters. Discarded.

Hu San's fingers tapped softly, once, on the table's edge.

◆

The ninth turn.

Qinglan drew the tile and passed his thumb lightly over its face, just as his father had taught him ten years before.

He set it upright before him, then laid down his entire hand.

“Self-draw.”

The tile was a Three of Dots.

Yuening's abacus crackled.

“One, Two and Three of Characters: one sequence. Five, Six and Seven of Bamboo: one sequence. Three of Dots, Three of Dots, Three of Dots: one triplet. Six and Seven of Dots, with the Red Dragon standing for Eight of Dots: one sequence. Two North Winds: the pair of eyes.”

Her voice rose.

“A concealed hand, self-draw, Red Dragon acting as a wild tile! Sixteen points from each of the other three players. The payment within the Tao side cancels out. From the two Hengchang players—thirty-two points!”

Her voice rose higher. “Total across four hands: the Tao side wins by four points!”

Three hands' losses. Recovered in a single hand, with four points to spare.

The room erupted. The young embroiderers flung themselves at Tao Lingtao, screaming. Luo Jinke cradled her swollen hand and laughed through her tears. An old sugar-figure seller crushed the figure he was holding.

The steward's face turned grey, shade by shade.

Hu San stared fixedly at Qinglan. Beneath the table, his long hands closed into fists.

◆

Qinglan was not looking at them.

He was looking at the tiles on the table. As he laid his hand down, his fingertips had brushed a face-down tile that had slipped from Hu San's wall. An exceedingly faint incision marked its back, fine as a hair.

He turned it over.

A Red Dragon.

Qinglan looked up at Yuening. His voice was calm, so calm that the clamour of the room fell away.

“Scorekeeper, would you count the tiles on this table?”

Yuening stared, then understood. She set her abacus down and counted them, one by one.

One, two, three… The room held its breath. Nothing could be heard but the soft clicking of bone tiles. Click. Click. Click.

One hundred and thirty-four. One hundred and thirty-five. One hundred and thirty-six—

Yuening's hand stopped. One tile remained on the table.

“One hundred and thirty-seven.” She spoke each word distinctly. “There is one Red Dragon too many.”

Hu San's chair crashed to the floor.

◆

The room dissolved into chaos.

Hu San barged through the crowd towards the door. The steward covered his head and shouted, “Nothing to do with me!” Workmen ran after him or tried to stop him. A table overturned. Tiles scattered across the floor.

Qinglan stood motionless amid the uproar.

His gaze passed over the jostling heads and settled on the doorway.

A man in a woven hat stood there.

He neither ran nor pushed. He stood quietly within the doorframe, brim so low that his face was hidden. Only his raised hand was visible, sheathed in a black leather glove. The ghostly hand embroidered on its back in gold thread flashed in the afternoon sun.

Across the crowded room, the man in the woven hat gave Qinglan a small nod.

Then he pressed something into the wooden sign beside the door, turned and walked into the flow of people on Crossroads Street.

By the time Qinglan forced his way to the entrance, the man was gone.

A bone tile was embedded in the wooden “Mahjong Arbitration” sign.

A Red Dragon.

On its back, a fine incision: five fingers, spread wide.

(End of Chapter One)
