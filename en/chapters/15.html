Chapter 15A Different Table

The second day of the challenges dawned overcast.

Heavy cloud pressed over the sea. The wind was stronger than yesterday, rattling the four Pinghe banners. Yet the crowd was larger: Pinghe Hall had lost a seat yesterday, and all Nanchao wanted to see whether the arena that had never lost all four in thirty years could still hold today.

Before dawn, Jinghe had led an inspection of all four tables. Tops, legs, undersides. They looked over every inch. Found nothing.

“Change the table?” Liuxia’s right hand was still bandaged, her arm in a sling. “All four were guarded through the night. Who could have changed one?” But the hired hands who kept the night watch had been recommended by the trading house.

Qinglan stood below the platform, watching the four tables without speaking.

Four rosewood mahjong tables. The same colour. The same carvings. The same legs. He had watched them from the window all yesterday. Seen each clearly.

But today, the third table looked a fraction taller.

Only a fraction. As though someone had slipped a sheet of paper beneath its legs.

Meng Chuan emerged from behind the platform in a clean robe, his hair bound without a strand out of place. Xiaohe held the hem of his clothes and looked up.

“Brother. Will you win today too?”

“I will win when I can.” Meng Chuan crouched and smoothed the loose hair at her temple. “From now on, I’ll discard whatever tile I like. When I can win, I’ll win for you to see.”

Beside them, Liuxia turned away and rubbed her eyes hard with her good hand.

◆

At seven, the challenges began.

Qinglan, Zhumo and Yuening returned to the second-floor window. Jinshi and Huo Xiao kept watch below the platform. Pei Zhiwei stood in the crowd. Changfeng perched on an arcade column with a little red flag. They had agreed: if he saw anything wrong, he would raise it.

The first challenger was from Sea Whale Trading House. A lean middle-aged man in grey robes, wearing three gold rings. He chose Meng Chuan’s seat.

The third table.

“That one,” Qinglan said quietly.

“Which?” Yuening asked.

“The one that’s a little taller.”

◆

Meng Chuan sat, drew and arranged his tiles. His hands flowed like water.

The man in grey played neither quickly nor slowly. Each discard was set down gently, making a soft knock on the tabletop.

Qinglan’s frown deepened.

“Listen,” he said.

Yuening listened for a while. “To what?”

“The tiles landing,” Qinglan said. “On Meng Chuan’s side, a tap. On that man’s side, a hollow knock.”

Zhumo’s ears rose. After listening a while, his face changed. “That side sounds different, as though there’s a hidden layer. We need to inspect beside the table. From another building, we can’t decide what’s inside.”

◆

On the fifth turn, the grey-robed man lowered both hands beneath the table, as though adjusting his sleeves.

Qinglan’s ears were still on that hollow knock, but his eyes had already fixed on the man’s left hand.

On every previous turn, his left hand had rested on the table edge, the three gold rings dull beneath the clouded sky. Before drawing, his little finger had tapped lightly against the edge, as if finding something by touch.

“Yuening,” Qinglan said. “Which turn did he discard a Seven of Bamboo?”

“He discarded Seven of Bamboo on the third turn,” Yuening said. “I couldn’t see all his later concealed draws. We can’t say he never drew another copy.”

“Remember that Seven of Bamboo.”

The hand stayed beneath the table only a moment, then returned.

On the sixth turn, he drew, discarded and again lowered his left hand under the table. A pause.

On the seventh, he laid down his tiles.

“Self-draw.”

Meng Chuan stared at the hand. The Seven of Bamboo from the third turn was still in the river, and here stood another. On the tile’s corner clung a speck of fresh wood shavings.

“Stop!” Meng Chuan slapped a palm down over his own river. “Don’t—”

Unhurriedly, the man pushed his tiles into the middle and mixed them with the rest. “Brother Meng. The hand’s already been cleared. You’ve lost. Please step down.”

Jeers filled the grounds. The supervisor recorded who had first pushed the tiles and marked the table as disputed pending investigation.

Meng Chuan stood red-faced, fists white with tension. Below, Changfeng raised his red flag and waved frantically.

They had not preserved the original hand.

Another Pinghe seat was provisionally entered under the other side’s name.

Meng Chuan stepped down. Instead of returning to the shelter, he went straight to the arcaded building and shouted up at the window. “Tuidao! That Seven of Bamboo—”

“I remember that Seven of Bamboo from the third turn,” Qinglan said, leaning out. “But there are four copies of each face. One Seven of Bamboo alone can’t prove he swapped a tile. The hand you saw, the wood shavings at the tile’s corner, who pushed the tiles in first—leave every one of them for the supervisor.”

Meng Chuan’s fists loosened, then tightened. “I didn’t lose to his tiles,” he said through his teeth. “I lost to his table.”

◆

“A hidden compartment.”

In the shelter behind the platform, someone had spent a long time crouching and tapping beneath the third table. He crawled out with wood shavings all over his head.

Chai Shouheng, from the blade shop.

About fifty, a shiba dog in humanlike form, short and sturdy, with short tawny fur, white on his chin and chest, and arms as thick as posts. Ten short, thick fingers. Rust beneath his nails. When Jinghe’s messenger came for him, he had been forging a blade. He dropped his hammer and came at once.

“I didn’t make this table,” he said.

Jinghe spoke slowly beside him. “Master Chai’s hands made the copper rulers and bone-cutting knives used to examine tiles at the Nanchao branch of the Bureau of Balance. There is no timber or iron in Nanchao he doesn’t know.”

“You made them?” Qinglan asked, surprised.

“All four challenge tables. With my own hands, twenty years ago.” Chai Shouheng slapped the top. “Rosewood. One solid piece. Mortise-and-tenon joints. Not a nail. This one’s carvings copy mine. Well copied too. But its legs are nailed, and the top has two layers with a gap between.”

He reached beneath the edge on the challenger’s side. Click. A palm-sized wooden panel slid open.

Within was a narrow compartment, just large enough for three or four tiles.

“The player on this side nudges it with a knee, reaches down and swaps a tile from the hand for one inside.” Chai Shouheng’s face was cold. “Fine work. No more than three carpenters in Nanchao could do it.”

He paused, then added, “One of those three moved away three months ago. Said a big trading house had hired him. Paid in gold coins.”

“And our table?” Jinghe asked. “The original?”

Chai Shouheng pointed at another. “That one’s been changed too. Two out of four.”

The old master’s table.

◆

“Expose them now!” Liuxia stamped her foot. “The compartment is right here! Witness and evidence!”

“Expose whom?” Qinglan said. “The man’s already cleared his hand. The tiles are mixed. He’ll say the table belongs to Pinghe Hall. That Pinghe Hall made the compartment to frame its challengers.”

Liuxia had no answer.

“And,” Qinglan said, looking at Jinghe, “they changed two tables but have used only one today. They still mean to use the other.”

Jinghe watched him and slowly nodded.

But the old man’s gaze did not withdraw. It moved from Qinglan’s face to the hands hanging at his sides, then back to his chest.

“You were standing below the platform before dawn,” Jinghe said. “You saw a table leg raised by a sheet of paper. You heard which side the tiles landed dull. How many times that left hand tapped the edge, the Seven of Bamboo on the third turn—you let go of none of it.”

He tapped his cane lightly on the ground. “A third current has joined your breath.”

Qinglan started. His hand pressed to the front of his robe and found nothing. The White Dragon was no longer there. Since yesterday, a thin thread had appeared beside the two currents in his chest, there one moment and gone the next. Standing below the platform this morning, it had not broken once. He had not yet had time to think about it.

“It has joined,” Jinghe said. “Whether it holds steady remains to be seen.”

“Tuidao. What do you intend?”

“Seal the inspected table so they can’t switch it again,” Qinglan said. “Stop play and check the other, unopened one first. We can’t let the old master suffer again just to make tomorrow’s exposure easier.”

Jinghe called the branch office guards and had the compartment, remaining tiles and inspection process sealed and recorded separately. Play at the other table had already begun. A supervisor was sent at once, but the crowd surged, blocking the man carrying the call to stop at the steps.

Chai Shouheng looked at Qinglan, his gaze lingering on his face a moment longer, then suddenly slapped his thigh. “If we’re exposing it tomorrow, we need something to do it with. I’ll go back tonight and make a copper table-testing ruler. Slide it beneath the edge, tap once, and it’ll sound out every solid patch and hollow.”

“How long?”

“Before dawn.” Chai Shouheng rolled up his sleeves. “Twenty years ago, I spent three months on each of those tables. Someone’s forged my work. I won’t bear that disgrace.”

◆

By now a Sea Whale challenger had already gone up and chosen the old master’s seat.

The first table. The other one that had been changed.

The old master was over seventy. After a lifetime of play his hands were steady, but his eyes had dimmed. On the ninth turn, the messenger had only just squeezed through to the table when the challenger’s hand was already reaching underneath. “Stop!” the old master barked. Another witness saw it too: that hand was taking a tile from beneath the table. The challenger rushed to shout, “Self-draw!” The supervisor pressed both hands down on all four players’ tiles. “This win call is recorded as disputed for now.”

The old master slowly rose, left his seat sealed by the supervisor, saluted the crowd and stepped down for the time being.

He took the stairs slowly, one at a time, holding the rail. Meng Chuan rushed to help, but he waved him away.

“Fifty years playing,” the old man said softly. “I’ve never lost to a table.”

He looked at that table, then at the silver whales below, straightened his back and walked slowly into the crowd alone.

Sea Whale’s silver whales applauded again.

Qinglan’s gaze swept the crowd, every window of the arcaded buildings, the little table beside Chaosheng Teahouse’s stairs.

It was empty.

The patch of red had not come today.

His heart sank. Her absence unsettled him more than her presence would have.

Of the four provisionally entered seats, only Pinghe Hall’s champion seat remained undisputed. It held an empty armchair, a small purple-clay teapot resting on it.

◆

“I’ll play.”

At the upstairs window, Yuening suddenly stood.

Qinglan turned to her.

“The man in grey is only holding Meng Chuan’s seat provisionally.” Yuening’s voice was steady. “The table has been replaced with an inspected clean one. If he’s defending, he has to take challengers. I’ll go up and win it back with a clean hand.”

“But do you know Pinghe Hall’s play?” Zhumo asked anxiously. “Chows allowed, no wild tile…”

“I’ve spent five years at the scorekeepers’ table.” Yuening tucked her abacus into her belt. “I’ve recorded a thousand Pinghe Hall hands.”

She paused, her voice quieter. “My mother kept score too. Scorekeepers record other people’s games. They never take a seat themselves. But she wrote ‘This hand is suspect’ at the end of a game record. If she were here, she’d go up.”

Qinglan looked at her. After a moment, he nodded.

“Watch his left hand,” he said. “Before he swaps tiles, the gold rings on it touch the table edge.”

◆

When Yuening mounted the platform, the whole crowd stared.

A young girl with an abacus. Challenging for a seat?

The grey-robed man sat on the defender’s side. He looked her up and down and smiled. “Here to announce the scores, miss?”

“I’m here to challenge for your seat.” Yuening sat on the challenger’s side and set her abacus by the table. “Please.”

Before sitting, she watched the supervisor inspect the clean replacement, then ran her hand beneath the empty edge herself. No compartment. She had not come to test who could use one better.

◆

Yuening did not play quickly.

Pinghe Hall prized speed. She did not follow its pace. Before every discard, her finger tapped the edge of the table as though moving an invisible abacus bead.

One turn. Two. Three.

She remembered. Every discarded tile. Who had played it. On which turn. What the grey-robed man had chowed or punged. How many tiles remained in his hand. What they might be. One by one, she arranged them in her mind.

As they played, sweat appeared on the man’s forehead.

He could not understand it. This girl with her abacus played so slowly, tapping the table before each discard, like a child just learning. Yet every tile she played was one he did not want. Every tile he wanted, she kept.

As though she could see his hand.

He tried reaching beneath the edge again. Halfway there his fingers met solid clean wood and traced emptily along it. In an upstairs arcade window, a scholar watched his hand.

On the eighth turn, the grey-robed man became ready to win.

Yuening glanced at his discard: Five of Characters. He had clutched that Five for several turns and let it go only on the turn he became ready to win; what he had kept was most likely the Two and Three of Characters. Her finger paused against the table edge.

Looks like he’s waiting on One or Four of Characters. Three Ones are already in the rivers, one Four seen; at most three tiles left that he can wait on, and not all of them are necessarily in the wall.

She happened to hold a Four of Characters.

She kept it and discarded a Nine of Bamboo.

On the tenth turn, Yuening drew a tile and laid her hand down without looking.

“Self-draw.”

First, utter silence. Then the crowd exploded.

◆

Yuening’s legs felt weak as she came down.

Qinglan caught her at the foot of the steps.

“I won,” she said, her voice still shaking. “Clean table, won by the tiles. I won.”

“I saw.”

“That Four of Characters.” She tugged at his sleeve, eyes bright. “I kept it. Never let it go.”

“I saw that too.”

Zhumo pushed through the crowd, game record in his arms, ears trembling with excitement. “I wrote it all down! Every turn! Miss Yuening, this hand deserves a name!”

“What name?”

“‘A Single Four of Characters’!”

Jinghe stood with his walking stick beside the platform. He looked at Yuening for a long time, then saluted her.

“Young scorekeeper,” he said. “Pinghe Hall owes you a seat.”

“No debt,” Yuening said. “I’m holding it for Brother Meng. After tomorrow, it goes back to Pinghe Hall.”

Liuxia pushed through, arm in its sling, and looked Yuening up and down. After a long struggle, she managed, “You play faster than him.” Her chin jerked towards Qinglan.

Yuening paused, then raised her chin. “Of course.”

For once, Qinglan did not argue.

◆

Watching them, Jinshi could sit still no longer.

“My turn!” He rolled up his sleeves. “That fat fellow took Miss Liuxia’s seat. I’ll get it back!”

“Troupe master.” Liuxia glared at him, one arm hanging in its sling. “Do you know Pinghe Hall’s rules?”

“Yes! Chow and pung, right? Pung’s what I do best!”

Jinshi went up and challenged the stout man.

He called pung three times that hand. First East Wind. Then White Dragon. Then Green Dragon. Three exposed triplets of honour tiles. The spectators stared. How many years since anyone had punged like that on Pinghe Hall’s platform?

Still thinking of making another pung, he discarded a tile from his hand—exactly the one the stout man needed.

“Mahjong!”

Jinshi stared at the winning hand for ages, then slapped his forehead. “Why did I throw that one away again?”

The stout man’s flesh shook with laughter. “Lion-dance master! Plenty of noise from your pungs. Awfully quiet when it comes to winning!”

“Making noise takes skill too!” Jinshi lifted his chin. “Can you pung three triplets in one hand?”

The stout man choked. Someone below called, “Can he?” The crowd took it up. “Can he?” His face turned a dark, angry red.

Laughter filled the square.

Jinshi came down without a trace of dejection. He grinned at Liuxia. “Lost! But three pungs. Worth the trip!” The grin done, he flicked a quick glance up at that second-floor arcade window, then turned his head away.

She looked at him, tried to hold it in, then finally laughed aloud. Her first laugh since her hand had stiffened.

◆

At dusk, Qinglan wrote a letter and gave it to Ke Jingduo, the lead courier at the post station.

Ke Jingduo was in his twenties, a red dog in humanlike form, two pointed ears standing upright, travel-worn, his boots caked in mud. He took the letter and read the name on its cover: Tiandu. Bureau of Balance. Qi Tingjun.

“Ride through the night?”

“Through the night,” Qinglan said. “Three things in the letter. The burns on the back of the man’s hand. ‘Star’ inside his glove. And the street behind the Scorekeepers’ Institute.”

“Three days.” Ke Jingduo tucked it inside his clothes and mounted. “Give Li Xingzhou’s name, and every post station along the road will change me onto a good horse.”

He drew rein and glanced back. “Any word for Yunchuan?”

Qinglan thought. “For Master Xiao at the Rain Pavilion. Tell him the story isn’t finished. He can take his time telling it.”

Ke Jingduo grinned and shook the reins. Hooves rang down the flagstones.

The sound faded.

◆

In the blade shop south of the city, the forge burned all night.

Bare-chested, Chai Shouheng hammered a glowing copper bar, blow after blow. Sparks struck the short fur on his arms, singeing a small tuft. He did not so much as frown. In his hands, the bar grew thinner and straighter until it became a ruler about a foot long, thin enough for light to shine through.

He plunged it into cold water. A hiss. White steam rose high.

He lifted the ruler and tapped gently beneath the edge of the old table in the corner of the room. It had no carvings; its edge ran in a single straight line. Solid wood gave a dull sound. Hollow wood rang clear, like an empty bowl.

“Tomorrow,” the old smith murmured to the ruler, “all Nanchao will hear that sound.”

◆

At the inn, they sat together.

“Tomorrow,” Jinghe said from the table, cradling his little purple-clay teapot, “is the defending champion’s match.”

He sipped his tea and spoke slowly. “Twelve years ago, I played nine hands in succession. Won all nine. I heard from Master Zhumo that I slowed in the last one. Held a tile and looked at it for a long time.”

Zhumo’s ears stirred.

“In that hand, the player opposite swapped a tile,” Jinghe said. “I saw. But I had no proof. So I held that tile, looked at it for a long time, then laid down my hand anyway. Self-draw. I won, but it wasn’t a clean victory. I knew there was a ghost at the table, and I said nothing.”

He set down the teapot. “For twelve years, I’ve waited for that ghost to sit opposite me again.”

“Who’s challenging?” Jinshi asked.

Jinghe took a list from his sleeve and laid it on the table. In the space for the champion’s challenger was a single name:

Nameless.

Beside it was a little mark.

A woven hat.

(End of Chapter Fifteen)
