Chapter 6The Old Stone Archway

The old stone archway south of the city was the oldest in Yunchuan.

Four stone pillars. Three tiers of eaves. Wind and rain had long since worn the faces from the stone beasts at the corners. Once, words had been carved on the plaque in the centre. Now only blurred hollows remained, and no one could read them. Wild grass surrounded the archway; beyond it lay abandoned brick kilns. Few people came even by day. At night, there was only the chirping of insects.

Shortly before eleven, four people left through the Rain Pavilion's back door.

Xiao Cheng'an carried a lantern to the mouth of the alley and pressed it into Yuening's hand. “These old legs are no use. I won't go with you. Remember, if things turn bad, run first. A rule of storytelling: the main characters have to live until the final instalment.”

“Master Xiao.” Yuening did not know whether to laugh or cry. “Is that a curse or a blessing?”

“Both.” The old man stroked his beard and went back inside.

The road south took them across half the city. Yunchuan was quiet at night. In the moonlight, the Three-Coloured River had only one colour left, a heavy black, but its waters sounded louder than in daylight. Qinglan walked at the front and scarcely spoke.

He was thinking of two things: the White Dragon his father had left him, and the Two of Dots clasped in Gan Henian's palm. Each man had left a tile. Neither had managed to say what he meant.

“You're wondering,” Yuening said, coming alongside, “whether that cat will turn up at all tonight.”

“No,” Qinglan said. “I'm wondering what he will bring if he does.”

At midnight, the moon hung over the archway, cold and white.

When Qinglan arrived, someone was already crouching on top of the archway.

“Right on time.” Lu Zhaoye perched on the highest tier of eaves, his tail hanging down and swaying. “I thought you might bring a squad of Guards of Balance to arrest me.”

“The captain knows someone took the evidence.” Yuening hugged her ledger and looked up. “Whether you hand it over tonight or not, there has to be an outcome in the record.”

Lu Zhaoye laughed and leapt from the eaves, landing without a sound. He stopped in front of Qinglan, reached inside his clothes and flicked the yellowed old Red Dragon towards him.

Qinglan caught it, but did not put it away at once. Yuening brought the lantern closer, inspecting first the old chip beneath the final downward stroke of the red character, then the Ghost Hand mark on the back.

“He took it from his robe and placed it in your hand. We didn’t see what happened during the day between.” She recorded its provenance clearly. “The old marks we can see now match. We won’t fill in that missing day.”

Only after asking her did Qinglan take out the White Dragon his father had passed to him and bring the two objects together again in the starlight. Still the rim of a box; still that soft sound. Nothing more emerged of what had happened after the chip. He wrapped the old Red Dragon separately and returned the White Dragon to its red cloth.

“The tile’s back in your hands.” Lu Zhaoye said. “I carried it inside my robe all day, turned it over a hundred times. At last I worked it out.”

“Worked out what?”

“The man in the woven hat didn't leave this tile to frighten you.” Lu Zhaoye's smile vanished. “He wanted you to bring out something else.”

He looked at Qinglan's chest. “The White Dragon.”

◆

Qinglan's hand moved instinctively to the front of his robe.

“Before the night of the lion dance, I followed the man in the woven hat to where he was staying,” Lu Zhaoye said. “He had someone ask whether the Qing boy still carried that White Dragon. The other man asked if it was one from the tile wall. He said no. It was the one Qing Yuanshan had left his son.”

Qinglan looked at him. “So that was why you told me to bring it.”

“I wanted to see what he was really looking for,” Lu Zhaoye said. “With the old Red Dragon in my hands, you would come asking. With the White Dragon in yours, he would come looking.”

“You saw just now that there is more to it than a carved mark,” Yuening said. She filled in what had happened by the window on the first night.

Lu Zhaoye's tail stopped moving.

“They never told me that,” he said.

Yuening's expression changed. “So when you told him last night to ‘bring your father's White Dragon’—”

Lu Zhaoye looked down at the weeds around them.

“I meant to take the old tile and draw him out of the Rain Pavilion after me. But he never came,” he said. “Instead, I’ve used the old tile to bring the person with the White Dragon here. If he followed you rather than me—”

Qinglan and Yuening both understood the words left unsaid.

Jinshi stood with the gold-scarred lion head on his shoulder, thoroughly confused. He scratched the back of his head. “So… do we run, or do we wait?”

“Wait for someone.” Lu Zhaoye turned and whistled towards the grass.

◆

A man stepped out of the weeds.

About forty. Not tall, but his shoulders were broad as a door. His face was weather-beaten, his escort guard's short clothes dust-grey. A short ebony staff sat at his waist, an oilcloth bundle on his back. He came up to Qinglan and studied him from head to foot for a long time. Slowly, his eyes reddened.

“So like him,” he said. “So very like him.”

“This is Li Xingzhou. Twenty years escorting goods along the post roads,” Lu Zhaoye said. “I met him at the relay station outside the city. He was asking everyone whether the scholar who played as a substitute at the Rain Pavilion two days ago had the family name Qing.”

Li Xingzhou took the oilcloth bundle from his back and opened it layer by layer. Beneath the oilcloth was oiled paper. Beneath the paper, a flat wooden box. A yellowed sealing strip lay across it, the handwriting faded.

To be opened by Qinglan alone, on the day he is willing to take his place at the mahjong table.

Qinglan's hand trembled. His father's writing.

“Master Qing entrusted it to me nine years ago,” Li Xingzhou said. “He knew you’d sworn an oath. He said this letter mustn’t be used to force you to the table. I was to give it to you only when you chose to sit down yourself.”

Qinglan looked at the seal without speaking. His father’s condition was no pardon. At the Rain Pavilion, it had been Qinglan himself who said, “I’ll do it.” Only after he made that choice had the letter reached him.

Li Xingzhou took a tightly tied purse from inside his robe and set it beside the wooden box. When he opened it, gold coins lay heavy against one another.

“A bag of gold was the fee for this delivery,” he said. “Master Qing paid it back then.”

Qinglan opened the purse, took out one coin and placed it on the box lid. Moonlight brushed its face, revealing the seven stars of the Dipper.

Li Xingzhou glanced at Lu Zhaoye. “Only when word of the Rain Pavilion reached the relay station did I know to ask in Yunchuan. I couldn’t take one person’s word that the scholar from the day before yesterday really was a Qing. Brother Lu said he could bring me to meet him, so I came.”

Looking at the coin on the box, Qinglan’s throat tightened. “Brother Li, my father… was he well when he asked you to make this delivery?”

He had held that question for nine years. After his father’s tile-breaking, he had left Tiandu with him and lived in a small town for less than a year. After his father left, Qinglan had travelled farther alone and never gone back.

Li Xingzhou thought for a moment, then spoke slowly. “He rented a shop in the town and taught the children to read. Beyond their letters, he told them about the rules of the tiles, and the reasons behind them.”

“About the tiles?”

“How many tiles make a set. What cheating is. Why every tile at the table must be counted, one by one,” Li Xingzhou said. “He said that if children understood that, then when they grew up, no one could conjure an extra one before their eyes.”

He paused. “The day he entrusted this to me, his cough was terrible. But his hand did not shake at all. He took a long time to write those eight characters. When he finished, he said, ‘Xiaolan will understand.’”

Qinglan lowered his head and said nothing for a long time.

◆

The box held only one sheet of paper. On it, only eight characters:

Beneath the Dipper, the White Dragon bears a mark.

Moonlight fell across the paper. The ink looked freshly laid.

Qinglan knew that hand. When he was five, his father had guided his hand through his first written character: the word for “upright”. A horizontal stroke, a vertical, another horizontal, another vertical, then one long horizontal to anchor the whole. His father said its five strokes were like the five tile groups in a mahjong set. Without any one of them, it could not stand.

On the paper, the last dot of the character for “beneath” had been pressed down hard.

“The White Dragon bears a mark,” Yuening repeated softly. “We’ve seen the Ghost Hand mark. But if he wrote a letter about it, could there be something we haven’t seen?”

Qinglan took out the red cloth wrapping, opened it and turned the White Dragon over to the moonlight. The little hand at the lower right of its back showed clearly, five fingers spread.

But there was nothing beneath the hand.

“Where’s the Dipper?” Jinshi leaned over and squinted for a while. Then he took a gold coin from the purse beside the box and set it next to the White Dragon. “Here it is! There’s a Dipper right on the coin!”

Yuening rolled her eyes. “Master Jin. We're solving a riddle, not making change.”

Jinshi sheepishly returned the coin to the same purse, without putting it in his own belt.

“Just because the eyes can’t see something,” Zhumo, silent until now, slowly lowered his basket, “doesn’t mean it isn’t there.”

“I can try a rubbing through paper. No grinding, no new carving,” Zhumo said.

Qinglan laid the White Dragon on the spread red cloth. “Try. Record it as it is first.”

Zhumo took ink, inkstone and thin cotton paper from his basket. He first drew the tile’s existing marks separately, then covered it with paper and gently dabbed on pale ink. Yuening held up the lantern and shielded it from the wind. The ink appeared layer by layer. No one hurried him.

“A lifetime making rubbings of tile histories and inscriptions,” he murmured. “If it’s too shallow, it may not come out clearly. Let’s see how much it leaves us.”

When he lifted the paper, everyone held their breath.

On the white sheet, the ink revealed the hand with its five spread fingers. And beneath the palm, close against its heel, lay seven white points the size of pinpricks, arranged like a ladle.

The Big Dipper.

“Seven dots, shaped like the Dipper.” Yuening raised the lantern. “But turn this rubbing around and top becomes bottom. We can’t decide what lies beneath what just from how we’ve laid it out.”

Qinglan turned the paper back to match the White Dragon’s original orientation. His father’s words still lay in the wooden box. The seven white dots gave them only a shape.

“The Dipper often appears in the old records of the Hall of the Mahjong God,” Zhumo said softly. “I can look them up. I can’t say now that this must point there.”

“At least let’s keep these seven dots,” Qinglan said. “Let’s not let the people hunting the White Dragon finish the sentence for us.”

◆

Before he had finished, Lu Zhaoye's tail bristled.

“Down!”

A white streak flew from the brick kilns and struck the stone pillar behind Qinglan with a hard thunk.

A bone tile, embedded half an inch deep in stone.

Eight black-clad figures rose one after another from the weeds. The foremost wore a bamboo hat pulled low. He raised a black-gloved hand; the Ghost Hand embroidered in gold thread on its back flashed in the moonlight.

“Qing boy.” His voice was low and steady, as though rising from the bottom of a well. “Leave the White Dragon.”

Qinglan tucked the tile inside his robe.

“My father did not leave it to you,” he said. “Neither will I.”

Lu Zhaoye's short blade was already in his hand, every hair on his tail standing on end.

The brim of the woven hat tilted slightly, as though its wearer had only just noticed him. “The Lu family's little cat. Three years. Still not tired of chasing?”

“Not until you take off that hat,” Lu Zhaoye said through clenched teeth.

“Then you have a long chase ahead.”

The man said no more. His wrist turned. Three more bone tiles appeared between his fingers.

◆

All three came straight at Qinglan's chest.

Jinshi roared and swung the lion head in front of Qinglan. Thunk, thunk, thunk. The tiles buried themselves in the head, one right beside its golden scar.

“My lion!” Jinshi's eyes reddened with anguish. “Just mended it!”

He pulled the head over his own and charged the black-clad men.

Two raised their clubs to stop him and were sent flying backwards by the lion head. A third lunged from the side. Jinshi had no time to turn—

“Lend me a shoulder!”

Lu Zhaoye came up from behind, planted one foot on top of the lion head and sprang. Turning in mid-air, he struck the attacker's wrist with the back of his short blade. The club fell. So did its owner, flat on his back.

“Good cat!” Jinshi laughed from inside the lion head.

“Catfolk!” Lu Zhaoye landed without looking back.

Lu Zhaoye kept moving the moment he landed. His yellow-and-black shape flashed through the weeds, blade drawn. Two ringing blows turned aside the short clubs aimed at Yuening. Li Xingzhou drew his short ebony staff and stood guard before Zhumo, striking down their attackers and swearing all the while. “Nine years to deliver this, and it's delivered! Nobody's stealing it in the last few steps!”

Zhumo crouched behind a pillar with his bamboo basket, one hand clamped over the rubbing and inkstone, the other covering his eyes. He could not help peeking through his fingers.

A man in black came round the pillar and reached for the rubbing against his chest.

Zhumo cried out and, without thinking, snatched up the inkstone and flung its contents forward. Thick ink struck the man square in the face. The black-clad attacker rolled on the ground, clutching his eyes and cursing.

Clutching the empty inkstone, Zhumo stood stunned himself. After a long while, he murmured, “…Such good ink. What a waste.”

Yuening did not hide. With her back to the stone pillar, she worked the abacus rapidly. “Eight! Master Jin’s brought down two, Lu Zhaoye one, Brother Li one, Master Zhumo one—three left!”

The man in the woven hat remained before the brick kiln. Five white streaks swept across the weeds in succession, forcing Jinshi to turn the lion head across their path. Bone tiles bit into its wooden frame with five dull thuds.

The impacts drove Jinshi back half a step, but he kept the lion head before Yuening and Zhumo. The man sent no more tiles their way. His wrist turned towards Lu Zhaoye.

Qinglan stood beside a pillar, watching him.

Before every throw, his left shoulder dipped. Each time he threw right, his toes turned half an inch that way first.

Just as a player's finger always lingered on a tile before discarding it.

“Lu Zhaoye!” Qinglan shouted. “The next one goes right! At your leg!”

Lu Zhaoye sprang up. The tile skimmed beneath his foot and buried itself in the grass.

“The one after that—he'll switch hands! Left!”

Lu Zhaoye was still in the air when his short blade left his hand.

Steel flashed. The man's left hand jerked back. The blade split his woven hat down the middle.

◆

Moonlight slipped through the cut in the hat.

Qinglan caught only the outline of half a face: a hard jaw, thin lips, the faintest smile at one corner, impossible to read.

The man raised a hand to hold his split hat in place.

“You read very well,” he said softly. “As accurately as your father.”

“But your father read accurately too.” A pause. “Accuracy is no use.”

Suddenly, a line of torches flared in the distance. Hoofbeats approached. Someone shouted, “Bureau of Balance!”

The man in the woven hat stepped back into the brick kiln’s darkness. Those still able to run scattered after him. Two who had fallen earlier struggled up and stumbled behind the kiln. Someone groaned in the weeds. The guards of Balance dismounted and kept those who had not escaped apart.

Qinglan stood where he was. The words still rang in his ears. Accuracy is no use.

His father had read the tiles accurately. Ten years ago, he had won a hand by self-draw exactly as he had foreseen, yet could not stop the accusation that followed. Where that Red Dragon came from, who had touched the tiles, what the original record left out—Qinglan had not established any of it. Reading accurately could win a hand. A winning hand could not testify to the facts.

What would be enough?

He looked down at the rubbing in Zhumo's hands. In the moonlight, the seven white points lay quietly in their pattern.

◆

Qi Tingjun reined in his horse. Torchlight lit half the archway.

He glanced at the bone tile embedded in the stone pillar, then at the lion head bristling with tiles. His face darkened. “I left a man outside the Rain Pavilion. When you headed south after nightfall, he came back to report it. We reached here just as the noise broke out.”

He went to the pillar and tried to pull out the tile. It would not move. With a harder tug, he freed it. Not one of its four corners was chipped.

“A bone tile driven into stone, its four corners still intact.” Qi Tingjun held it up to the firelight and examined the hole in the pillar. “It may be more than strength in the hand. We must investigate both the tile and whoever threw it.”

Qinglan glanced at the wrapped White Dragon inside his robe, but did not take it out. He could not yet say whether its hint of green in the starlight and the bone tile driven into stone were the same kind of power.

Qi Tingjun had the extraction point recorded. This tile and those left in the lion head were listed separately, without mixing them into the provenance of the old Red Dragon.

“You followed us all the way, Captain?” Yuening asked.

“I do not trust you yet,” Qi Tingjun said evenly.

Qinglan approached and offered the yellowed old Red Dragon in both hands.

“Lu Zhaoye returned the old Red Dragon tonight,” he said. “Check it separately against the original sealed record, then register it in storage.”

Yuening opened the entry from the night they had recovered it. Qi Tingjun checked the old chip and marks, called for a box, and sealed it before them. He recorded that he had received it from Qinglan and gave a signed receipt. What had happened during the day after Lu Zhaoye took it remained open to inquiry. The new seal did not cover that gap.

Lu Zhaoye sidled up to Yuening, grinning. “Scorekeeper. Change last night's entry for me.”

“Change what?”

“‘One catfolk male’. Make it ‘one catfolk gentleman’.” He puffed out his chest. “From today, I'm coming with you.”

Yuening glared. “On what grounds?”

“I've hunted him for three years.” Lu Zhaoye's smile slowly faded. “Seven years ago, the Lu family shipping house lost everything in a sworn match of honour. A Red Dragon bearing the Ghost Hand mark turned up at the table. My father refused to accept the result. He was going to petition the Bureau of Balance. The night before he could, there was a fire. My brother pushed me out the back window. All he had time for was one shout: ‘Little cat, run.’ Eleven people in the Lu family. Only I survived.”

Even the insects in the weeds seemed suddenly silent.

“And because tonight, he came for you.”

He looked at Qinglan. “If I stay with you, sooner or later he'll show himself again.”

Qinglan watched him for a long time, then nodded.

Yuening did not scrape away the old entry. On tonight’s page, she only added: “Lu Zhaoye, one travelling companion.”

“Last night’s unauthorized breaking of the seal stays as it was,” she said. “Tonight’s return is a new entry for tonight.”

Delighted, Jinshi spread his arms and lunged towards him. “Brother! We're family now!”

Lu Zhaoye's face changed. In a flash, he shot up onto the archway and crouched on the highest eaves, his tail bristling like a feather duster. “You can talk without grabbing!”

“Just one hug!”

“Catfolk don't do hugs!”

Zhumo looked up for a while, then said unhurriedly, “I've recorded that too.”

◆

Near dawn, they started back.

Jinshi carried the tile-studded lion head on his shoulder, pulling the tiles out as he walked and wincing at each one. When he finished, he counted. “Seven!”

“Eight,” Yuening said without looking back. “You missed the one between its brows.”

Jinshi turned the lion head around. Sure enough, a tile remained embedded in its brow, right in the middle of the golden scar. He gaped for a while before managing, “…This lion’s hard to kill.”

Li Xingzhou tied the purse and pushed it into Qinglan’s arms.

“The delivery’s complete,” he said. “Take this bag for the road. Where you go now is your choice.”

Qinglan pushed the purse back into Li Xingzhou’s hands and took only the Dipper coin from the box lid.

“I’ll keep this one.” He put his father’s letter away separately and bowed to Li Xingzhou. “The rest is what you earned over these nine years.”

Li Xingzhou watched him for a moment, then returned the purse to his belt.

“I know the post road south,” Li Xingzhou said. “If you're going to Nanchao, give my name at the relay stations. Someone will change your horses for you.”

“How did you know we were going to Nanchao?”

Li Xingzhou grinned. “Who at the station doesn't know? That Hu Shan who cheated at the Rain Pavilion came from Nanchao. Your father's letter points to the Dipper, but Ghost Hand's tail lies to the south. North or south, you've got to start somewhere.”

Walking beside him, Yuening carefully folded the rubbing and slipped it into the inner pocket of her ledger.

“I keep the rubbing. You keep the White Dragon,” she said. “If one is lost, the other remains.”

Qinglan glanced at her. “You have thought of everything.”

“A scorekeeper's rule.” Yuening gave a small snort. “Keep two copies of the accounts.”

Day had just broken when a little embroiderer with her hair in two buns came running towards them, breathless. Reaching them, she almost fell to her knees.

One of the girls from the Tao family embroidery workshop.

“Young Master Qing! Sister Yuening!” She could barely breathe for crying. “Xingyu… the girl who embroidered the bamboo for you… She's collapsed! She's cold all over, and we can't wake her!”

Qinglan pressed a hand to the letter and coin inside his robe. His voice tightened at once. “Lead the way.”
