Chapter 6The Old Archway

Under the old archway there was a stone table, and at first only three people had been there.

Qinglan was still twenty paces from the archway when he saw the fourth.

That one sat on the empty stone stool on the west side of the table, back turned to him, narrow-shouldered, head bowed, as if studying the tiles in his hand. The moon shone down from behind the cracked plaque on the archway's brow, and it cast the shape of a person on the stool. Yet on the ground in front of the stool there was no shadow.

Qinglan stopped.

Behind him Jinshi stopped too, the lion head swaying in his arms. “What is it?”

“That stool on the west side,” Qinglan said. “Do you see anyone on it?”

Jinshi squinted at it for a long while. “It’s empty.”

Zhumo’s claws had already caught hold of Qinglan’s cuff. Every hair on the panda stood on end in the moonlight. He saw nothing, but he smelled it—the smell of old bone tiles, damp and dried, dried and damp again, year after year.

When they looked again, the stool was empty.

On the south stool someone was sitting now. One on the east, one on the north. Four shadows around the stone table, raising their hands as one, pushing tiles toward the center as one. The hands that pushed were ashen, knuckles swollen, as though they had soaked in water for many years. There was no sound. The instant the tiles reached the center, the four corners of the table lit up.

Four palm-sized copper plates were set into the corners. Their copper had been dull and dark, but now each gave off a faint yellow glow, very weak, like a lamp behind window paper. The moment the glow rose, the four shadows shuddered together and scattered, like a breath blown apart by the wind.

The glow of the copper plates went out with them.

The stone table was only a stone table again. A layer of moonlight lay on it like white frost, and the copper plates at the four corners had gone dark once more.

Qinglan looked at the four plates. The copper was alive; it had been opened as a boundary. When a shadow entered the circle, it lit.

“An old tile stall.” Zhumo’s voice was pressed very thin. “More than a hundred years ago there was a tile stall under this archway. People in the city who had lost at tiles and didn’t dare go home came here to win it back. The ones who couldn’t win it back, some of them, on this archway…”

He did not go on. He pointed a claw at the archway’s crossbeam.

Someone was crouching on the crossbeam.

That was halfway through the hour of the Pig, half an hour before midnight.

Go back further, and it was the back door of the Rain Pavilion.

Yuening had not come. The debt-collecting game kept no scorekeeper and left not a single sheet of paper; had she gone, she would only have been standing witness for the man in the woven hat. “What is seen awaits proof; it is not testimony”—she had said those words twice on the stairs. But she had tied a stack of trimmed rough paper, a stub of ink, and a brush into a small roll with a thin hemp cord and pressed it into his left hand.

“Your right hand is still numb,” she said. “Write with your left. Ugly characters don’t matter. Just don’t get the tiles wrong.”

When he took it, her fingertip pressed on the roll a moment longer, as if setting a seal. Under her nail was a thin line of cinnabar, left from the afternoon at the academy, when she had pressed the resealing fingerprint on the little medicine-packet box of Captain Qi’s; she had not washed it all off.

“You write it down,” she said. “When you’re back, I’ll copy it out.”

She glanced again at the blue silk cord at his waist. Two pieces of Mahjong Jade hung on it, and the knot was still the one she had tied, loop pressed over loop.

“While the cord is there,” she said, “you have to be there too.”

Then she turned and went upstairs without looking back.

Before he left, he had changed out of his old blue robe. The old robe was thin, had no hidden pocket in the cuff, and the river wind at midnight was cold. At noon Yuening had had the substitute player’s robe sent back to the Rain Pavilion, folded square and neat, Xingyu’s length of red hair cord still on its red sash. He put it on and tucked the copper coin into the hidden pocket of the right sleeve.

The White Dragon was over his heart. That morning, in front of the evidence chest, Zheng Shouzhuo had told him: “At midnight tonight, don’t bring that White Dragon.” Qinglan had thought about it all afternoon and brought it anyway. Last night, on the roof ridge, Lu Zhaoye had tossed off a line: bring your father’s White Dragon. On the back of the copper coin was carved the collection of one game; what was to be collected needed no writing. If he went without the White Dragon, it meant he had conceded the debt-collecting game himself; the debt would not be cleared, and that man could still come. If he left it in the pavilion, there were only Yuening, Xiao Cheng'an and two waiters there, and that man had been on the Rain Pavilion’s roof ridge before. Carried on his body, it was at least in his own hands.

The copper coin with two file marks was in that hidden pocket. Zhumo had said the vine recognized debts, not people: carry an uncollected debt on you, and the vine would not touch you—the creditor wanted you to repay with your own hands, and no one else could collect it for him first. This morning Zheng Shouzhuo had been caught by the vine precisely because his copper coin with one file mark had been turned out the day before yesterday in front of a full house, pressed under the paper, and was now locked in the evidence chest at the branch office.

Qian Youqing was in the Bureau of Balance’s prison, and Hengchang had already been judged to have betrayed the Way. There were not many people left in the city who could run errands for the man in the woven hat.

Qi Tingjun’s men were waiting at the fork in the road a li away. At noon, at the door of the branch office, he had put it very plainly: “If you shout, they come in. If you don’t, they wait till dawn.”

Qinglan had no intention of shouting. He wanted to see a hand.

The figure on the crossbeam jumped down.

He landed without a sound. It was Lu Zhaoye, the orange-striped cat, his golden eyes glowing like two little lamps in the moonlight, a layer of dust from the archway on his black-and-gold close-fitting clothes. He shoved his short blade into his belt and grinned at Qinglan, one small fang showing.

“You’re early,” he said. “He isn’t here yet.”

“The old Red Dragon?”

Lu Zhaoye took an oilpaper packet from inside his jacket and weighed it in his hand, but did not pass it over. “Here.”

“You picked it out of the notarized wooden box,” Qinglan said, “just so you could bring it here tonight?”

“I picked it out because I wanted to know who wanted it.” Lu Zhaoye tucked the packet back inside his jacket. “I squatted in the broken temple north of the city for a day and a night, waiting for that vine to come and snatch it. The vine didn’t come.”

His tail flicked once behind him.

“The vine didn’t come because it didn’t need to,” he said. “Last night on the roof ridge it could have held me. It let go. I thought I’d just run fast. Only this morning did I work it out: it let me go.”

“Why let you go?”

“Let me take the old Red Dragon, and I have to hide. I hide, so you all have to find me. You all look for me, so you come here.” Lu Zhaoye tapped his own forehead with the hilt of his blade. “I’m afraid he picked the date of this game for me long ago. Whether I took that Red Dragon or not, you’d have had to come tonight. I thought I’d intercepted one of his tiles for you. Really, I drove you through this door for him.”

When he finished, the corner of his mouth twitched, as if he wanted to laugh, but no laugh came.

“I fell for it,” he said. “Go ahead and curse me.”

Qinglan did not curse him. He looked at Lu Zhaoye’s ringed tail; the fur at its tip was bristling and had not settled.

“Which side will you sit?” he asked.

Lu Zhaoye was taken aback.

“The debt-collecting game needs four people,” Qinglan said. “Him, one. Me, one. Two seats left.”

“West,” said Lu Zhaoye. “I want to watch his hands.”

The midnight watchman’s clapper sounded faintly from the city.

Before its sound had died away, someone was already sitting on the north stool.

No one saw how he had got there. A dark-brown bamboo woven hat was pulled down low, and beneath it showed a pale stretch of chin. The hem of a black robe hung over the edge of the stool, perfectly still. Two hands rested on the stone table in black leather gloves, and on the back of each glove a hand with five spread fingers was embroidered in gold thread. The right glove was half an inch larger than the left.

On the east stool someone sat as well.

Tall and thin, yellow teeth, long slender fingers, clothes of greyish coarse cloth. Hu Shan.

Two days ago he had overturned the table at the Rain Pavilion, crashed out through the front door, and run. The whole city had hunted him for two days. Now he sat here with a wall already built in front of him, tiles face down, the head and tail of the wall as even as if measured with a ruler.

He smiled at Qinglan, his yellow teeth grey in the moonlight.

“Young Master Qing,” he said, “we meet again.”

Qinglan ignored him. He walked to the south stool and did not sit.

“The wall. Push it over and build it again,” he said.

Hu Shan’s smile froze on his face. He turned to look north.

From under the woven hat came a voice, very low: “As you like.”

Qinglan reached out his left hand and pushed Hu Shan’s wall apart.

The bone tiles spilled across the table with a clatter. Lu Zhaoye sat down on the west side and pressed both paws into the heap, shuffling along. Hu Shan did not move; neither did the gloves to the north. Qinglan shuffled for a long time, until every tile had changed position three or four times, before he stopped.

“Each builds his own,” he said.

The four of them each built a wall, seventeen stacks long, two tiers high. As Qinglan built his own, he ran his left thumb over the face of every tile—of the one hundred and thirty-six tiles, thirty-four passed under his hand, and all thirty-four were ordinary bone faces with bamboo backs, a brand mark on the back, no little hand carved there.

“The agreement,” said the man in the woven hat.

From his sleeve his left hand drew out a sheet of paper, and with two fingers pressing it down, he pushed it to the center of the table.

On the paper was written:

One hand, Tuidao School rules. The third tile from the tail of the wall is turned up to fix the ghost; the turned tile stays in the wall and is not drawn. Pung and kong allowed; no chow. Seven Pairs may win. Only South and North count: if South wins a hand, South wins; if North wins a hand, North wins. If East or West wins a hand, or a drawn hand, it is a tie. A tie goes to the creditor.

If South wins, the debt is cleared and North answers one question. If North wins, he takes the stake.

The stake: the White Dragon.

Qinglan read the paper through, and when he reached the line “A tie goes to the creditor,” he stopped.

The creditor was the man in the woven hat. If East or West won, it was a tie, and a tie counted as a win for the man in the woven hat. Which meant that in this game, if Hu Shan won a single hand, Qinglan lost.

A Hu Shan serving as dealer, plus a North seat that could feed him tiles. Of the four people, two were on the same side.

“I’ll add a clause,” Qinglan said.

There was no sound from under the woven hat.

“One discard, two winners,” Qinglan said. “Each counts as winning a hand; if South or North is among the winners, judge by South and North.”

By the ordinary rules, when two players could both win on one tile, the next player went first and intercepted the win—a tile discarded by North, the next player was East; East would win first, and South could not win. With this clause added, both could win together; and when both won together, there was no need to argue over whether the East or West win counted as a tie. Only South and North mattered.

Hu Shan’s eyes darted, and he looked north again.

After a moment the man in the woven hat said, “As you like.”

He took up a brush from the edge of the table with his left hand and added a line beneath the agreement. The characters were thin and leaned to the left, every final stroke dragging down and to the left.

“Inspect the stake,” said the man in the woven hat.

Qinglan took the White Dragon from over his heart and set it in the very center of the stone table.

The White Dragon was warm, its back unmarked. The moon shone on it, white as a bone freshly pared.

The man in the woven hat raised his left hand and held it over the White Dragon, palm down, less than an inch from the tile’s face.

He did not touch it.

The White Dragon went suddenly cold.

The cold shot up from the tile’s face all at once; Qinglan, standing an arm’s length away, felt it in his fingertips. A layer of white frost formed on the face of the White Dragon. The frost crept from the center of the tile out to its four edges, reached the rim, and then crept along the tabletop toward the underside of that hovering glove—and up onto the glove’s fingertips.

At the very moment the frost climbed onto the glove, beneath the heel of the hand of the man in the woven hat, from the side of the White Dragon pressed against the stone table, a little light seeped through.

The light came from beneath the tile, through a layer of bone, and showed as seven points.

They were fine as pinholes, arranged in the shape of a dipper, and lit for only the blink of an eye before going out. The frost drew back after them, back off the glove, back to the rim, back to the center of the tile, until not a trace remained, and the White Dragon was a lukewarm White Dragon again.

Qinglan’s gaze fell on the four corners of the stone table.

Of the four copper plates, not one had lit.

He thought of the table of justice at the Rain Pavilion. In the fourth game, when Hu Shan had done his tile swapping up his sleeve, the White Dragon had turned cold as ice over his heart, and the copper boundary had not given off a thread of light. That was the White Dragon recognizing a counter-breath; the White Dragon had gone cold by itself, not because anyone had moved breath at the table.

The man in the woven hat wore those gloves, and the gloves could hide the counter-breath on him so the copper boundary could not reveal it; but they could not hide breath he worked. If he moved so much as one breath at this table, the four copper plates would light all the same.

The four copper plates had not lit not because the gloves had suppressed it for him, but because he had only held his palm there and had not moved his breath.

He was only looking. With one left hand, from an inch away, he had taken a look at whether this White Dragon recognized him.

The White Dragon had recognized him, and then had not.

The man in the woven hat drew back his left hand and laid it on the table’s edge, the five fingers curling slowly once.

“It’s the one,” he said. “Sit.”

Hu Shan sat East and opened as dealer.

The man in the woven hat reached out his left hand, counted from the tail of the wall to the third tile, and turned it up.

The White Dragon.

Lu Zhaoye sucked in a hissing breath. Hu Shan bared his yellow teeth in a grin. “What luck.”

Qinglan did not smile. The turned tile stayed in the wall and would not be drawn. Of the one hundred and thirty-six tiles, there were four White Dragons; with this one showing, three remained, and all were ghosts. A ghost could stand in for any tile. Whoever held more ghosts would be faster.

He stood his thirteen tiles up.

Two Two of Characters, two Six of Dots, two East Wind. Seven of Characters, Three of Characters, Four of Dots, One of Dots, Eight of Bamboo, Nine of Bamboo, one each. And one White Dragon.

A ghost. He had a ghost in hand.

Three pairs, one ghost, six unpaired tiles. Four pairs short of Seven Pairs. Further still from being able to win a hand the ordinary way.

He made up his mind: go for Seven Pairs. Seven Pairs needed only pairs, no incomplete meld, no fighting anyone for a sequence, and a ghost could match any unpaired tile to make a pair.

In the first round, Hu Shan discarded a North Wind. Qinglan drew a South Wind and discarded it as soon as he drew it. Lu Zhaoye discarded a Nine of Characters. The man in the woven hat drew a tile.

Qinglan watched very closely how the man in the woven hat drew.

He drew with his left hand and did not look at what he drew. His thumb swept once over the tile’s face, and the tile stood into the row before him. The sweep was vertical, top to bottom. After the sweep, he did not even need to think about where in the row the tile went.

Through a layer of black leather glove, he could still read a tile’s face by touch.

In the second round, Qinglan drew a North Wind and discarded the North Wind. Lu Zhaoye discarded a One of Bamboo.

In the third round, Qinglan drew a Nine of Bamboo, making a fourth pair. He discarded the One of Dots.

In the fourth round, Hu Shan discarded an Eight of Characters. Qinglan drew a Five of Characters and discarded the Five of Characters. The man in the woven hat drew a tile and swept it vertically with his left thumb—paused—then swept it once more, crosswise.

Qinglan’s eyes fixed on that thumb.

The vertical sweep was to feel the suit and the number; the crosswise one meant he could not feel it clearly. Through a glove, the circles of Dots and the strokes of Characters could be told apart with one vertical sweep; but the joints on Bamboo were dense, and a Seven of Bamboo and an Eight of Bamboo differed by only one joint. Through a layer of leather, a vertical sweep would confuse them.

The man in the woven hat put the tile he had swept crosswise at the far right of the row in front of him.

Then he discarded a Three of Bamboo.

Hu Shan slapped the table. “Pung!”

He showed two Three of Bamboo, scooped the one from the river over, laid the three side by side in front of him, and discarded a Nine of Dots.

In the fifth round, Qinglan drew a West Wind and discarded the West Wind.

In the sixth round, he drew a Seven of Characters.

The fifth pair.

Two of Characters, Seven of Characters, Six of Dots, Nine of Bamboo, East Wind: five pairs. The Four of Dots was no use now; besides that, he still had a White Dragon, a Three of Characters, and an Eight of Bamboo. The White Dragon was a ghost; ghost with the Three of Characters made a pair, ghost with the Eight of Bamboo made a pair too. If a Three of Characters came, the Three of Characters paired and the ghost went with the Eight of Bamboo; if an Eight of Bamboo came, the Eight of Bamboo paired and the ghost went with the Three of Characters.

He was ready to win. Seven Pairs, waiting on Three of Characters or Eight of Bamboo; drawing another White Dragon would do as well.

He discarded the Four of Dots.

That round the man in the woven hat discarded a Seven of Bamboo.

He pulled it from the middle of his row, not the far right.

Qinglan lowered his eyes and looked at the Seven of Bamboo in the river.

If the tile at the far right, the one swept crosswise, were a Seven of Bamboo, he should have discarded from the far right; he had discarded a Seven of Bamboo from the middle, so the one kept at the far right was, eight chances in ten, an Eight of Bamboo.

An Eight of Bamboo, held in North’s hand, not discarded.

Hu Shan, the East seat, had made a pung of Three of Bamboo, and not one tile he had discarded was Bamboo.

In the seventh round, Qinglan drew a Five of Dots.

His unpaired tile was the Three of Characters. Three of Characters, Five of Dots: both unpaired. Discarding either would not change how many tiles he waited on; Seven Pairs was Seven Pairs. Swap one unpaired tile for another, and the tile he waited on changed with it.

He discarded the Three of Characters and kept the Five of Dots. Waiting on Five of Dots or Eight of Bamboo.

From the west, Lu Zhaoye shot him a glance and said nothing.

This hand was played for the man in the woven hat to see.

A player going for Seven Pairs, once ready to win, should stop changing his unpaired tile and discard whatever he drew. But a Seven Pairs hand not yet ready also swapped one unpaired tile for another. Had he discarded whatever he drew from the sixth round on, the man in the woven hat would have known at a glance he was ready and known which tile to guard against. By swapping unpaired tiles, he looked as if he were still one pair short.

In the eighth round, Hu Shan drew a tile and discarded a Four of Characters. His fingers paused on the edge of the table, but did not tap.

Qinglan drew a Two of Dots and discarded the Five of Dots, waiting on Two of Dots or Eight of Bamboo.

The man in the woven hat drew a Two of Characters and discarded it without even looking. Two of Characters: two in Qinglan’s hand, this one in the river, the third.

In the ninth round, Qinglan drew a Nine of Characters and discarded the Nine of Characters. The man in the woven hat drew a South Wind and discarded the South Wind.

There were forty-odd tiles left in the wall.

In the tenth round, Qinglan drew a Six of Characters. He held the Six of Characters and did not discard it right away.

Among the tiles North had discarded were a Three of Bamboo, a Seven of Bamboo, a Two of Characters, and a South Wind. He had discarded two Bamboo, both from the suit Hu Shan wanted. The tile at the far right he had never touched.

He was waiting for Hu Shan.

Hu Shan was making a Full Flush in Bamboo. He had a pung of Three of Bamboo and had not thrown a single Bamboo into the river. From the North Wind in the first round on—Eight of Characters, Nine of Dots, Four of Characters…—everything he threw was from other suits. To win a hand, he needed a Bamboo.

The man in the woven hat was holding an Eight of Bamboo, waiting for Hu Shan to be ready first. If Hu Shan was not ready and he discarded it, it was a tile thrown away for nothing; once Hu Shan was ready, he would discard it, East would intercept the win, a tie, and the creditor would win.

Qinglan discarded the Six of Characters.

Lu Zhaoye discarded a One of Bamboo. Hu Shan glanced at the One of Bamboo and did not pung; he held only one One of Bamboo and could not. The man in the woven hat drew a tile and discarded a Nine of Characters.

In the eleventh round, Hu Shan drew a tile.

He brought the tile up before his eyes, frowned for a moment, relaxed, then pulled a One of Characters from his standing tiles and dropped it into the river, his fingers tapping lightly on the table’s edge three times.

Tok, tok, tok.

Two days ago at the Rain Pavilion, in the eighth round, he had tapped exactly like this. Discarded a Nine of Characters, tapped three times, as if counting how many tiles he still had to win on.

He was ready to win.

The White Dragon over Qinglan’s heart neither cooled nor warmed.

He drew a tile. A West Wind.

He brought the West Wind before his eyes, looked at it once, did not put it into his standing tiles, and discarded it straight away.

Discarding whatever he drew. Only a player ready to win played like that.

This tile he discarded deliberately, for the man in the woven hat to see.

The man in the woven hat saw it. The woven hat dipped half an inch.

South was ready to win. If South self-drew first, South won. For North to win, only one road was left: let East win a hand now, ahead of South.

Lu Zhaoye discarded a Nine of Dots.

The man in the woven hat drew a tile. His left thumb swept it vertically, and the tile went into the middle of his standing tiles. Then his left hand moved to the far right, picked up the tile that had never been touched, and laid it in the river.

The Eight of Bamboo.

Hu Shan’s hand had already shot out. “Win!”

He pushed his tiles over flat on the table: the three Three of Bamboo he had punged, One, Two, Three of Bamboo, Four, Five, Six of Bamboo, a pair of Five of Bamboo, and a Seven of Bamboo and a Nine of Bamboo with the Eight of Bamboo between them.

The Eight of Bamboo in the middle, a Full Flush in Bamboo.

“East wins—” Hu Shan’s voice shot up high. “A tie! A tie goes to—”

“Win,” said Qinglan.

He pushed his own thirteen tiles over.

A pair of Two of Characters, a pair of Seven of Characters, a pair of Six of Dots, a pair of Nine of Bamboo, a pair of East Wind. An Eight of Bamboo, and with the Eight of Bamboo in the river, a pair. The last tile was a Two of Dots, and beside it stood a White Dragon.

The ghost matched the Two of Dots.

Seven Pairs.

For an instant the stone table was silent.

“One discard, two winners. Each counts as winning a hand; if South or North is among the winners, judge by South and North,” Qinglan said. “That line at the bottom of the agreement is in your hand. East’s win does not count as a tie.”

He looked at the woven hat.

“South wins a hand. South wins.”

Hu Shan’s face went from red to white, and from white to ashen. He turned to look at North. North did not move.

The left hand of the man in the woven hat rested on the edge of the table, and not one of its five fingers moved. After a while he lifted that hand, drew from his standing tiles the tile he had just drawn, turned it over, and laid it on the table.

It was a White Dragon.

A ghost.

He had already held this ghost; he could have made himself ready. He had not waited. He had fed out the Eight of Bamboo first.

“Good,” said the man in the woven hat.

Just that one word.

Qinglan took the copper coin with two file marks from the hidden pocket of his right sleeve, laid it on the table, and pushed it across.

The man in the woven hat reached out his left hand and pressed two fingers on the copper coin, pressing once.

“Cleared,” he said.

He pushed the copper coin back. The two file marks were still on it, but the cold that had clung to the White Dragon had receded.

Qinglan put the copper coin back in his right sleeve.

“One question,” said the man in the woven hat.

Qinglan asked: “Why do you want the White Dragon?”

He had long since decided what to ask. Ask “Who are you?” and the man would give a false name; ask about “that game at the Wangjiang Teahouse ten years ago,” and the man would say something he could not understand; ask “Who sent you?” and the man would say “I came myself.” Only by asking what he wanted would the man have to name a thing.

Under the woven hat there was a long silence.

“I don’t want it.” The voice was so low it seemed spoken against the stone table. “…Someone else does.”

The four copper plates at the corners of the stone table had not lit once, from beginning to end.

Hu Shan moved first.

He flung the row of tiles in front of him forward, bone tiles crashing across the table, and he had already vaulted off the stool and was plunging into the bamboo grove to the east.

Lu Zhaoye did not chase him.

Lu Zhaoye’s blade was already out of its sheath. He sprang from the west stool, cleared the stone table, and drove the blade’s point straight at the woven hat on the north side.

The man in the woven hat leaned back and slid three feet away, stool and all. His left hand flicked from his sleeve, and three points of white light came flying. Lu Zhaoye twisted at the waist to dodge two; the third grazed past his ear and, with a sharp tak, drove into the stone pillar of the archway.

It was a bone tile. Half of it was sunk into the stone, and its face had not cracked.

The man in the woven hat was already on his feet, retreating behind the archway. Behind the archway was an abandoned brick kiln, its mouth a black hole, like an open mouth.

Qinglan followed him in.

Behind him Jinshi roared something, and Zhumo’s claws slipped from his cuff. He had no time for them. Qi Tingjun needed to know whether that hand was the left or the right. He had watched a whole game at the stone table, and all he had seen was the left. But sitting and discarding was one thing, and fighting was another—when his life was at stake, a man would use the hand that came most naturally.

It was darker inside the kiln than outside; only a hole where the kiln roof had collapsed let down a pillar of moonlight.

From the darkness on either side of the kiln’s mouth three black shapes darted out, all dressed in black, faces wrapped in cloth, short clubs in their hands.

The light of Lu Zhaoye’s blade flashed in the kiln. He weaved in and out among the three black shapes like a cat running along a wall top. The back of his blade struck one man’s wrist, and the club flew from his grip; a sweep of his tail caught another behind the knee, and that one went down kneeling.

The man in the woven hat stood at the edge of the pillar of moonlight and raised his left hand again.

Bone tiles flew. One drove into a crack in the bricks by Lu Zhaoye’s foot; one slit open his left sleeve. The sleeve of the black-and-gold close-fitting clothes split open, and the orange fur beneath showed through.

Qinglan watched that hand.

When the left hand rose, the left shoulder of the man in the woven hat thrust forward. His right hand hung at his side the whole time, drawn back into his sleeve and held in front of his waist, as though guarding something that must not be bumped. Every time he threw a bone tile, his left shoulder had to thrust forward once and draw back, and in between there was a gap of half a breath.

“His left shoulder!” Qinglan shouted. “When he throws, his left shoulder is open!”

Lu Zhaoye heard.

The fourth time the man in the woven hat raised his left hand, Lu Zhaoye did not dodge. He shot forward low along the ground, slipped under that bone tile, and flicked his blade upward from below.

The man in the woven hat leaned to the right, a fraction too slow.

The point of the blade caught the brim of the woven hat and swept upward. A gash split open in the dark-brown bamboo woven hat, running from the brim almost to the crown, seven or eight inches long. The bamboo strips curled up, and moonlight leaked in through the gash, showing a wisp of grey-white hair beneath the hat.

Only a wisp.

The man in the woven hat raised his left hand and pressed the woven hat down.

From the kiln’s mouth came a crash.

Jinshi had charged in. He kept one hand tucked inside his jacket—that hand had blistered at the academy in the afternoon, and the fingertips were still numb—and with his other arm swung the lion head and rammed sideways, slamming one black-clad man who was lunging at Qinglan with his club, man and club together, into the kiln wall. The fleck of gold set in the crack on the lion head’s brow flashed once in the moonlight.

Another black-clad man lunged in from the side and brought his club down at the back of Jinshi’s head.

A dark, heavy thing flew in from the kiln’s mouth and struck that man on the back of the skull.

The man swayed and pitched forward to the ground.

It was an inkstone. Zhumo stood at the kiln’s mouth, the panda’s two paws still raised, the bamboo basket askew on his back, his mouth wide open, as if he had frightened himself.

“I, I…” he stammered. “What I threw was my inkstone… that inkstone I’ve used for two hundred years of copying tile manuals…”

The third black-clad man was at the far end of the kiln, about to climb toward the hole.

From outside the kiln’s mouth, another man came in.

Broad-shouldered, about forty, a blue kerchief wrapped around his head, in a short jacket and trousers of grey cloth, a blue cloth sash at his waist, and a grey-brown oilcloth bundle on his back. In his hand was a short ebony staff. He came through the entrance without breaking stride, jabbed the short staff forward, and struck that black-clad man square in the small of the back.

The man did not even grunt. He went limp.

The man in the woven hat had retreated to the very back of the kiln.

At the very back was the rear wall, grass growing in the cracks between its bricks. He stood with his back against that wall, the gash in his woven hat gaping in the moonlight, his left hand hanging down, his right still drawn into his sleeve.

He looked at Qinglan once.

Through the woven hat Qinglan could not see his eyes. But he felt that look land on his heart, on the White Dragon, very cold.

Then the man in the woven hat leaned back.

With a roar, a hole burst open in the rear wall. Bricks toppled outward, dust billowed up, his figure flickered in the dust, and he was gone.

Qinglan rushed to the edge of the hole. Outside was a desolate slope covered in wild bamboo. The moonlight shone on the bamboo tips, and there was nothing on them.

He shouted at the top of his voice toward the fork in the road:

“Qi Tingjun—!”

The lanterns of the Guards of Balance arrived the time it takes to drink a cup of tea later.

Qi Tingjun walked at the very front, his Balance blade half drawn. He looked at the three black-clad men lying in the kiln, looked at the hole in the rear wall, looked at the bone tile half sunk into the stone pillar, and finally let his gaze rest on Qinglan.

“Where is he?”

“Gone,” Qinglan said. “Hu Shan ran into the bamboo grove to the east. The man in the woven hat left through the rear wall.”

Qi Tingjun raised a hand, and the Guards of Balance split into two groups, one heading east, one heading up the back slope. The two who remained tied up the three black-clad men.

He walked up to the stone pillar and touched the bone tile with his scabbard. “Which hand threw it?”

“The left,” Qinglan said. “From inspecting the stake to discarding to throwing hidden weapons, from beginning to end, only the left hand. His right hand didn’t move at all on the table, and once the fighting started he drew it into his sleeve and held it in front of his waist. His right glove is half an inch larger than his left.”

Qi Tingjun’s scabbard stopped on the bone tile.

“I watched a whole game at the stone table,” Qinglan said, “and watched again in this kiln. Both times, the left hand.”

Qi Tingjun pushed the blade back into its scabbard, very slowly.

“Good,” he said. “That Two of Dots—I’ll turn it over as soon as I’m back in Tiandu.”

Lu Zhaoye crouched at the edge of the stone table, took the oilpaper packet from inside his jacket, and tossed it to Qinglan.

“Take it,” he said. “I took it to catch a fish. Didn’t catch the fish, and nearly caught you instead.”

There was a line of blood on his ear where the bone tile had grazed it. He wiped it with the back of his paw and smeared a patch of red.

“That wisp of his hair,” Lu Zhaoye said suddenly, “was grey.”

Qinglan lifted one corner of the oilpaper. Inside was the old Red Dragon, the little five-fingered hand, ten years of black grime packed between its fingers, no dot.

“The fire at the Lu house,” Lu Zhaoye said, “seven years ago. At last I’ve seen one hair of his.”

He slid his short blade back into his belt, leapt up onto the archway’s crossbeam, and crouched there, not coming down.

The man with the oilcloth bundle had stood at the kiln’s mouth all this while without moving.

Only when the Guards of Balance had dispersed did he come over and stop in front of Qinglan. He tucked the short staff under his arm and cupped one fist in his other hand.

“Young Master Qing?”

“I am.”

“The son of Mr. Qing Yuanshan?”

Qinglan’s throat tightened. “Yes.”

“Post Road Escort Agency, Li Xingzhou,” the man said. “Ten years ago, on the night things went wrong at Wangjiang, your father entrusted me with a consignment at an inn in Tiandu. The agreement was, if he didn’t come back, I was to wait ten years and deliver it to his son.”

He glanced at the archway, then at the sky. “Ten years and three days. I’m late.”

“How did you find your way here?”

“What happened at the Rain Pavilion the day before yesterday has already spread along the post road—they say a scholar named Qing has turned up in Yunchuan, carrying a White Dragon,” said Li Xingzhou. “I reached the Rain Pavilion halfway through the hour of the Pig. A young lady upstairs, holding a ledger and an abacus, asked me my name, where I came from, and the date on the consignment slip three times over. Only when it all matched did she say you were at the old archway.” He paused. “She told me to hurry. She said there were Guards of Balance at the fork in the road, and to give your name, not the agency’s.”

Qinglan thought of her fingertip pressing on that roll of paper before he left.

“And the consignment?”

“A consignment must be opened by the recipient’s own hand.” Li Xingzhou patted the bundle on his back. “This is no place to open it.”

The way back to the city passed through a bamboo grove.

When they came, the grove had not been long. On the way back, it had grown.

They walked for the time it takes to drink a cup of tea, and ahead there was still bamboo; they walked for another, and ahead there was still bamboo. Moonlight leaked through the bamboo tips, and the bamboo shadows on the ground leaned every which way. When the wind passed, the leaves rustled, very low, like someone counting tiles under his breath: One of Characters, Two of Characters, Three of Characters… counting to nine, then starting again from one.

Zhumo’s claws caught hold of Qinglan’s cuff again.

“Don’t listen to it count.” The panda’s voice shook. “A bamboo goblin. It follows people and pulls the path crooked. Count along with it, and every time you count, the path bends once.”

Qinglan looked back.

Three paces behind them was a bamboo shadow.

It was taller than the other bamboo shadows, as tall as a man, with knobs swelling on its joints like human knuckles. All the other bamboo shadows swayed with the wind; only it did not sway. When they stopped, it stopped too.

Jinshi swung the lion head out in front of him. “I’ll ram it head-on—”

“It fears fire and copper,” Zhumo said. “Ramming won’t do any good.”

The lanterns of the Guards of Balance had gone ahead after Hu Shan. The two following them had paper shades, the flames shrunk small inside.

Qinglan slipped his left hand into the hidden pocket of his right sleeve and took out the copper coin with two file marks.

The debt was cleared, but the copper coin was still a copper coin.

He held the copper coin up toward the bamboo shadow.

Moonlight fell on the copper coin. Its copper was dull and dark, but the ring around its edge, rubbed bright by handling, gleamed once.

The bamboo shadow shrank back a step.

Then another. The knobs flattened one by one; it grew shorter, until it was just an ordinary clump of bamboo, swaying with the wind.

The bamboo leaves stopped counting tiles.

Less than ten paces ahead was the edge of the grove. Beyond the edge was the wall of Yunchuan’s South Gate, a lamp hanging on the gate tower.

Jinshi let out a long breath. “So by day this thing is just a clump of bamboo?”

“By day it’s just a clump of bamboo,” Zhumo said. “By day you could cut it down for firewood and it wouldn’t make a peep.”

Qinglan put the copper coin back in his sleeve.

Only one lamp was lit in the main hall of the Rain Pavilion.

Yuening sat on the stairs leading to the second floor, on the third step, the small ebony abacus resting on her knees, the ledger spread open beside her. She had not slept, but she was close to it; her chin dipped once and lifted again, and her finger clicked one of the abacus beads. She sat with her knees drawn up, her skirt heaped on the step, only the tips of two embroidered shoes showing side by side. The lamp shone down from upstairs and cast her shadow on the wall, and in the shadow her waist looked so slender it might snap at a touch.

She saw Qinglan and stood up.

She did not ask whether he had won or lost. She looked at his waist first.

She came down two steps and stood one step above him, her eyes exactly level with his brows. She reached out a finger and pressed her nail against the sealed-box knot. The knot was still that knot, loop pressed over loop. When her nail had finished with the knot, it touched each of the two pieces of Mahjong Jade in turn, as if counting.

“Two,” she said.

Her head was bowed, very close to him. In her hair bun was still that small white flower, and the petal with the crease folded into it was still folded. The smell of ink on her had faded and mixed with a little lamp-oil smoke, and beneath it was a wisp of warm fragrance drawn out by the heat of her body, which could only be smelled up close. She had been sitting under this lamp for nearly two double-hours.

Qinglan moved his eyes to the stair rail. There was an old crack in the rail, running from the third step to the fifth. He looked at that crack from one end to the other.

“What are you looking at?” Yuening asked.

“The rail’s cracked.”

“It’s been cracked for ten years,” Yuening said. “You only noticed today?”

Without waiting for his answer, she took his left hand and turned it over to look. The pads of his fingers were covered in ink smudged from recording the game. She wiped them for him with her thumb, from the base of each finger to the tip, finishing one and moving to the next, wiping very slowly, the ball of her thumb circling against his fingertip. Not much of the ink came off, but his hand grew hot first.

“It won’t come off,” she said, yet she did not let go.

She held out her hand.

“The paper.”

Qinglan handed her the roll of rough paper. She untied the hemp cord and, by the lamp, read the game he had recorded with his left hand—crooked and scrawled, some characters so big they took up two lines, some so small they were squeezed into the cracks between the squares. Eleven rounds, and for every round, what each of the four had discarded; hardly a single tile was missing.

When she had finished, she rolled the paper up and tied it again.

“Your handwriting is too ugly,” she said. “I’ll copy it out tomorrow.”

She turned and slipped the roll of paper inside the cover of the ledger. As she turned, she lifted a hand to tuck a stray strand of hair that had fallen from her temple behind her ear. It didn’t stay the first time, so she tucked it again.

Xiao Cheng'an had been fetched from the back courtyard by a waiter. The old man wore a brown overcoat patterned in gold, leaned on his cane, his small cap askew, a little tea froth still clinging to his short white beard.

He looked at the oilpaper packet Qinglan had set on the table and did not touch it.

“The old Red Dragon?”

“Lu Zhaoye returned it,” Qinglan said. “Under the archway, in front of the Captain of the Bureau of Balance.”

Qi Tingjun nodded once by the door.

Xiao Cheng'an had a waiter bring a new wooden box. Yuening opened the ledger and turned her sleeve back one fold, baring a length of wrist so slender on its inner side that two faint blue veins showed through. She took up the brush and recorded the hour, the source, and the handlers. With a pair of copper tweezers she lifted the oilpaper, picked up the Red Dragon, turned it over, looked at the back of the tile against the lamp, turned it face up again, and laid it in the box.

Xiao Cheng'an closed the lid. Yuening applied the sealing strip, each of them pressed a fingerprint on it, and Yuening wrote the hour on the back of the sealing strip.

“At dawn it goes to the branch office,” said Yuening. “The evidence courier is still in the city. Once he’s received it, he’ll escort it to Tiandu, into Vault One.”

“I’ll guard it,” said Qi Tingjun.

He did not come further in. He sat by the door, the Balance blade across his knees.

Li Xingzhou set the oilcloth bundle on the table and untied it.

Inside was an escort box, iron-capped at the corners, a sealing strip wound around the lock. The sealing strip had long since yellowed, and the writing on it had blurred; only a signature remained, and Qinglan recognized it—it was his father’s hand.

“To be opened by the recipient’s own hand,” said Li Xingzhou.

Qinglan tore open the sealing strip. The lock had only been hooked shut; a flick, and it opened.

There were three things in the box.

A cloth pouch, heavy in the hand. A letter, its flap pressed with a fingerprint. And a slip of paper folded in four.

He opened the slip of paper first. On it was a single line, its ink faded to brown:

Beneath the Dipper, the White Dragon bears a mark.

Behind him, Zhumo sucked in a sharp breath.

“The back courtyard,” said the panda. “Tonight the Dipper is overhead.”

The back courtyard was not large: one well, one old pagoda tree. There were no clouds tonight. The moon was past the fifteenth, still round, but it had already sunk to the west, behind the ridge of the front building, and this corner of the back courtyard lay in the ridge’s shadow. With the moonlight held off, the Dipper showed itself: seven stars hanging northwest of the pagoda tree’s crown, the handle pointing up, tilted toward the west.

Qinglan took the White Dragon from over his heart, turned it over, held it back-up on his left palm, and tilted it toward the Dipper.

The little carved hand in the lower right corner of the tile’s back, five fingers spread. Beneath the heel of the little hand, seven points slowly surfaced.

Fine as pinholes, arranged in a dipper, exactly like the one in the sky. Not light, but hollows—seven pinhole-sized pits, as if someone had touched the back of the tile seven times with a needle point, so lightly that an ordinary sweep of the thumb could never have felt them.

The seven points that had flashed beneath the heel of the hand of the man in the woven hat were these seven.

Zhumo had already pulled a sheet of thin paper and an ink stick from his bamboo basket. His paws shaking, he spread the paper over the back of the White Dragon and rubbed the ink lightly across it. The seven pinholes beneath the paper showed in the ink as seven white dots.

The rubbing was done.

Zhumo lifted the ink rubbing and held it cupped in his paws, as if holding a flake of snow that might melt.

Qinglan looked down at the White Dragon.

The White Dragon had grown warm in his palm.

Not the usual lukewarm warmth, but a warmth welling outward from the heart of the tile, flowing along his palm up into his arm, reaching his chest, and settling at the root of that strand.

That root had been empty since yesterday, without a single thread.

Now there was one thread at the root.

And another thread.

Two threads. He saw it in his mind: a slender bamboo, two tender leaves sprouting at its tip.

Then, beside that slender bamboo, something stirred.

Like a bamboo root buried in the soil for many years, scalded by the warmth, nudging at the earth. It broke through the earth, and the tip of a bamboo shoot poked out, a touch of green at its point.

The second strand.

The strand he had once grown had lain asleep all through the ten years this White Dragon had rested against his heart. Under the Dipper’s light, with the seven points revealed, it had woken.

Two of Bamboo.

Beside him Zhumo said softly, “The Two of Bamboo, newly born.”

Qinglan pressed the White Dragon back against his heart and picked up the letter.

The fingerprint on the envelope was his father’s.

He touched that fingerprint with his left thumb.

The White Dragon gave a violent jolt.

Of the two threads newly returned to that strand in his heart, one was drawn away.

The back courtyard before his eyes vanished.

He saw a lamp. An inn’s lamp, its shade smoked yellow. Beneath the lamp was a table with letter paper spread on it, and beside the letter paper lay a White Dragon. He was looking up from where that White Dragon lay. A hand holding a brush was writing—two characters, then a stop for a fit of coughing that shook the shoulders, then two characters more. The hand was thin, calluses on its knuckles. It was his father’s hand.

He could not make out a single character on the letter paper. He saw only the last line: the hand stopped halfway through writing it, the brush tip hovering, and a drop of ink fell from the tip and spread into a blot.

He heard his father’s voice, coughing, saying low:

“That hand isn’t—”

He did not write any further. He folded the letter, pressed a thumbprint on the flap, and put it into a box with iron-capped corners. Someone knocked twice outside the door; a broad-shouldered figure came in, took the box, cupped his fist in salute, and went out.

The hand came back to the table and picked up the White Dragon.

His vision swayed. He saw a bed, and curled on the bed a boy, both hands wrapped in cloth, blood seeping through the cloth. The hand placed the White Dragon in the boy’s palm and closed the boy’s fingers over it.

It was himself, at fourteen.

The lamp went out.

Qinglan was kneeling on the blue bricks of the back courtyard, his right hand braced on the ground.

His right hand—he could not feel it. From fingertips to shoulder, the whole arm felt like someone else’s, numb as a stick of firewood. He looked down: his fingertips were red, covered in a layer of small bumps like chilblains.

In his mind he looked at the two bamboos. The slender bamboo of the first strand had one leaf left at its tip. The bamboo shoot of the second strand—the touch of green at its point had faded to white.

It had leaked.

Not a full breath, only half a breath. With the Dipper overhead, the White Dragon had not burned the threads in his body clean; it had taken only half. Had it not been for the Dipper, that half breath would have been enough to burn the bamboo shoot away entirely.

Jinshi hauled him up.

“What happened to you? Your face is as white as that tile—”

“It’s nothing,” said Qinglan. His voice had gone hoarse. “That letter is a key. Touch it once, and a door opens. Behind the door is my father.”

He did not touch the letter again.

He put the letter back in the escort box and snapped the lock shut.

The cloth pouch in the escort box Li Xingzhou untied in front of everyone.

Inside was a pouch of gold coins, each one cast on its face with seven stars arranged in a dipper.

“Dipper gold coins,” said Li Xingzhou. “The escort fee. Your father said the whole fee for this consignment was in this pouch; the recipient was to take one coin for himself, and the rest go back to the agency as they are.”

Qinglan took one with his left hand and put it inside his robe.

Li Xingzhou tied the pouch shut and put it back in his own bundle. “A ten-year consignment, settled in full tonight.” He cupped his fist once more. “Young Master Qing, if you ever travel the post road, give my name.”

Zhumo handed the ink rubbing to Yuening.

Yuening took it and said nothing. She opened the ledger, laid the ink rubbing flat in the inner pocket inside the cover, pressed it down, and ran her nail all the way around the edge of the cover.

“This one,” she said, “goes on my account too.”

Qi Tingjun rose from beside the door, walked to the doorway of the back courtyard, looked up at the Dipper in the sky, then looked at Qinglan’s hanging right arm.

“A left hand,” said Qi Tingjun. “Whatever my master wrote on that sealed endorsement—I have to turn over that Two of Dots first.”

Just before dawn, someone pounded on the back door.

The pounding was frantic, the blows running together. A waiter went to open it. Outside stood a young embroidery girl, thirteen or fourteen, in pink, her hair come loose from running, her face drenched in sweat.

“Young Master Qing…” She was too out of breath to get a whole sentence out. “Sister Xingyu… Sister Xingyu has collapsed! She’s ice-cold all over, and no matter how we call her she won’t wake!”

(End of Chapter Six)
