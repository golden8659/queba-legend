Chapter 17Tiandu

The boat spent twelve days on the Great River. Yuening had counted out the coins for the six tickets one by one; Qinglan’s Dipper gold coin stayed tucked against him, untouched.

Before dawn on the thirteenth, Jinshi was jolted awake by bells.

Not one bell. Dozens, hundreds, pealing from beyond the riverbank in overlapping waves, their deep hum rolling across the water. He climbed on deck with his quilt around him, ready to curse. Then he looked up, and his mouth fell open.

At the river’s end stood a city wall.

Nanchao’s walls were grey and low, weathered by the sea wind. This one was red ochre, so high he had to crane his neck. Battlement followed battlement to the edge of the sky, with no end in sight. Lamps hung at intervals along it. Dawn was close, but they still burned. From a distance, they looked like a chain of fire let down from heaven.

“Tiandu,” Pei Zhiwei said beside him, very softly, as though afraid of waking someone.

The wharf where they landed was smaller than Nanchao Harbour, but cleaner. Bluestone paving ran to the water’s edge, every slab worn bright. No one hawked fish. Porters in marked uniforms stood in a line waiting to unload, even their coughs subdued.

They entered through Yuheng Gate. Its passage was deep as a dark alley, their footsteps echoing within it. As they emerged, day broke.

A broad street stretched straight north.

Eight carriages could have driven abreast along it. The shops on either side rose three storeys, identical brass bells hanging beneath their eaves. A breath of wind set the whole street ringing. Far away at its end stood a tower taller still than the walls. An immense bronze instrument crowned it, ring within ring, shining in the morning light.

“The Starwatch Pavilion,” Zhumo said. “The highest place in Tiandu. They watch the heavens and set the calendar. Where the Dipper will be, and when—it is the people up there who decide.”

Jinshi stared, spellbound. Then he turned, and the roadside caught him instead.

Tiandu’s morning market was nothing like morning tea in Nanchao. There, people sat and ate at leisure. Here, they ate standing, walking. Chestnuts rattled in iron pans. At a sugar painter’s stall, a ladleful of syrup became a dragon, claws spread and jaws agape. White steam rose from sweet-potato ovens. Steamer lids lifted to reveal tray upon tray of buns, each as big as a fist.

Pei Zhiwei sniffed as he walked. At a mutton-soup stall, his feet stopped altogether. “That stock has been simmering for more than one night.”

Jinshi already had a bag of chestnuts in his arms and was eating them, hissing at the heat. “Ten years away, and everything in Tiandu is still this big!”

“Everything in the world gets sent to Tiandu.” Lu Zhaoye leaned against a hitching post to catch his breath. His chest wound had not fully healed; Jinshi had carried him on and off the boat throughout the journey. He was pale now, but his eyes kept searching the crowd. “Everyone, too. Good people. Bad people. All of them.”

Farther on, flags grew more numerous.

A red flag hung outside a mahjong house, embroidered with an enormous Red Dragon. An attendant at the door shouted, “The Tiandu branch of the Red Dragon Society! Four Red Dragons act as wild tiles! Draw bonus bird tiles to settle the score—” Across the street, a blue-green flag marked a branch of Pinghe Hall. Tiles clicked fast and thick within. Beyond it stood an old residence, its courtyard gates shut. A space above the entrance lay bare. The nails for a plaque were still there. The plaque was gone.

Qinglan paused before the house.

Only a moment. He pressed a hand to the front of his robe, then walked on.

Yuening followed without asking. On the boat, Zhumo had told her it had once been the Tuidao School’s branch in Tiandu. Ten years ago, the man who had hung that plaque had received a tile-breaking sentence. She had heard that the plaque had been pulled down and burned for firewood.

Lu Zhaoye brought up the rear. As he passed the courtyard wall, the corner of his eye caught a small side gate at its foot. It had not been shut properly. Through a gap a finger wide lay a stretch of dark covered walkway. Someone seemed to be standing under it, in a dark-blue long robe faded with washing. Very tall, yet stooped.

Lu Zhaoye checked his stride and looked again. Under the walkway there was only the shadow of a pillar. He said nothing and followed the others.

Two streets farther stood a teahouse overlooking the river. Three storeys, upswept eaves, a string of brass bells at their corner. Its sign had been freshly painted, the gold lettering dazzling in the sun:

Wangjiang Teahouse.

Zhumo’s steps suddenly slowed. Jinshi knew those front steps too: ten years ago, he had danced the lion at their foot all day long.

Qinglan did not stop. He passed the entrance facing straight ahead, never once looking in. But Yuening saw his hand at his side. Knuckle by knuckle, it tightened. Only after a long time did it slowly open.

He had left this city at fourteen. He had never returned.

Tiandu was still Tiandu. The streets were just as wide, the bells just as loud. The teahouse sign had a new coat of paint. It shone more brightly than it had ten years ago.

◆

Two stone xiezhi crouched before the Bureau of Balance, single horns pointing skyward, glaring at everyone who passed.

Qi Tingjun waited at the entrance. He wore the same deep blue-green xiezhi robes, his Balance blade at his waist, but he was thinner than he had been in Yunchuan, dark with exhaustion beneath the eyes.

“You received the letter?”

“We did,” Qinglan said. “We boarded without waiting for word back from Ke Jingduo.”

Qi Tingjun nodded. Without pleasantries, he turned and led them inside. Through two courtyards lay an evidence room, locked wooden cabinets lining all four walls. A white cloth covered the table at its centre.

On the cloth lay an ebony abacus frame.

The frame was scorched black, all four corners charred. Its thirteen rods had snapped. Not a bead remained. Only the bottom crossbar, for some reason, was less blackened than the rest. At its centre was a tiny carved “Yun”.

Yuening stood before the table without reaching out.

She untied the small ebony abacus at her waist and placed it beside the frame. One large, one small. One fresh, one charred. On the first bead of the small abacus’s lowest rod, “Yun” was carved too. The same strokes. The same slight flick towards the upper right.

“It’s my mother’s,” she said. Her voice was level. Not a ripple in it.

“No one has lived in that house for ten years,” Qi Tingjun said. “I checked the landlord’s accounts. For ten years, someone has paid the rent on time, once a year, never a day late. The person paying never appeared. They sent the money through others, under the name of a theatre troupe: Yansheng Troupe.”

“A theatre troupe?” Jinshi scratched his head.

“The most celebrated in Tiandu. Its leader is Shen Yansheng.”

Yuening’s fingers stopped on the abacus.

“Two other things.” Qi Tingjun lowered his voice. “First, the fire started inside, at the writing desk. The door was barred from within. But once the fire was out, no one was found in the house. Second—”

He took a small paper packet from his sleeve and opened it. Inside lay a pinch of grey-white incense ash.

Pei Zhiwei leaned close to smell it and his face changed. “Like the bone-numbing herb in Nanchao. What else was in the ash still needs someone who can identify medicines.”

“Half a wall inside was piled with burned medicine packets. The labels are sealed; we’ve asked for identification,” Qi Tingjun said. “Your line ‘Send to Tiandu, the street behind the Scorekeepers’ Institute’: whether this room is where the goods were received, we’re still checking.”

“And then someone burned this room down,” Qinglan said.

Qi Tingjun looked at him without speaking.

◆

Yansheng Troupe performed at Wansheng Theatre in the east of the city.

That night, every seat was filled. People stood even in the aisles. Two great ornate lanterns hung above the stage, a red silk curtain across its front. The gongs and drums sounded. Everyone in the theatre hushed.

The musicians came out first.

On the left sat a tall, slender young woman, a seven-stringed zither across her knees. Her features were quiet, her head bowed over the strings she tuned. The musician on the right was a young woman too, a pair of golden-feathered wings folded behind her. She held a golden mouth organ, a fine ring of gold feathers inlaid around its pipes. Zhumo murmured in Yuening’s ear, “The zither player is Lansheng. The mouth-organ player is Huang Lingyin. People in Tiandu say half a Yansheng Troupe performance is sung, and the other half is breathed and plucked.”

The zither began. The mouth organ followed. Slowly, the red silk curtain opened.

A woman stood onstage.

She wore a phoenix crown, her hair adorned with pearls and jade. A ring of red pom-poms trembled, and tassels fell to her shoulders. Her red costume was embroidered with golden peonies; beneath it was a yellow skirt embroidered with phoenixes. Two snow-white flowing sleeves hung down, a delicate border of abacus beads embroidered at the cuffs. Her stage make-up was heavy, the red at the outer corners of her eyes drawn long and bright. When she stepped to the front of the stage, every lamp in the theatre seemed to dim.

Yuening’s hand tightened sharply around her tea bowl.

She knew that face. Not from meeting it in her waking life. From mirrors. From dreams. From her mother’s face. The slight downward curve at the tips of the brows, the faint crease when the lips pressed together—just like her mother. Only thinner. Colder. Like a blade returned to its sheath.

Shen Yansheng began to sing.

Setting the World in Order told an ancient story. A woman scorekeeper oversaw a sworn match of honour on behalf of all the world. When she counted the tiles at its close, however often she counted, there was one more than there should have been. Everyone told her to be quiet. What did one tile more or less matter? She refused. So they drove her out of the city, and no one knew what became of her.

Midway through the play, the scorekeeper worked her abacus by lamplight. There was no abacus onstage. Shen Yansheng held her empty hands in the air, all ten fingers moving, and the zither notes fell one by one beneath them, as though real beads were clicking.

She sang:

One hundred and thirty-six, till the lamp’s spent wick falls;

That one tile too many has no place here at all.

The reckoning unfinished, I leave to those who come;

Wherever you have counted to, I’ll count from there again.

The theatre rang with applause.

Yuening did not applaud. She sat there, her face already wet, and she did not even know it.

She had said that line to the rafters more times than she could count. Ten years ago, her mother had worked her abacus by lamplight. Yuening had leaned against the table and asked what she was counting. Her mother had not answered, only stroked her head.

Beside her, Zhumo was white as paper. He stared at the hands working an invisible abacus onstage. His lips moved. Nothing came out. He only lowered his head and clenched his brush until his grip could grow no tighter.

Qinglan glanced at him. He did not ask.

◆

After the performance, Yuening wanted to go backstage.

A dresser guarded the door. In his twenties, round-faced, smiling at everyone, he held a tea tray and blocked their way with a smile. “The troupe leader sees no visitors after a performance. If you have business, miss, come early tomorrow.”

“My family name is Shen,” Yuening said.

The dresser’s smile faltered. Then returned. “There are plenty of Shens in Tiandu.”

A voice came from inside. “Let her in.”

Backstage was cramped, packed with costume chests and stage clothes. A square table stood in the middle, the tiles already laid out. Shen Yansheng sat at a mirror, half her make-up removed. She did not turn, but watched Yuening in the glass.

Yuening went over and set the small ebony abacus on her dressing table.

“Master Shen,” she said. “Do you recognise this name?”

Shen Yansheng glanced down.

Only once. She took up a handkerchief and slowly wiped the paint from the corner of her eye. Her voice was cold as well water. “No. You have the wrong person, miss. I have no elder sister. And no niece.”

“I hadn’t even asked about a sister. How did you know I meant yours?”

In the mirror, Shen Yansheng’s hand paused.

The round-faced dresser came over with his tray and topped up her tea. Still smiling, he stood behind her. He did not leave.

Shen Yansheng put down her handkerchief, turned and looked at Yuening for a long time.

“The Yansheng troupe’s rule,” she said. “After the play, one hand of Red Dragon. Outsiders may sit. Win, and I hear you out. Lose, and you leave through this door and never return.”

“Agreed.”

◆

The four rolled for seats, ties rerolled. Shen Yansheng took East, Lansheng South, Huang Lingyin West and Yuening North. East began with one extra tile and discarded first, the others waiting with thirteen. Qinglan stood behind Yuening; Jinshi and Zhumo squeezed among the costume boxes, while Pei Zhiwei supported Lu Zhaoye at the doorway. Lansheng and Huang Lingyin were only filling the table; each played her own hand and fed the troupe leader no tiles.

The Red Dragon Society’s game was different from the Tuidao School’s.

One hundred and twelve tiles. Only the three suits—Characters, Bamboo and Dots. The four Winds, Green Dragons and White Dragons were all removed. Only the four Red Dragons remained. Those four could “act as wild tiles”: kept in the hand, they could stand for any tile a player wished. But they had rules of their own. They could not be discarded, punged or konged. They stayed among the concealed tiles, revealing their power only at the moment of winning. No chows, only pungs and kongs. No winning on another player’s discard. Only self-draw.

Before play began, Huang Lingyin took two tiles from the end of the completed wall and set them aside face down. These were the “bonus bird tiles”. They still counted among the one hundred and twelve, but would not be dealt into anyone’s hand or used for draws or replacement draws; only the remaining one hundred and ten were available for play that hand. The two bird tiles would be revealed only after a self-draw win had been verified. A One, Five or Nine of Characters, Bamboo or Dots, or a Red Dragon, counted as one bonus bird hit for each tile revealed; the other numbered tiles did not count. If the hand ended in a draw, no bird tiles would be revealed and no winning points would be paid. For the next hand, the two bird tiles would be shuffled back in with the entire set.

Before play, all four agreed the scoring: ordinary win, base two; closed hand or all pungs, base four; pure suit, base eight. Combined patterns take only this game’s highest bracket; Red Dragon substitutions add nothing. Then birds: each hit adds one to each payer’s base, at most two birds, with no further doubling. The winner receives separately from the other three; no one covers another’s payment.

Standing behind Yuening, Qinglan looked at the four Red Dragons on the table and felt his chest tighten. Without his noticing, his hand had come to rest on the front of his robe.

In the Tuidao School, a tile was revealed from the wall each hand to determine the wild tile. It changed from hand to hand. Here, it was always the Red Dragon. Ten years ago, at Wangjiang Teahouse, the tile taken from his father’s wall had been a Red Dragon.

This city was full of Red Dragons.

Play began.

Shen Yansheng played quickly, but without haste. Draw, glance, keep or discard. Never a hesitation. Lansheng and Huang Lingyin had played with her for who knew how many years. The three players’ discards followed one another like the beat of the stage gongs and drums.

On the third turn, Yuening drew a Red Dragon.

She tucked it into her hand, playing and counting. No Wind tiles on the table, no Green Dragons or White Dragons. One hundred and twelve tiles, twenty-four fewer to count than usual, yet every one mattered more. Her fingers rested at the table’s edge, tapping softly to the numbers in her head. Once. Twice. Three times.

On the fifth turn, her fingers stopped.

Beside her, Shen Yansheng’s fingers rested at the table’s edge too.

Tapping. Once. Twice. Three times. With the ring finger, an inch in from the edge. Three taps for every tile drawn.

Exactly like Yuening.

Her mother had taught her that. When counting tiles, she had said, give your hand a place to rest, and your mind will not wander. It was the Shen family’s method. Passed down only to their own.

Yuening looked up.

So did Shen Yansheng. Their eyes met across the square table.

Only for an instant. Shen Yansheng looked away. She drew her hand back from the edge and pressed it flat on the tabletop, so hard her fingertips whitened. For the remaining turns of that hand, she did not tap again.

Behind her, the round-faced dresser lifted the teapot and added a little more to her cup.

On the ninth turn, Shen Yansheng drew a tile and pushed it onto the table without looking.

“Self-draw.”

She spread her hand: One, Two, Three and Four of Characters; Six, Seven, Seven, Eight and Nine of Bamboo; three Fives of Dots; a Red Dragon; and the Eight of Bamboo she had just drawn.

The Red Dragon acted as a wild tile, becoming a One of Characters to make the pair of eyes with her own One. Six, Seven and Eight of Bamboo; Two, Three and Four of Characters; three Fives of Dots; Seven, Eight and Nine of Bamboo. Four melds. One pair.

“Draw bonus bird tiles.” Huang Lingyin turned over the two tiles set aside.

A Nine of Dots. A Six of Characters.

“One bonus bird hit,” Lansheng said.

“Closed hand base four, one bird adds one. Five each.” Lansheng made it clear. “South, West and North each minus five. East plus fifteen.”

Shen Yansheng watched Yuening. “Miss, you lost.”

Yuening’s fingertips paused on the backs of her row of tiles. She did not reveal them. One by one, she laid them face down.

She too held a Red Dragon. She had been ready to win. One tile short. Red Dragon Society rules allowed only self-draw; with both hands ready, it came down to who drew first.

“I’ve lost.” She stood and tied the small ebony abacus back at her waist. “Master Shen. You play very well.”

Shen Yansheng did not look at her. She turned to the mirror and went on removing the make-up from the unfinished half of her face.

◆

It was fully dark when they left Wansheng Theatre.

They walked the empty streets without a word. Jinshi held it in as long as he could, then burst out, “But she clearly is—”

“Hush.” Lu Zhaoye stopped abruptly.

Footsteps behind them.

Someone carrying a seven-stringed zither hurried from the theatre’s side door. Lansheng. She caught up with Yuening, drew breath, glanced about and lowered her voice almost to nothing.

“The troupe leader asked me to give you a message.”

Yuening watched her.

“It isn’t that she won’t acknowledge you,” Lansheng said. “She daren’t. That dresser pouring the tea joined us three years ago. He isn’t a theatre man.”

She turned away with her instrument, then looked back after two steps. “The troupe leader also said, ‘That hand of Red Dragon—she was ready by the eighth turn. Tell her she counted correctly.’”

Yuening stood still for a long time.

When she had laid her tiles face down, no one had seen her hand.

◆

That night, Qi Tingjun carried the burned abacus frame to the inn himself. It had to go back to the Bureau of Balance before dawn, he said, and he would wait next door.

Yuening sat alone beneath the lamp, holding it.

She had only wanted to look at her mother’s writing. But as her fingers passed over the crossbar that had escaped blackening, they stopped.

The wood was smooth.

Not the natural smoothness of ebony. The slickness of oil. She brought her fingers to her nose and caught a faint, familiar scent. Walnut oil.

Her mother had a habit. On the first day of every month, she took a soft cloth, dipped it in a little walnut oil and wiped her abacus from end to end. Ebony drank oil, she said. Leave it unwiped for a month, and it would dry. Dry wood would split.

A room with no guest registration for ten years. An abacus untouched for ten years.

Yet this crossbar still bore traces of oil care. That habit she knew too well.

Yuening shot to her feet. Her chair crashed over behind her. Qinglan rushed in from next door. “What is it?”

She held the frame and looked at him, her eyes as bright as they had been at the banquet in Nanchao.

“The door was barred from inside.” Her voice shook. “The fire started at the writing desk. The room was full of what looked like Ghost Hand’s bone-numbing herb.”

“Yuening—”

“Someone cared for this abacus by her old rules,” Yuening said. “If she came back, if she herself lit that fire—”

She stopped.

She drew a deep breath and said it:

“I’m going to find her. Perhaps she really is still alive.”

◆

On the rooftop opposite the inn, a figure in a grey cloth cloak stood motionless in the night, watching the lighted window.

The wind lifted a corner of the cloak, exposing a hand.

It clutched a string of ebony abacus beads, threaded on an old red cord. One by one, beneath the moon, they shone with walnut oil.
