Chapter 10The Sunken Ship

Before dawn, someone began pounding on the inn door.

“Grandpa! Is my grandpa here?”

Qinglan threw on his robe and went downstairs. A boy stood at the door, deeply tanned, barefoot, trousers rolled to his knees, smelling of salt and sea wind. He spotted Old Hong snoring on a bench in the common room, let out a long breath and rushed over to help him up.

“Grandpa! You stayed out all night! I’ve searched half the docks!”

Old Hong opened bleary eyes. Recognising the boy, he grinned. “Changfeng… I was talking with young Pei here… all night…”

The evening before, Pei Zhiwei had helped the drunken Old Hong back to the inn. Pot after pot of sobering tea, he had listened as the old man told him everything about the match three months earlier.

Only now did the boy notice the others: a scholar, a girl holding an abacus, a man built like an iron tower, a panda in a long robe, and a catfolk fellow crouched on a roof beam with his tail swaying.

He blinked. “Are you… actors?”

Jinshi roared with laughter. Lu Zhaoye leaned down from the beam. “Who looks like an actor to you, boy?”

“You.” Changfeng pointed at his tail. “Cats on stage wag their tails just like that.”

Lu Zhaoye’s tail froze in midair. Everyone laughed. Even Old Hong managed a couple of chuckles.

Changfeng settled his grandfather at a table, wiped his face with practised hands, then ran to the kitchen for a bowl of plain rice porridge. He blew on each spoonful before feeding it to him. Clearly, he had done this more than once.

“My parents went to sea and didn’t come back,” the boy said without looking up, as though speaking of another family. “After the dockyard was gone, Grandpa drank every day. I haul mooring lines and mend nets at the docks. On days there’s work I earn forty or fifty coppers. If no ships come in, I have to find work elsewhere.”

Yuening looked at his sunburnt, peeling shoulders and quietly closed her ledger.

◆

Over breakfast, Pei Zhiwei repeated Old Hong’s account of the match in full.

Three months earlier, a new firm called Sea Whale Trading House had come to Nanchao, spending freely and buying wharves and dockyards everywhere. Old Hong’s dockyard stood at the far eastern end of the harbour. An out-of-the-way place, but the water was deep. Sea Whale Trading House offered to buy it. Old Hong refused.

“My father left it to me.” Holding his porridge bowl, Old Hong was mostly sober now. His voice was hoarse. “His ship lies in the water outside that dockyard. Keeping watch over that water is keeping watch over him.”

So Sea Whale Trading House invited him to a sworn match of honour.

“I had the Four and Six of Dots, waiting for the Five,” Old Hong said. “By the seventh turn, there was a Five of Dots lying plain as day in the opposite player’s discard pool. I thought, that’s one fewer Five of Dots left. I can’t keep waiting. So I broke up my pair of Fives of Characters and discarded one…”

He set down the bowl. His hands shook. “The player opposite was waiting for the Five of Characters.”

“Who was he?” Qinglan asked.

“A player Sea Whale Trading House had hired.” Old Hong struggled to remember. “Tall and thin. A pale, greenish face. Deep-set eyes. Ten long, slender fingers. When he shuffled, it looked as though he were playing music…”

Jinshi slapped the table. “Hu Shan!”

Qinglan frowned. Qi Tingjun had said he pursued Hu Shan from Nanchao to Yunchuan. The timing fit—but a thin face alone did not settle it.

“No,” Qinglan said.

Everyone turned to him.

“If that Five of Dots really was added to the river later, he wanted you to think another winning tile was gone,” Qinglan said. “Seeing one Five of Dots doesn’t mean you had to break up Five of Characters. Tell us the order as you remember it. We’ll check against the original record.”

Old Hong stared at him. “How… how do you know?”

“Someone laid down exactly the same tile in front of me.”

Yuening and Zhumo exchanged a glance. At the Rain Pavilion, Qi Tingjun too had set a false Five of Dots in the river. But this was not the only Nanchao match in doubt.

◆

“But why do they want that patch of water?” Jinshi asked. “However good a dockyard is, it’s still only a dockyard.”

Old Hong glanced at his grandson, then at the door, and lowered his voice.

“My father’s ship was called Dingchao. One summer night, twenty years ago, she was coming home from the Outer Ocean when a gale caught her outside the harbour,” he said. “A Hong family heirloom was kept aboard: a tile. An old bone tile, yellow with age, set in a little shrine at the bow. Father said an ancestor had brought it up from the sea. No one knew how many generations it had been passed down.”

“That night, at the height of the storm, a crack suddenly opened in the clouds. The Big Dipper showed through.” Old Hong’s eyes brightened. “Father said that was when the tile in the shrine began to glow. Green as jade. Like a pool of spring water.”

“And then?” Yuening’s voice was a little tight.

“Then the ship sank,” Old Hong said. “Father clung to a plank and drifted all night before someone rescued him. The tile went down with the ship, outside the dockyard.”

“What tile was it?” Zhumo asked.

“No one could say.” Old Hong shook his head. “The face had long since worn smooth. You couldn’t tell which Character or Bamboo it was. Father said my great-grandfather called it ‘a tile with a spirit’. Aboard ship, if it shone, wind was coming. If it stayed dark, fair weather. Our family sailed by it for a hundred years. Not one ship went down.”

He gave a bitter smile. “Until it went down itself.”

No one in the room spoke.

Qinglan pressed a hand to the front of his robe. Inside, the White Dragon his father had left him lay against his chest.

Beneath the Dipper, the colour of jade shone through. The White Dragon held traces of the past; this tile of the Hong family, however, had once warned ships at sea of the wind.

Perhaps it was another Heavenly Tile.

But he had not seen it.

◆

“For three months, Sea Whale Trading House has had lamps out on that water every night,” Changfeng said suddenly. “I’ve seen them from the docks. Several little boats in a circle. Men diving over and over.”

“Have they found it?”

“They can’t.” Changfeng shook his head. “There’s mud down there. Twenty years of it. Deeper than a man is tall. Two of the men they sent down never came back.” His face was taut. “Then they started looking everywhere for divers. Offering ten gold coins a man.”

Jinshi sucked in a breath. “Ten gold coins? You could buy a small boat for that!”

“But the best diver in Nanchao won’t take the job,” Changfeng said. “His family name’s Li. Li Boyuan. Sea Whale’s men went to ask him three times. He threw them out all three.”

“Li?” Qinglan started.

He remembered the escort guard on the post road, with shoulders as broad as a door. I know the southern post road. If you’re going to Nanchao, give my name at the post station.

“Li Xingzhou,” Yuening remembered too. “He said he had people at the Nanchao post station.”

“Perhaps,” Zhumo said slowly, “they’re family.”

Just then, a head appeared in the doorway: a lion-dance apprentice of ten or so, carrying a scarlet invitation. He laid it in front of Jinshi, turned and ran.

A single line was written on it in thick, forceful strokes:

Two days from now. Noon. The plum-blossom posts at the docks. Lion from out of town—do you dare? Huo Xiao

Jinshi slapped the invitation onto the table so hard the porridge bowls jumped. “Dare? Of course I dare!”

Yuening glanced at him. “Troupe master. The day after tomorrow is the day before the qualifiers.”

“Perfect!” Jinshi lifted his chin. “First I’ll show him how a lion with a scar gets onto the posts!”

◆

After breakfast, Jinshi went to the two shop-opening jobs he had accepted the day before, under neighbouring arcades. He used low-post antics and carried silk through the doorway instead, still making the children clap beneath the arcade. The jobs finished before noon. The drummer wanted another booking, but Jinshi was already sitting beside a low post, one hand braced against it. When both jobs were paid, he first settled the drummer, helpers and lion-head craftsman, then gave Yuening the remaining twenty-two taels before rewrapping his split knuckles.

That afternoon, Zhumo spread a tableful of old game records at the inn.

“The hundred fastest Pinghe Hall games in thirty years.” The panda pointed with his worn brush. “Look: chow in the third turn, pung in the fourth, win in the fifth. These plays are quick in their choices, not every one relying only on luck. They don’t always wait for the widest, prettiest hand. They’ll take an earlier completed win too.”

Qinglan traced those three turns of draws and discards twice over. “I used to keep waiting for a better tile.”

“You can learn another way of choosing.” Zhumo nodded. “To be fast, you still need to know what you’re giving up. It doesn’t mean calling every other style slow.”

Qinglan closed the record, rose and went alone to Pinghe Hall.

Liuxia was still shelling peanuts by the door. Seeing him, she raised an eyebrow. “It isn’t next month yet, Tuidao.”

“I haven’t come for the entry trial.” Qinglan sat opposite her. “I’ve come to make a bargain.”

“What bargain?”

“Teach me to be quick.”

Liuxia burst out laughing. Peanut shells scattered across the table. “Why would I teach you?”

“Because yesterday, while you were on the door,” Qinglan said, “you looked upstairs seven times. And every time, you shelled a peanut afterwards. Very slowly.”

Her hands paused.

“Tuidao,” she said slowly. “Have you come here to read me?”

“Reading the tiles has never meant reading only tiles.”

Her smile disappeared.

“I don’t know who lives upstairs,” Qinglan went on. “But something has been weighing on you these past few days. Something you can’t solve alone.”

Liuxia stared at him for a long time.

“…My second senior fellow disciple,” she said at last, her voice dropping. “Meng Chuan. The fastest player of our generation at Pinghe Hall. The Hall Master chose him to compete in the qualifiers three days from now. But for the past fortnight, he’s been losing strangely. Every hand is as fast as ever. Yet at the final step, every time, he’s one tile short.”

“You’ve watched him play?”

“More than a dozen hands.” Liuxia bit her lip. “I couldn’t see a thing.”

Qinglan nodded. “Let me watch him play a hand tomorrow.”

“You’ll see it?”

“I won’t know until I look.” Qinglan rose. “I’ll read your fellow disciple’s play. You teach me how to be quick.”

Liuxia watched him for a long while, then slapped a shelled peanut down in front of him.

“Deal,” she said. “But understand this. I’ll teach you. If you’re slow to learn, I’ll still shout at you.”

“Fair.”

“Then here’s your first question.” Liuxia tapped the table. “I’m one step from being ready to win. The previous player discards a Four of Bamboo. I have the Five and Six. I can chow them into a sequence. Chow, and I’m ready at once. Don’t chow, and I need two more turns of draws. Do I chow?”

Qinglan thought. “If you chow, that’s two fewer tiles you can change, and fewer winning tiles. If you don’t, two more draws might give you a two-sided wait. Two Fours of Bamboo are already visible. The Five—”

“Slow!” Liuxia slapped the table. “I asked whether to chow, and you give me a whole ledger! Pinghe Hall’s rule: decide at a glance. Chow and you’re ready now. Don’t chow and wait two turns. At a mahjong table, two turns is enough for someone else to win several times. Which is quicker?”

Qinglan opened his mouth.

“Chow,” he said.

“That’s better.” Liuxia tossed a peanut into her mouth, pleased with herself. “Dawn tomorrow. Chaosheng Teahouse. A quarter of an hour late, and I won’t teach you.”

◆

That night, Changfeng brought a little sampan.

“They guard the water outside the dockyard by day and work under lamps at night. Only at the change of watch around midnight do the waterway guards go off duty, leaving a two-hour gap to slip past behind the wooden posts.” He planted the scull in the water. “Get in. We have two hours.”

Qinglan, Lu Zhaoye and Yuening boarded. Jinshi wanted to follow, but after looking at the little sampan and then at his own bulk, he sheepishly stepped back. “I’ll… keep watch on shore.”

“Afraid of being seasick, troupe master?” Lu Zhaoye grinned.

“Who’s afraid?”

The sampan slipped quietly away from the quay.

Changfeng handled it with a skill beyond his years. The scull seemed part of his body, each stroke almost soundless.

“Grandpa taught me,” he said softly. “Says Hong children go into the water at three and handle a boat at five. When I was five, he threw me into the harbour. I splashed about for ages and swam out on my own.”

Yuening huddled in the middle, arms round her knees. “Your grandfather sounds… fierce.”

“He is.” Changfeng grinned. “But when he’s drunk, he hugs me and cries. Says he’s let down my great-grandfather, my father, and me.”

The harbour at night was another world. The great ships slept, their masts rows of thin shadows in the starlight. Seawater lapped the hull. Pat. Pat. One sound after another. Far away at the eastern end of the harbour, a few lights swayed on the water. Sea Whale Trading House’s boats.

Changfeng eased his strokes and edged forward beside a line of abandoned piles.

“Here.” He stopped sculling, his voice barely a whisper. “Dingchao lies a hundred feet east of the third pile.”

Yuening leaned over the gunwale and peered down.

Black water. Nothing to see.

But Qinglan looked up. Seven stars hung slantwise in the northern sky like a ladle, its handle pointing straight towards this water.

Beneath the Dipper.

He could not help reaching inside his robe to touch the White Dragon. It lay cool against his chest. Nothing happened.

“What are you looking for?” Yuening whispered.

“I don’t know,” Qinglan said. “Perhaps a lamp.”

◆

Voices came from Sea Whale Trading House’s little boats. Lu Zhaoye’s ears pricked up. His tail stopped moving.

“…Another whole night diving for nothing.” A rough voice swore. “Nothing but mud. Can’t even feel anything down there.”

“The Master is pressing us,” said another. “Says it must be brought up before the Assembly.”

“That Li fellow still refusing?”

“Still refusing. Says his elder brother sent word telling him to stay clear of this job.”

“Then stop asking.” A third voice, cold. “That old shipowner. Isn’t he shouting about it in the teahouse every day? He talks too much. No one remembers where his father’s ship sank better than he does. Bring him here. Let him point it out.”

In the sampan, Changfeng’s hand shook. The scull struck a pile with a hollow knock.

All the lamps swung towards them at once.

“Who’s there?”

◆

“Down!”

Lu Zhaoye pushed Changfeng flat into the bottom of the boat, snatched up the scull and thrust it against a pile. Like a fish, the sampan slipped away along the shadow of the timbers.

Splashes and shouts rose behind them. Two little boats gave chase.

Lu Zhaoye sculled without looking back. Qinglan watched the pursuing lamps. One boat fast, one slow. In the fast one, the man on the scull changed hands every three strokes.

“Left!” Qinglan said. “Behind that row of piles. Shallow water. Their boats draw too deep to follow.”

Without a word, Lu Zhaoye swung the scull and turned between the piles. The fast boat followed too eagerly. A muffled thud: its bottom scraped the shoal and stuck.

The slower boat stopped outside the piles. Its lamp swayed for a long while. At last, it turned back.

“How did you know it was shallow on the left?” Lu Zhaoye asked, breathing hard.

“When Changfeng brought us past those piles, only half his scull went down into the water,” Qinglan said. “He didn’t even notice.”

Changfeng stared at him as though he were a strange creature.

The sampan drifted in the dark. None of the four dared speak. All they could hear was their own heartbeats.

After a long time, Yuening whispered, “Changfeng? Are you all right?”

He did not answer. He lay in the bottom of the boat, face buried in his arms, shoulders trembling.

“They…” At last he raised his head. His face was wet, whether with seawater or tears, no one could tell. “They’re going after Grandpa.”

◆

Dawn was near when the sampan reached shore.

Jinshi had paced the quay all night. Seeing them, he rushed over and lifted Changfeng out. “Well? Did they give you a fright?”

Changfeng broke free and ran for home.

Qinglan and the others followed. A row of low plank houses stood beside the docks. The last was the Hong family’s.

The door was open.

The room was empty. An oil lamp still burned on the table, its wick almost gone. A porridge bowl lay overturned on the floor. The spilled porridge had gone cold.

Old Hong was gone.

Changfeng stood in the doorway, motionless as a nail driven into wood.

Qinglan went inside, crouched beside the overturned bowl and picked something up.

A bone tile, face down. On its back was a tiny carved hand. Five fingers, spread wide.

He turned it over.

A Five of Dots.

The room was so quiet they heard the lamp wick crack.

Jinshi punched the doorframe. A split opened in the wood. “Animals!”

Lu Zhaoye was already crouching at the entrance, examining footprints in the growing light. “Three men. Someone dragged between them. East, towards the docks.” He looked up, golden cat’s eyes narrowing in the dawn. “Towards that water.”

Changfeng clenched his fists until his nails bit into his palms. He said nothing.

Qinglan cupped the Five of Dots face down in his palm and stood.

“They still need the old man to point out that stretch of water, so they may keep him alive for now,” he said. “But we can’t stake everything on them not hurting him. Send word to the branch office, find Diver Li too, and keep the trail here at the docks.”

He looked at the Five of Dots. “Once they bring it up, they’ll have even less reason to keep him—”

He left the sentence unfinished. On the table, the oil lamp’s wick crackled once more, and went out.

(End of Chapter Ten)
